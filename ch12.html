The Engineer Factory

Chapter 12. The engineer factory (1998-2012)

After one year in his first software job, a young engineer whom the anthropologists Chris Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan, in their published study, call Raghu earned 200,000 rupees a year (about $4,400). His father, after thirty years as an engineer at the nuclear power station at Kalpakkam, south of Chennai, earned 400,000.1 Raghu had grown up in Kalpakkam, taken a degree in electronics and communication engineering at a college in Coimbatore, and joined one of India’s six largest software companies, which the anthropologists call Indian Computer Services (ICS). When the researchers met him in Chennai he was 22, unmarried and a Tamil Brahman, a member of a community that made up about 2.5 per cent of Tamil Nadu’s population and that, in the authors’ ‘strong impression’, dominated many of the city’s software firms.2

Raghu worked on production support for a British client’s system. That is the work of keeping software that is already running in use: fixing what breaks, answering the client’s requests, handling the next fault. He told the researchers that every day brought a new problem to solve, so he was never bored. But in their words he ‘thinks he is not learning much’, except how to turn down demanding foreign clients, and he wanted to leave the industry soon and study for an MBA. In the meantime he enjoyed the money.3

Raghu stands near the start of a machine that, between the late 1990s and the early 2010s, turned out software workers on a scale India had never seen. It had two halves. One was a mass of new engineering colleges, most of them private, which multiplied the number of young Indians with an engineering degree. The other was inside the firms: recruiting drives that tested hundreds of thousands of graduates a year, and training campuses that turned engineers of any branch into programmers in a few months. Both halves served the bargain on which the industry ran. The firms sold engineers’ time, so their revenue grew with the number of engineers they could bill, and the factory existed to keep that number rising.

Colleges by the hundred

For forty years after independence India built engineering colleges slowly. The government’s statistical compendium counts 31 engineering and technology colleges in 1950-51, 149 in 1980-81 and 289 in 1990-91.4 Then the pace changed. There were 486 such colleges in 1997-98, 678 in 2000-01, 1,077 in 2001-02 and 1,562 in 2005-06.5 The country added more engineering colleges in those eight years than in the previous five decades together.

V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, describes ‘a rapid expansion of engineering colleges between 1998 and 2010’, and ‘a rapid growth of private engineering colleges and colleges offering MCA courses’.6 The Master of Computer Applications (MCA) was the postgraduate course for graduates in science and commerce, combining computing, mathematics and management. The law made room for the private colleges. On 31 October 2002 eleven judges of the Supreme Court, in T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka, overruled the fee and admission scheme the Court had laid down in the Unni Krishnan case of 1993 (Chapter 7).7 For a college that took no government money, ‘The decision on the fee to be charged must necessarily be left to the private educational institution’. Capitation fees and profiteering stayed forbidden, but ‘a reasonable revenue surplus’ for development and expansion was allowed.8 A share of seats could again be filled by the management.9 Private corporate bodies were allowed to set up universities in 2002, and under this policy, Rajaraman writes, private universities and private colleges ‘proliferated’.10 The supply of MCAs grew even faster than that of engineers, from about 7,000 graduates in 1998 to 120,000 by 2010.11

Quality did not keep pace, and the people who said so were the employers. Rajaraman, writing around 2012, notes that graduates of the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) and the National Institutes of Technology, about 10,000 a year, were excellent, but that ‘industry employers opine that the graduates of other institutions are not employable as soon as they graduate and need further training’.12 The regulators responded with inspection. The All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE), the statutory body that approves technical colleges, set up a National Board of Accreditation, and the University Grants Commission an assessment council to grade institutions.13

The expansion was uneven across the map, and the south’s colleges fed the south’s software cities.14 In Chennai, Fuller and Narasimhan found that of twenty software engineers under thirty on whom they had information, only three had studied outside Tamil Nadu. The rest could stay at home, because young men and women, Brahmans among them, could now get a good engineering education within the state.15

By the early 2010s the building had outrun demand. By 2012-13 AICTE had approved about 1.55 million undergraduate engineering seats, and about 37.6 per cent of them were empty, according to an analysis of the council’s data by the education non-profit Avanti Fellows.16 Approved undergraduate intake peaked at about 1.7 million in 2014-15. By 2016-17 almost half of the seats, 49.5 per cent, had no student in them.17 The colleges had been built for a market that was already starting to change.

Sidebar: How many engineers does India really produce?

No figure about Indian education is quoted more often, or more loosely, than the number of engineers the country turns out each year. In the mid-2000s, a study at Duke University found, typical press articles said that in 2004 India had graduated 350,000 engineers and China 600,000, against about 70,000 in the United States.18 Later, figures of well over a million a year appeared, including in Rajaraman’s own history. Neither kind was a like-for-like count of four-year engineering graduates.

The Duke study was an early attempt to check. In December 2005 a team of graduate students led by Gary Gereffi, a sociologist, and Vivek Wadhwa, an executive teaching engineering management, found that the large Indian and Chinese figures ‘include not only four-year degrees, but also three-year training programs and diploma holders’, and that they had been set against American four-year degrees alone.19 The figure they used for India in 2004, a projection by the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry’s lobby group, was 215,000 graduates in engineering, computer science and information technology. Only 112,000 of them held four-year bachelor’s degrees; the other 103,000 had three-year diplomas.20 Per million people, they reckoned, the United States produced about 750 such graduates a year, China about 500 and India about 200.21

The team also found something more striking. Nasscom’s projections rested on government data published with a ‘three- to four-year lag’, and ‘the most recent official Indian graduation data are from 1993’.22 Through much of the boom, on the Duke team’s account, official counts of the engineers the industry was hiring were erratic and years out of date, and the industry’s own lobby group was the ‘de facto authority’.23

Later figures do not settle the question. Rajaraman gives the best-known growth series: ‘In 1998 the number of engineering students was around 135,000 and in 2010 it grew to 1,300,000, a growth of almost 10 times.’24 Elsewhere in the same history he describes what appears to be the same figure differently: ‘Currently India graduates around 1.3 million engineers per year’.25 Neither passage defines it. A total enrolment of 135,000 in 1998 is far too small for 486 colleges teaching four-year courses, so the 1998 figure is most likely one year’s intake. By the same reasoning, 1.3 million in 2010 is most likely intake or approved seats rather than graduates. This book treats it as seats.

In 2014 Aspiring Minds, a company that sells employment tests to employers, wrote of ‘the six hundred thousand engineers that graduate annually’, without giving a source.26 If the Duke and Aspiring Minds figures are roughly right, India’s output of four-year engineering graduates grew about five times between 2004 and 2013, from about 112,000 to about 600,000. That is broadly in line with the more than threefold rise in college numbers between 1997-98 and 2005-06.27

The answer, then, depends on the question. By the early 2010s India approved about 1.5 million undergraduate engineering places a year, and a third or more of them stayed empty.28 Its output of four-year engineering graduates was about 112,000 in 2004 and perhaps 600,000 by 2013, neither of them an official count. The often-repeated claim that India produces 1.5 million engineers a year matches the seats, not the graduates. The real number grew very fast. It was never as large as the figure that travelled.

Any branch will do

The second half of the factory explains why so many colleges were worth building. The large software firms did not hire computer scientists in any great number. They hired engineers, and then made them into programmers themselves. Rajaraman sets out the logic plainly: ‘The IT companies’ common complaint was that there were not enough employable graduates with computer science degrees. Instead they recruited all engineering graduates with aptitude in computing; however they could not be directly placed as members of project teams.’29 So, he continues, ‘All major IT companies had in-house training programmes ranging from 12 weeks to 6 months.’30

A mechanical, civil or electrical engineer would do, if he or she could pass the firm’s aptitude test. That made a degree in any engineering branch a ticket to the best-paid jobs open to most graduates, and it gave families a reason to pay for one. A Nasscom projection, reported by the Duke team, was that 84.8 per cent of India’s four-year engineering graduates in 2004 took branches it classed as IT-related, a broad category that included electrical, electronics and instrumentation engineering as well as computer science.31

The scale of recruitment is clearest in the filings of Infosys, the Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) software company, made to American securities regulators in much the same categories from year to year. In the year to March 1998 it made about 1,790 offers.32 Ten years later, in the year to March 2008, it reported screening over 885,000 applications, testing over 227,000 applicants, interviewing more than 97,000 and making about 46,300 offers.33 Offers had grown about twenty-six times, though the filings do not separate new graduates from experienced recruits.34 Headcount tells the same story. Infosys had about 9,400 IT professionals in March 2002, about 36,800 employees in March 2005, about 91,200 in March 2008 (of whom about 18,700 worked outside India) and about 150,000 in March 2012.35 A generation earlier the pattern had been very different. Natarajan Chandrasekaran, who joined Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) in 1986-87, recalled in a 2020 oral history for the Computer History Museum a firm of about 500 to 600 professionals that ‘used to hire 20 people or 40 people a year’, often by taking a whole class from IIT Bombay or IIT Delhi.36

Infosys was not unusual in kind, only in what it published. Rajaraman writes that ‘every major software company was recruiting around 8000 graduates each year’ in the years from 1998 to 2010, and that with attrition of 15 to 20 per cent and workforces ‘of the order of 120,000’, ‘the replacement numbers themselves were over 15,000 each year’.37

How dependent the colleges had become on this hiring was shown by the one year it faltered: after the dot-com bust, campus placements at Anna University in Chennai by January 2002 were less than a third of the year before, as Chapter 11 describes.38 In Bangalore, many of the engineers interviewed in 2004 and 2005 by the researchers Carol Upadhya and A.R. Vasavi of the National Institute of Advanced Studies (NIAS) described the downturn of 2000-02. Some, the researchers wrote, ‘who had been campus recruited were later turned down by the companies, and remained unemployed for months’.39 Infosys, at least, came back quickly, and on a much larger scale. In the year to March 2004 Infosys alone made about 13,600 offers of employment.40

Mysore

Before there was a training campus, there was a training building. In 2001 the magazine Business Today described the Infosys headquarters campus in Bangalore, where in a three-storey chrome and glass centre ‘fifty of the country’s best software instructors put every batch of engineers recruited by the company through a gruelling three-month training programme’.41 By 2002, its filing said, Infosys employed 57 faculty members who ran three-month sessions for new recruits, and it had a Leadership Institute for its staff on a 200-acre campus in Mysore (Mysuru since 2014), elsewhere in Karnataka.42

The trainee campus came next, and Mysore became the factory floor. In its filing for the year to March 2005, Infosys reported that it had ‘recently completed the construction of an employee training facility in Mysore’. The facility could ‘house 4,500 trainees at any one time’ and train about 12,000 employees a year.43 The company said it did not fully use new employees on client work while they were in training, ‘particularly during our 14-week training course for new employees’.44 Three years later the capacity was 4,800 at a time and about 15,200 a year.45 By the year to March 2012 the filing had a new name for it. ‘We train the new engineering graduates that join us at our Global Education Center in Mysore,’ the company wrote. ‘With a total built-up area of 1.44 million square feet, the Infosys Global Education Center can train approximately 14,000 employees at a time.’46 The course had lengthened too. The same filing describes a ‘20-29 week training course for new employees’.47 The filings give capacities and square feet, not the voices of the trainees.48

The firm also reached back into the colleges. Through a programme it called Campus Connect, Infosys worked with engineering colleges so that their own teachers could give industry-related training. In the year to March 2012 it reported more than 190 workshops for more than 4,500 college teachers.49 The boundary between the college and the company, which in 1998 had been the campus interview, was becoming harder to see.

The pyramid and the bench

The shape of the workforce followed from the shape of a project: a small team at the client’s site and a much larger one in India, the pyramid set out in Infosys’s 1999 registration statement.50 Its base had to be refilled every year with new graduates, and the growth of the base was what moved people upward.

The firms measured how much of the pyramid was earning money. Infosys called this utilisation. ‘We define employee utilization as the proportion of total billed person months to total available person months excluding support personnel,’ its filings said.51 In the year to March 2012, utilisation of billable staff was 69.0 per cent including trainees and 75.9 per cent excluding them, down from 72.1 and 79.6 per cent the year before, a fall the company told investors had hurt its profitability.52

Put the other way round, on average in that year nearly a third of Infosys’s billable staff, counting trainees, was not billed to a client. Some were in training. Others were between projects, or waiting for one to start. The industry called this the bench. In the firms’ own terms it was not waste. Most work was still billed by the time spent on it: in the year to March 2008 only 31 per cent of Infosys’s services revenue came from fixed-price projects, and the rest, 69 per cent, was billed on a ‘time-and-materials basis’.53 A firm paid that way must be able to staff a new contract at once, so it keeps trained people waiting. Here the book’s bargain shows at the scale of one firm. Revenue followed the number of people billed, so growth meant hiring ahead of demand, and hiring ahead of demand meant a bench. But the bench is a cost that grows with every hiring wave, and it is the first thing cut when demand falls, as it was in 2001-02 and again in 2008-09.

Billing by time also made the engineer’s time the thing to be managed. In Bangalore, Upadhya and Vasavi concluded that ‘extracting the maximum amount of time (and “effort”) out of software engineers is key to the profitability of services companies’, and one respondent described the firms’ systems for tracking hours as ‘a system of “time slavery”’.54 In their survey 61 per cent said they worked nine to ten hours a day on average, and staff of large Indian companies reported an average maximum of 13.8 hours, against 12.3 in multinationals.55 The complaints they recorded were mundane: ‘They’ll give out work at 5:30 in the evening and expect you to have it done by the next morning, which isn’t fair’.56 One informant explained why flexitime did not shorten the day: ‘What’s the point of working till late if you aren’t seen working?’57

Turnover was the other side of the pyramid. Infosys estimated the industry’s attrition rate, the share of staff leaving each year, at ‘approximately 20%’. Its own rate rose from 9.7 per cent in the year to March 2005 to 17.0 per cent in the year to March 2011.58 People in Chennai talked about job-hopping constantly. Fuller and Narasimhan found less of it than the talk suggested: among the more senior ICS staff they knew, at least half had spent all or most of their working lives in the company.59

One in twelve

Who passed through the gate? The best evidence for these years comes from the Chennai study. Between August 2003 and February 2005, Fuller, an anthropologist at the London School of Economics, and Narasimhan, who did most of the fieldwork, studied IT professionals in the city.60 Most of their interviews were at ICS.61 They interviewed nineteen of its staff and described their sample as ‘tiny’ and ‘the haphazard product of the problems of access’ in an industry concerned with security. ‘All names used are pseudonyms,’ they wrote.62 Everyone from the study named here is a real informant under a false name given by the authors, and most of their words come to us as the authors reported them.

At ICS the gate was a series of filters on school and college marks. Only students with a consistent record of high examination marks could sit the company’s aptitude test and go on to an interview, where one in twelve was offered a job.63 Starting salaries, in the middle of the range for large software firms, were ‘around 15,000 rupees per month’ for people in their early twenties (about $330), and could ‘easily double within a few years’.64 That put a new recruit’s own income near the level that only about 6 per cent of Indian households reached in 2001-02, according to estimates by the National Council of Applied Economic Research cited by the authors.65

The study also shows where these recruits came from. Most of the younger people the authors interviewed had fathers, and occasionally mothers, in professional or managerial posts in government or in public- or private-sector firms; the only exceptions were a few small businessmen and farmers.66 In this sample, IT did not lift families into the middle class from below. It moved the children of the salaried middle class, many of them the children of government engineers and officials, into a richer, private-sector version of the class they were born into. That is one city and one firm. In Bangalore, Upadhya and Vasavi’s survey of 132 software engineers found the same pattern: 84 per cent of their fathers worked in occupations ‘that can be identified as solidly middle class’.67 Together the two studies are the best evidence against the common claim that software created India’s middle class.

Inside, the work was organised around appraisal and deadlines. Staff were appraised every six months; those who beat their targets could expect quick promotion, and those who fell short were told they would be ‘let go’. Most worked a 40 to 50-hour, five-day week on flexitime, with extra hours when deadlines pressed. The offices were modern and open-plan, and young men and women, many of them unmarried, mixed there more freely than in most social settings in Chennai.68

The gate had a keeper. ICS’s personnel manager, whom the authors call Ramakrishna, was in his late thirties, held an MCA, and described his job as ‘tough and challenging’. In the ‘war for talent’ among the leading firms, it was his responsibility to make sure ICS recruited enough good people, above all by picking the best graduates from the top colleges. He earned about 70,000 rupees a month, plus stock options, and through much of 2003-04 he was negotiating for a rise while threatening to leave for a job in Bangalore.69

A young man the anthropologists call Shoaib shows what the move felt like. He was 29, ran the company’s intranet and website, and had a degree in computer science from a Muslim college in Chennai and an MCA. His father had been an engineer in the state electricity board. After his father’s early death Shoaib was offered a post at the board, and turned it down. He said he would never work in the public sector, because there you always ‘have to bow your head to somebody’.70 He was also, the authors noted, more worried than most of his colleagues about the future: ‘there is no security in this job’.71

Older managers in Chennai’s manufacturing firms watched the move with some resentment. Sampath, the personnel manager of a bus and lorry maker the study calls Chakra Vehicles, said the IT industry had ‘disturbed the social fabric’, with its ‘quick promotions’, ‘fancy designations’ and ‘phenomenal compensation’.72 One of his colleagues, the authors report, said that at campus interviews by the late 1990s only the weakest men still wanted to join Chakra, because everyone better was chasing IT salaries. Sampath himself, after more than twenty years, probably earned about 60,000 rupees a month, a level a competent IT professional could expect to reach in half the time.73

Where the money went is less glamorous than the stereotypes. As far as the authors could tell, most of it went on housing, bought, often with loans, much earlier than their parents had, and for those with children on private schooling.74 Most of the young people they met had never even considered a government or public-sector job. One ICS assistant manager had turned down a post at the Indian Space Research Organisation because he thought it would be unchallenging and monotonous.75 The city did not always welcome its new earners. Raghu shared a flat with two other young men in IT, and they were unpopular with their neighbours, who feared they would smoke, drink, cook meat and make a nuisance of themselves. In fact, the authors found, they never cooked at all. They ate every meal out, came home on weekdays only to sleep, and had friends round at weekends to drink beer.76

Marks, English and caste

The Chennai study’s most discussed finding is about caste, and the authors were careful with it. ICS, like many other IT companies in the city, ‘is said to be dominated by the high-status “Forward Castes”’, they wrote, especially Tamil Brahmans, who made up about 2.5 per cent of the state’s population, ‘and it is our strong impression that this is true’. But ‘no accurate figures exist’, and the guesses of people in the industry varied widely.77 In a later paper they repeated that Tamil Brahmans were ‘disproportionately well represented’ in south India’s IT industry, and that ‘no figures to confirm this assertion exist’, although all the circumstantial evidence pointed that way.78 The study’s terms are the ones used here. In its usage, ‘Forward Castes’, Brahmans among them, are those that cannot benefit from the reservation system of quotas in education and public employment, which is operated for the ‘Backward’ (and ‘Most Backward’) and ‘Scheduled’ Castes.79

The interest of the study lies less in the impression than in the explanation. Many people in Chennai said the reason was reservations. Tamil Nadu reserved 68 per cent of state-sector posts and college seats for the Other Backward Classes and the Scheduled Castes and Tribes, and many said this was why private companies employed so many Brahmans. ‘Yet reservations alone cannot fully explain upper-caste predominance in IT companies.’80

The authors offered three further reasons. First, Brahmans had become an increasingly urban community, more likely than others to have the social skills and cultural knowledge, fluent English among them, that global IT companies wanted and that their selection tests demanded.81 Second, the firms’ own rules did the sorting. ‘Because ICS allows only candidates with consistently high marks to apply for posts, its selection policy in practice favours Forward Caste candidates who, from a tender age, have been motivated to achieve repeated scholastic success.’82 Third, Brahman families placed a high value on education, notably in mathematics and science, and they were the best-educated community in the state. The authors dismissed as ‘fanciful’ the popular idea, held by many Tamil Brahmans themselves, that the caste had a special aptitude for software because of its traditional religious learning.83

A footnote shows how small the measurable differences were. In 2004 the cut-off mark for admission to electronics and communication engineering at Anna University, the most competitive course in the state, was 293.28 out of 300 for Forward Caste candidates, 290.57 for Backward Castes, 283.67 for Most Backward Castes and 276.13 for Scheduled Castes.84 The gap at the top was less than three marks in three hundred. The authors’ point was that the belief in needing exceptionally high marks shaped how children were raised, even where the arithmetic was less harsh than families thought.85

The argument does not need the firms to have looked at caste at all. A selection system that screened on marks, then on an aptitude test, then on an interview in English, reproduced the advantages of urban, educated, upper-caste families on its own. The Chennai evidence is one city and one firm, and it is not a national count.86 Bangalore points the same way. In Upadhya’s account of the NIAS survey, ‘Brahmins constituted 48 per cent of our sample’, upper castes as a whole 71 per cent, and upper and dominant agricultural castes together 86 per cent. ‘Only one respondent said that he belonged to a scheduled community.’87 The authors warned that 132 engineers, drawn mainly from multinationals and large and medium Indian firms, were ‘much too small to be representative’.88 The mechanism is visible elsewhere. In 2014 Aspiring Minds argued that in screening applications ‘the college name is a key signal, and resumes from unknown colleges aren’t shortlisted’, so that more than half of the graduates who met its benchmark for IT services jobs, studying outside the top 750 colleges, were ‘invisible to most employers’.89 It also estimated that a student from a third-tier college, with the same test score and marks as a student from a first-tier one, had 24 per cent lower odds of getting a job, and if hired was paid 66,000 rupees a year less.90 The claims come from a firm with an interest in selling another way to find such students. They point in the same direction: a filter that worked by college name fell hardest on students from the new private colleges and the smaller towns.

Women in the factory

At ICS, ‘approximately one-fifth’ of new recruits were women, close to the share across the industry.91 According to Nasscom, 79 per cent of India’s software professionals were men in 2001-02, a share it expected to fall to 65 per cent by 2005.92 In the colleges the gap was narrower. Among undergraduates enrolled in engineering and technology courses at AICTE-approved institutions in 2012, 33.7 per cent were women, according to the council’s figures as compiled by the Society of Women Engineers.93

What the Chennai researchers heard about women differs from what studies of Western software firms had found. Almost everyone they met, men and women alike, ‘insisted to us that both sexes have equal technical skills’.94 The difference between men and women at ICS lay elsewhere: ‘Married women professionals consistently give their families higher priority than their work.’95 Many men wanted ‘home-loving’ wives who would not work. So women in IT preferred husbands in IT, and men in IT tended to prefer wives who were not.96 One leading company, the authors reported, had started an in-house online matrimonial service to encourage its staff to marry each other, and so reduce the number of married women who resigned to follow husbands working elsewhere.97 The managers of two small software firms, one of them a woman, admitted that they were reluctant to recruit women, because family duties took them away from work.98

The study’s women show the range. A project manager in her early thirties, whom the authors call Anuradha, had made it a condition of her marriage that she and her husband return from America to Chennai. Her daughter was looked after by both sets of grandparents, and she ‘firmly believes that the IT industry has helped to empower women’.99 Jayashree, about 35, an assistant project manager with an MSc in physics from IIT Madras, had a different experience. Childcare for her two children was ‘an endlessly negotiated series of arrangements’ among hired help, her parents and her parents-in-law. Her husband, she complained, did not do his share at home; many chores, she joked, had been ‘outsourced’. She later resigned from ICS and did contract work for it from home.100

Two younger engineers on Jayashree’s team, called Sarojini and Parvati, were in their mid-twenties. Sarojini had an electrical and electronics degree from Coimbatore and joined ICS as a trainee in 2002. She told the researchers she found the work exciting and challenging, though her first ambition had been medicine, and she insisted that any husband would have to let her keep working. Her mother also worked, and she appeared, the authors thought, to put her determination down less to the IT industry than to her matrilineal Nayar background. Later in 2004 the company sent her to New Jersey.101 Parvati, with a BSc in computer science and an MSc in information technology, found her work boring after six months. A year later she was a team leader, determined to get a posting in America. One of her weekly tasks was to search an internet matrimonial site for a husband for her elder sister, and the profiles asking for ‘home-loving’ wives increasingly irritated her.102

The authors’ verdict held both halves together. ‘Plainly, the IT industry has not revolutionised the position of Indian women.’103 Yet when they compared their informants with the upper-middle-class Chennai women studied by Patricia Caplan in 1974-75, for most of whom ‘employment outside the home is not considered at all desirable’, they judged that ‘real change has occurred’.104 Young women in IT appeared to ‘command greater respect and have more bargaining power within their families’, particularly over whom they married.105

Trainable, not skilled

By the early 2010s a second number had joined the count of engineers. Only a quarter of Indian engineers were employable, it was said, or only a fifth. Both figures can be traced, and both come from parties with an interest.

The ‘quarter’ comes from the McKinsey Global Institute, the research arm of the consultancy. As the Duke study reported it, ‘McKinsey concluded that only 10% of Chinese engineers and 25% of Indian engineers can compete in the global outsourcing arena’, citing language, the quality of education, culture and other factors.106 As quoted, its question was not whether Indian engineers could find work, but how many could compete for outsourced work from rich countries.

The ‘fifth’ comes from Aspiring Minds. Its 2014 report tested ‘more than 1,20,000 engineering students from 520+ engineering colleges’ who graduated in 2013, on the company’s own test, a test it said was used by more than 650 companies.107 It found that ‘only 18.43% of them are employable for the Software Engineer-IT services role’.108 These are the shares of a tested sample that met the vendor’s benchmark for a role. They are not measures of whether graduates found jobs, and the company that produced them sold the test.

Read for what it measures, though, the report says something precise about the factory. The IT services figure, it explained, was calculated under the firms’ ‘current hiring philosophy’, in which a recruit was not expected to have software skills already but was trained ‘over a period of 3 to 6 months’. So 18.43 per cent was the share judged ‘trainable into software engineers within a period of 3 to 6 months’.109 The firms’ bar was trainability, not skill. That was the bar Rajaraman described and the one the Mysore campus was built for.

The same report contained an early warning. ‘The IT services industry is not growing at the same pace as before and the growth of entry-level jobs is diminishing,’ it said. Because firms were hiring fewer people and wanted to cut training costs, there was ‘a slow but definite trend towards hiring candidates who already have decent expertise in programming’.110 It is a vendor’s observation, from a firm that sold programming tests, but it is an early documented sign that the factory’s demand curve was bending.

The price of a place in the factory points the same way. The figures are rough and measure different things. ICS’s starting salary of about 15,000 rupees a month in 2003-05 came to about 180,000 rupees a year.111 A study by the business website Moneycontrol, relayed by the trade site HR Katha, put the median annual pay of freshers at large IT firms in 2012 at about 245,000 rupees.112 Adjusted for consumer prices, the first is worth about 660,000 rupees at 2024 prices and the second about 480,000.113 One is a single firm and the other a median, so the comparison can only be suggestive. It suggests that as the gate widened, the price of entry fell.

Routine work

What were the factory’s products made for? Fuller and Narasimhan answered in a sentence that matters for the rest of this book. ‘Because software engineering in companies like ICS or TCS has progressively become more routinized and reliant on standard packages, IIT graduates are over-qualified and can secure more technically demanding, better-paid jobs.’114 Most new recruits in the major firms, they went on, had only a bachelor’s degree in engineering, usually from one of the leading colleges in Tamil Nadu.115

The people doing the work said similar things. ‘Software engineering does not demand exceptional ability, as some experienced people acknowledge,’ the authors wrote. Shoaib went further and claimed that any reasonably intelligent school-leaver could learn the job. He was exaggerating, they said, but they had come across several IT professionals bored by work that was too easy.116 Raghu, keeping a British client’s system running, thought he was learning little. Parvati was bored at six months, a team leader at eighteen and set on a posting in America. Sarojini found the work challenging, and was sent to New Jersey.117 The firms did not need all their recruits to be stretched. They needed them to be trainable, reliable and available. Some managers said so, though not in the way Aspiring Minds’ account of firms that looked only at top colleges would suggest. Several large Indian services companies, Upadhya reported, preferred graduates of lower-ranked colleges ‘because the best students are not suitable for the kind of routinised work that is on offer’. As one HR manager put it, they required ‘guys who can just sit and code and not ask questions’.118

Outside observers drew the same line. The Duke team divided engineering graduates into ‘dynamic’ engineers, capable of abstract thinking and high-level problem solving, and ‘transactional’ engineers, who might have the fundamentals but were ‘typically responsible for rote and repetitive tasks in the workforce’.119 It was ‘the jobs of transactional engineers’, the team wrote in 2005, that seemed ‘easily outsourced’ to relatively low-paid engineers in countries like India.120 The phrase was American shorthand, and it was unkind to many Indian graduates who did far more than that. But it described the bulk of what the factory was designed to supply.

That was the factory’s design, and it fitted the business the Indian firms had built. A client abroad specified a system; a small team onsite fixed the requirements; a large team in India built, tested and maintained it, to documented methods, on standard packages, billed by time. A trained graduate of almost any engineering branch could do most of that work after three months in Mysore, and every graduate the factory turned out was more time to sell. The design succeeded.
