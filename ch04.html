The Engineer Factory

Chapter 4. Tata's experiment (1968-1978)

In January 1975 the Computer Society of India held its annual convention at Ahmedabad. Its president that year was F.C. Kohli, a power engineer who ran Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), a small computer-services company in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) owned by the Tata group. In his presidential address, as he quoted it himself more than thirty years later, Kohli told the delegates: 'Many years ago, there was an industrial revolution. We missed it due to reasons over which we had no control.'1 The new revolution, in information technology, was one 'which requires the capability to think clearly. This we have in abundance.' He went on: 'We have an opportunity to participate in this revolution on an equal footing; we even have the opportunity to be leaders.'2

It was a large claim to make in a country with a few hundred computers, most of them IBM's.3 Getting permission to import another one could take between one and three years.4 The company Kohli ran had begun as a bureau doing bank reconciliation, payroll and accounts for Tata companies and other Indian firms, and by its own later account its revenue passed $1 million in 1976.5

In the decade behind that speech, a data-processing unit set up to serve one business group found something it could sell abroad: the work of Indian programmers, done partly in Bombay on machines the company had to fight to import, and partly on the premises of American clients. A second, smaller venture sent American documents to Poona (now Pune) to be typed onto tape. Neither firm set out to create an export industry. The rules on foreign exchange, the scarcity of computers and the shape of the American market pushed both of them towards one.

Half-hour slots

The people who did the early work are known mainly through the memories of men who later ran the companies, but the memories are specific. S. Ramadorai joined TCS in 1972 and became its chief executive in 1996. In 2008 The Economist, drawing on him and other long-serving staff, described the computers of his early years as bulky and temperamental, in need of assembly, installation and constant repair. 'You had to work with your own hands,' a colleague recalled.6 Jayant Pendharkar, later the company's chief marketing officer, remembered a new computer arriving in three truckloads.7 Programmers booked half-hour slots on the Burroughs terminals, and the magazine adds that 'One hour's computer time cost more than a programmer earned in a month.'8 Seturaman Mahalingam, later the chief financial officer, worked on an early offshore project in Feltham, outside London, and posted his instructions to the programmers in Bombay twice, a day apart, in case one postbag went astray. A telephone call from London to Bombay, the magazine adds, would be interrupted every few minutes by the operator, checking that the caller wanted to keep talking.9

In Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1972, Narendra Patni and his wife, Poonam, tested a business of their own in their apartment. Dinesh Sharma, a journalist and historian of the industry, writes that they made the living room stand for the United States and the bedroom for India.10 At the end of the decade Nandan Nilekani, as he recalled for the Computer History Museum, was interviewed with puzzles by N.R. Narayana Murthy in a small cabin at Nariman Point in Bombay and hired on the spot for Patni's software division. The firm's Data General computer was often idle at night, 'and for those of us who had grown up sending these cards, to have a terminal where you could do whatever you wanted was unbelievable luxury.' He worked all night on it, learning, and part of the job was teaching the customers who had bought the machine.11

The magazine's comparison of an hour of computer time with a month of a programmer's pay holds the economics of the decade in one line. The machine was dear and the programmer was cheap. The business that grew out of these years would be built on the difference.

A bureau for the group

TCS began as a computer centre for the Tata group's own companies. The likeliest reading of the record is that the centre existed, with an IBM 1401, by late 1967, and that the company built on it was constituted as Tata Consultancy Services in 1968.12

The founding staff came from management schools and statistics more than from engineering. Sharma names three graduates of MIT's management school who had worked as interns with the Tatas, Lalit Surajmal Kanodia, Nitin Patel and Ashok Malhotra, and a fourth founding member, Yashpal Sahni, a postgraduate of the Indian Statistical Institute.13 The Tata group's tribute to Kohli says that he recruited the most promising engineers, chartered accountants, and science and mathematics postgraduates.14 Whatever the industry later became, its first company was not staffed only by engineers, still less only by graduates of the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs).

The work was the work of a service bureau: bank reconciliation, payroll and accounting.15 A profile of Kohli in MIT Technology Review, based on interviews with him, adds two jobs: reconciling transactions between the branches of a nationalised bank, and producing Bombay's telephone directory.16 These were the standard uses of a commercial computer in India at the time. The Committee on Automation, appointed by the Ministry of Labour to study what computers would do to jobs, noted that one of the important uses of the State Bank of India's computer was to reconcile inter-branch transactions, about 70,000 of them a day.17 Most Tata companies already rented IBM 1401s, so a Tata bureau that sold computing to Tata companies was doing, inside the group, what IBM did for its clients.18

The control engineer

Faqir Chand Kohli came to computers through electric power. Born in Peshawar in 1924, he took a physics degree at Government College, Lahore, and in 1946, 'before the partition', as he put it, went on a scholarship to Queen's University in Canada to study electrical engineering.19 He worked for about a year at General Electric, took a master's degree at MIT on a scholarship, and learned load dispatch, the scheduling of power stations' output across a grid, at the New England Power Company.20 In 1951 he came back to India and joined Tata Electric in Bombay, which wanted to set up what he called 'the first load dispatch in India'.21

Ross Bassett, a historian of technology who interviewed Kohli, describes him as an MIT-trained control engineer, a specialist in the regulation of systems, which was one of the fields from which computing grew.22 Kohli's ties to IIT Kanpur gave him his practical introduction to computing, and he brought digital computers into Tata Electric's system control in the late 1960s.23 In 1973 he became the first Indian to sit on the board of directors of the IEEE, the international but American-oriented society of electrical engineers.24

The Tata group's chairman, J.R.D. Tata, asked him to take on the new computer company.25 In 1969 he moved from Tata Electric to TCS as general manager, succeeding Kanodia.26 In 1974, according to the Technology Review profile, the director of TCS died and Kohli succeeded him.27 He stayed for three decades. In 2020 he corrected an interviewer who dated his retirement to 1996: he had worked until 1999, when he was seventy-five.28

Machines on a budget

The company's first machine was a rented IBM 1401, in place from September 1967. The first it bought, on the likeliest reading, was an ICL 1903, made by the British firm International Computers, in use from 1971.29

How it bought the ICL is the best story in the Technology Review profile, and it rests on that profile, which drew on Kohli's own account. According to the profile, the machine, never used, had belonged to a government-owned insurance company where a labour union had blocked its installation, calling it a monster that would eat jobs. TCS bought it for a fraction of its original price.30 If the story is accurate, it connects the two halves of the Indian argument about computers. A union's veto in one public enterprise produced a cheap computer for a private firm, which would come to use machines like it for work that displaced no Indian clerks, because the clients were abroad.

New machines were harder to get than old ones. Every import needed a licence from the Department of Electronics (DoE), and the delay, V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, writes, 'particularly hurt companies which wanted to import computers for software export.'31 Old machines had been the way round the problem. Sharma writes that IBM and ICL had imported used computers until an audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General led the government to rein them in and give the DoE control of the matter.32

Up, but not running

The domestic market could not keep the company going. 'TCS was up, but it wasn't running,' Kohli told the Technology Review. The company struggled, and took on management-consulting work to stay afloat.33

Near the end of his life Kohli told a simpler story. In an interview for the Computer History Museum in January 2020, when he was ninety-five, he said: 'First few years it was built only on exports. People didn't understand.'34 Asked about strategy, he said that he had built strong links with the engineering colleges, starting with IIT Kanpur, so that people knew about the company: 'There was no question of my going for business. The people came to us, "Could you do this for us?"'35 The better evidence points the other way. The company's own histories list domestic bureau work as its first business,36 and Sharma found that Kohli, courting Burroughs, visited the United States at least nine times between 1972 and 1974.37

A second witness, from outside TCS, confirms how thin the Indian market was. Murthy, who later founded Infosys, had worked for a French company in Paris in the early 1970s. Within a couple of years of his return he started a firm to write software for Indian customers. Within seven or eight months, he told the Computer History Museum, he realised 'that there was no domestic market in India for software. There were hardly any computers in India'. He closed the firm and set out to learn whether his market would have to be global, 'a market particularly in the United States'.38 His is a founder's retrospective, but it comes from a different firm and points the same way.

Burroughs

TCS tied its fortunes to Burroughs, a computer maker based in Detroit and, in Kohli's words, the 'number two computer company at that time'.39 TCS became Burroughs' exclusive agent in India, in a relationship formed in 1973-74.40

In the account Kohli gave the Technology Review, Burroughs wanted a healthcare software package to sell with its new computers, and TCS had no Burroughs machine in India to write it on. Its programmers wrote the software on the ICL and built a filter, a program that converted it to run on Burroughs hardware. The job made Burroughs a large repeat client.41 Other accounts give other first jobs: Sharma describes operating-system work for a new series of Burroughs mainframes, and Rajaraman the conversion of software written for other makers' computers.42 The Tata group's own timeline counts the conversion of a hospital-accounting system for a Detroit client in 1973 as the first recorded case of offshore software delivery, which is the company's claim about itself.43 Sharma lists the clients who followed as the Detroit Police, the City of Detroit, the State University of New York and several banks.44

Whichever account is closest, the work had a pattern that would last. Programmers in Bombay wrote code for machines they could not see, and the code was carried to the United States to run. Software was the one product a computer-starved country could make without many computers of its own, so long as it had one machine to write on and a way to move the result.

The partnership spread beyond services. In 1975 Tata and Burroughs formed a company to manufacture computers in Bombay. They also set up a separate software unit, Tata Burroughs Limited, which, with TCS, remained India's top software exporter for a long time.45 TCS also looked well beyond Detroit. The company lists a project for an electric utility in Iran in 1971 and one for a British housing society, done for Burroughs, in 1974.46 Rajaraman says TCS 'actively scouted for programming work' in Iran, Switzerland, South Africa and Canada as well as the United States, and that it 'was able to demonstrate that software services export from India was feasible and profitable'.47 In 1976, according to the Tata group, Kohli received an award as a leading exporter from the Engineering Export Promotion Council.48 The Tata group's tribute to Kohli adds that customers valued the company's quality of service at a low cost, the more so after the 1973 oil crisis.49 That is the company's own statement of the cost advantage on which the industry would be built.

Programmers on site

Not all the work could be done in Bombay. William Aspray, an American historian of computing, writes that Burroughs asked TCS to send programmers to the United States to install systems software for Burroughs customers, and he treats that request as the birth of India's software export business.50 A manufacturer installing machines at its customers' premises needed programmers there, and TCS supplied them. The practice came to be called, derisively, 'body shopping', and once TCS had succeeded, other Indian firms began to offer the same service.51

How TCS charged Burroughs or its American clients in the 1970s, by the person-month, the hour or a fixed price for each job, is not shown by any contract or rate card that has come to light.52 The conversion and package work suggests fixed deliverables; the installation work on site suggests time-based billing. So it cannot be said that billing by the hour began here. A review of Ramadorai's own history of the company says only that the arrangement with Burroughs let TCS do part of the development work in India and part at client locations abroad.53 The industry's first exports combined two things: code written at home and people's time spent at a client's site. Chapter 6 follows how the second came to dominate.

Data by air

The second experiment was smaller, and in one way it went further. Narendra Patni had studied at MIT and, according to Bassett, worked with the computer pioneer and entrepreneur Jay Forrester, doing consulting work and taking part in his business ventures.54 Patni formed Data Conversion Inc., the forerunner of his later Patni Computer Systems, to convert paper records into data on paper and magnetic tape.55 After the test in the Cambridge apartment, a team of data-entry operators was hired in Poona, two dozen by Sharma's account, twenty by another.56 The work was keying American documents onto tape in India and sending the tapes back. It was an early form of the offshore 'process' work that would fill the back offices of the 1990s, and it began two decades before them.

Patni then moved into software, forming Patni Computer Systems with his brothers Gajendra and Ashok, both graduates of IIT Delhi, in the late 1970s. It began by reselling minicomputers made by the American firm Data General and then wrote software for Data General's customers.57 In August 1977 Patni hired Murthy to head its software division. By the time he arrived, he recalled in 2018, the Patnis had 'also started a business to develop software for export to the United States'.58 The division's staff by the end of the decade included Nilekani and Kris Gopalakrishnan; in 1981 Murthy and six colleagues left to found Infosys.59

Who did the work

The profession at this stage was defined by the task, whether systems programming, conversion or data entry, rather than by a degree. TCS's recruits included chartered accountants and science and mathematics postgraduates as well as engineers; the operators in Poona needed typing, not engineering; and the people who joined Patni's software division at the end of the decade had a year or so of experience each.60 Most of those in India who could already program had been trained by IBM, the public-sector Electronics Corporation of India, Kanpur's short courses or the state's software training centre in Bombay.61 TCS drew on these pools and trained its own staff on the job. Kohli said in 2009 that the company had judged early on that technology became obsolete 'in less than 24 months', so that its staff would need continuing education.62 His view of the raw material, as he put it in 2020, was simple: 'Indians are capable of doing a lot of things. And only thing is they have to receive some training.'63

Three readings

Three explanations of this decade can be drawn from the record, and they are not exclusive. The first is the founders' own. Kohli saw the potential of the technology, recruited the best people and, in 1975, told the profession what India could become. The Tata group's tributes tell the story this way, and so, mostly, did Kohli.64 It is a self-description, and Kohli himself resisted its strongest form. When the Computer History Museum's interviewer called him the father of the industry, he replied: 'Father is a misnomer.' He was, he said, the father of his three children, and that was all. Then he added: 'I did set up the computer industry in this country, and that is that. People know it.'65 The second reading, which Rajaraman's and Aspray's accounts support, is that the export industry was a by-product of import controls and foreign-exchange rules: firms exported because exports were the price of getting machines, and because there was too little demand at home.66 The third is Bassett's. What mattered, he argues, was the pairing of an American-style engineering education, at Kanpur and elsewhere, with men such as Kohli and Patni who had American training and American contacts.67

The record bears on two claims that recur in histories of the industry. The first is that TCS was India's first software company. It began as a bureau selling computing to its own group, as IBM's data centres in the big cities already did for smaller firms. As a firm that exported software services it was among the first, with Patni's venture beside it.68 The second is that the first offshore development centre in India was opened by Texas Instruments in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) in 1985 (Chapter 8). Software written in Bombay for Burroughs in 1973-74, and data keyed in Poona from 1972, show Indian firms doing work for foreign clients in India more than a decade earlier, though without satellite links and not in a centre owned by a foreign company.69

Twice the value

The rule that turned scarcity into an export business took shape in the early 1970s. The DoE allowed computers to be imported exclusively for software export. Rajaraman had told a conference in 1970 that foreign exchange could also be earned by software export.70 Its working is clearest in TCS's own purchase of a Burroughs computer in 1974. The machine cost $340,000. With Indian tariffs TCS paid more than twice that.71 The Economist itemised the tariff at 101.25 per cent: import duty, auxiliary duty, countervailing duty and a levy to help pay for the war in Bangladesh.72 The government let the machine in on one condition. In the Technology Review's words, 'Kohli had to commit to exporting twice the value of the machine over a five-year period.'73

On the machine's price alone, that meant at least $680,000 of earnings abroad in five years, or about $136,000 a year. The size of the commitment shows against the national figures. In 1977, by a contemporary West German estimate, the whole of India's software exports came to about Rs 10 million, roughly $1.1 million, and the firms named as reaching markets abroad were TCS and the Operations Research Group of Baroda (now Vadodara).74 In 1980, Aspray writes, twenty-one Indian firms were exporting about $4 million a year, mostly by sending people to client sites.75 One import licence could commit a single firm to earning abroad, each year, more than a tenth of the whole country's software exports in 1977.

A firm in Bombay had few ways to earn that money. It could not sell computers, which it could barely import. It could sell the work of its staff, as code written in Bombay or as programmers sent to the client. The machine was the dear part of the business and the programmer the cheap one, and the obligation could be met only by selling the cheap part abroad. Each new computer had to be paid for with engineers' work sold abroad, and each addition to the stock of machines meant more people whose time would pay for them. This is the bargain the rest of the book follows, written down for the first time in an import licence: a computer priced in the future labour of Indian programmers.

The rule was not watertight. Aspray concludes that by 1980 most of the hardware imported into India was being used for domestic purposes, not for producing software exports.76 But for TCS, and for the firms that copied it, the condition fixed the shape of the business.

1978

The decade closed with a break. The Tata group dates the end of TCS's partnership with Burroughs to 1978.77 The reviewer Rashmi Bansal writes that Ramadorai's history of the company presents that year as the gravest test TCS had faced, because Tata Sons formed a formal joint venture with Burroughs and left TCS to fend for itself.78 The same year IBM, which had installed most of India's computers and trained many of its programmers, left the country, and the government at last let private Indian companies make minicomputers (Chapter 5).79 India had fewer than 1,000 computers.80 In 1979 TCS sent Ramadorai to the United States to open its first overseas office. Free now to work with other computer makers, it had to find its own customers.81 What it offered them was what the export licence had already taught it to sell: the time of its programmers.
