The Engineer Factory

Chapter 11. Y2K and Nasdaq (1995-2002)

At twenty past six on a Tuesday morning in late 2000, a correspondent for Business Today, R. Sukumar, stood on a street in BTM Layout, a growing neighbourhood in south Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), opposite a restaurant called Udipi Garden. He was waiting for a company bus. It was one of the buses that carried close to 4,000 people a day to the campus of Infosys Technologies on Hosur Road, and he had been told that many of its staff lived nearby. Staff from outside the south, he learned later, often clubbed together in threes or fours to sub-let a flat or house in neighbourhoods like this one.1 On the bus, almost everyone was young and almost nobody talked. People read newspapers, one young woman read what looked like a technical book, and a few at the front fell asleep. The company discouraged its junior staff from riding motorcycles to work, because Hosur Road was so dangerous.2 The crowd was more mixed than he had expected: many from the south of India, as he had assumed, but also faces from the west, the north and the north-east.3

The bus reached the campus at a quarter past seven. Sukumar had visited in the summer of 1995, when it had one office block, one canteen and one small gymnasium. Now it covered 50 acres and had five office blocks, three canteens, a branch of the old Bangalore eating house Mavalli Tiffin Rooms, a coffee bar, a pizza outlet, a swimming pool, a sauna, a medical centre and a small golf green.4 It also had a three-storey training centre, built for Rs 23.6 crore (about $5 million), where every batch of new engineers went through a three-month course. The whole campus, the magazine reported, would eventually cost Rs 320 crore, about $71 million. Its chief financial officer, T.V. Mohandas Pai, showed off a building whose curved walls, he said, had taken more than 4,000 equations to get right.5 The cars that drove in a little before eight were mostly small Marutis and Santros. Everyone paid market prices for food in the canteens.6

The magazine had named Infosys the best employer in India, and its article was written in that spirit.7 But its numbers were specific. The share price had risen from Rs 63.75 in early April 1996 to Rs 7,160 on 15 December 2000. Of the company’s 7,925 employees at the end of September 2000, 7,853 had been offered stock options, creating, the magazine reported, ‘1,773 rupee millionaires and 213 dollar millionaires’.8

Five and a half years earlier this firm had walked out of a negotiation with its largest customer and lost a quarter of its business. In between, the leading Indian services firms turned a way of organising work into something they could certify, sell and list on an American stock exchange. A programming error in old American software, the millennium bug, brought them inside the largest companies in the United States. Then the market that had made their employees rich turned and fell.

A quarter of the revenue

In May 1995, in the telling of N.R. Narayana Murthy, the company’s co-founder, Infosys alone among the Indian suppliers of General Electric (GE) refused its demand for lower prices and handed its GE work to competitors (Chapter 9).9

Two lessons can be read in the company’s later filings. The first was about dependence. When Infosys registered its shares with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in February 1999, its largest client provided 10.5 per cent of revenue in the year to March 1998, and it had about 130 clients.10 The same document listed among the causes of uneven results ‘the size, timing and profitability of significant projects’.11 By the year to March 2002 the largest client provided 6.1 per cent of revenue and the five largest together 24.1 per cent. In both filings the company described its mix of work as chosen ‘in an effort to avoid service or client concentration’.12 The second lesson, in Murthy’s own account, was about price. He said in 2020 that ‘at least until 2007, 2008, probably 2009, Infosys always got 20-25 percent higher price compared to our competitors in India’. He credited a sales method the company called Business Value Leverage, which meant talking to clients about what the work would be worth to them rather than about technology.13 The claim is the founder’s alone; no independent price data for the period have been located.

What the filings do show is how fast the firm grew from its small base. Revenue rose from $9.5 million in the year to March 1994 to $68.3 million in the year to March 1998.14 Over the same years the number of its IT professionals rose from about 480 to about 2,200, and its development centres in India from two to nine.15 Revenue grew at a compound rate of 63.6 per cent a year.16

Part of that growth came from the currency. In the year to March 1998, 90.0 per cent of Infosys’s revenue was earned in US dollars, and the filing said that ‘a significant portion’ of its costs, including pay, would continue to be in rupees. Between December 1994 and December 1998 the rupee lost about 39.5 per cent of its value against the dollar.17 A firm that earned dollars and paid rupees gained from every fall in the rupee unless it raised salaries to match. Process, Y2K and good management all contributed to the margins of these years. So did the exchange rate.

Selling the process

To sell work that would be done ten thousand miles from the client, the firms had to show that it would be done properly. They sought two certificates. The international quality standard ISO 9001 was awarded after auditors inspected a firm’s documentation, testing and project methods, and the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) of the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University graded the ‘maturity’ of a firm’s development process on five levels.18 In the account of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, ISO 9001 certification was ‘essential for bidding for software projects in Europe whereas the CMM maturity level is valued more in the USA’.19

Motorola’s Global Software Group, set up in India in 1991, says that its Indian centre reached Level 5 in 1993 and was ‘the first commercial organization in the world’ to do so, a claim Rajaraman repeats.20 A Business Today report of January 2003 says only that the Indian unit was the first company in India to be certified, in 1994, at an unstated level, so the world first remains Motorola’s own claim.21

By the end of the decade Indian firms held a large share of the highest ratings, though the sources do not agree on how large. The researcher AnnaLee Saxenian, writing in 2001, said that in December 1999, 137 Indian companies had ISO 9000 or CMM Level 2 certification, ‘and ten companies were certified at Level 5 (the highest level, at which only six U.S. companies are certified.)’22 Rajaraman gives a different figure for the same year: ‘In 1999, 6 out of 12 CMM level 5 certified companies in the world were in India.’23 Either way, a handful of Indian firms held a large share of the top grade, and the industry said so loudly. ‘Quality certification’, Saxenian wrote, ‘serves as an important marketing device’.24 The share kept growing. By January 2003, Business Today reported, 54 of the 78 organisations assessed at Level 5 worldwide were Indian companies or the Indian operations of multinationals, against 19 American companies.25 The magazine also recorded what the grade was for. At a Nasscom-Gartner summit in Mumbai in September 2002, one of the chief executives present, whom it did not name, explained how it helped: ‘When you tell customers that you are a Level 5 company,’ he said, ‘they shut up and listen.’26

Infosys described its own standing in the February 1999 filing: it had ‘been certified at Level 4, a level achieved by only 2% of the more than 1,000 software companies tested under the Capability Maturity Model’.27 Both the 2 per cent and the word ‘certified’ are the company’s. The SEI’s process was an appraisal, and the industry described the result as a certificate.

Rajaraman also explained why process mattered to the firms themselves. Both certificates, he wrote, ‘require emphasis on well documented processes which every software engineer in the organization is expected to follow’. And ‘The process orientation allowed companies which were recruiting large numbers of employees each year to quickly train them to become productive members of a development team. Staff attrition also could be managed better due to the disciplined software development process.’28 Rajaraman presents this as a benefit, and it was one. But it also describes what process did to the engineer. A firm that hired thousands of graduates a year, and lost many of them to rivals or to American visas, needed work broken into documented steps that any trained graduate could follow and any other could take over. The certificate reassured the client. The process underneath it made individual engineers easier to replace. Rajaraman does not draw the conclusion, but his sentence points to it: documented, standardised work is also the kind machines learn most easily. Chapter 19 sets that reading against what the firms’ own filings later said about tools for coding, testing and documentation.

The pyramid

The work itself was organised as a pyramid. Infosys’s 1999 filing described a typical project: a ‘small team of two to five IT professionals’ visited the client to set the scope, then its managers went back to India ‘to supervise a much larger team of 10 to 50 IT professionals’, with the client’s systems ‘linked via satellite’ to the Indian centres.29 A few experienced people faced the client. Many junior people did the work in India. The filing said the company did about 80 per cent of its project work in India.30

The firm described its own model as an industry practice. ‘The Indian offshore development model was initiated in the mid-1980’s,’ the filing said (Chapter 6 tests that dating against the founders’ own careers), and it listed what attracted clients: a large pool of English-speaking professionals, ‘relatively low labor costs’, international quality standards, the ‘capability to work on specific projects on a 24-hour basis by exploiting time zone differences’, and the ability to run phases of a project in parallel.31 By 2002 the language had changed. The annual report for the year to March 2002 said that ‘Our clients leverage our proprietary Global Delivery Model to achieve higher quality, rapid time-to-market and cost-effective solutions.’32 In three years an industry practice had become a company brand.

For the industry as a whole, the move offshore was real but slower than the brand suggests. On each of the period’s series, more than half of the industry’s export work was still done at clients’ sites abroad, which is why the story of the H-1B visa (Chapter 13) runs alongside this one.33

The pyramid was efficient at turning cheap hours into exports, but by international standards it produced little revenue per head (Chapter 10 gives Saxenian’s comparison with Israel and Ireland). Saxenian saw the largest firms, such as Wipro and Infosys, trying to escape that trap: winning larger contracts ‘often on a turnkey basis’, charging higher rates, and turning what they learned on one project into software components they could reuse for the next.34 The same pages record the other way out. Indian programmers, aware of what their skills earned abroad, were leaving, many of them on American visas.35

The pyramid had a consequence that a senior Infosys manager described in the Business Today article, published in January 2001. Sales had more than doubled in the first half of the year to March 2001, and the magazine noted that growth widened the middle and top of the organisation. ‘We are growing so fast that many people with two to three years experience are getting into managerial roles,’ said Hema Ravichandar, the senior vice-president for human resources.36 Fast promotion for early recruits depended on a base that grew faster still. That was the book’s bargain in miniature: revenue grew with the number of engineers billed, so the base of the pyramid had to keep widening. Not everyone wanted to climb. ‘I don’t have to become a project manager if I don’t want to,’ said Sushil, who worked in the company’s mobile-commerce practice and is identified in the article only by his first name. ‘I can focus on the technology and become a technical consultant to projects.’37

The millennium bug

The work that filled the base of the pyramid in the late 1990s was Y2K. Rajaraman explains the problem simply. Programs written in the 1970s and 1980s, ‘mostly in COBOL’, stored the year as two digits to save memory, on the assumption that the first two were 19. When the year became 2000 they would give wrong results, and every such program had to be found and corrected. ‘This bug fix was labour intensive. There were not enough software engineers in the USA and Europe to fix the bug and it had to be outsourced. This was a godsend opportunity to many fledgling software services companies in India.’38

At Infosys, the 1999 filing shows how quickly the work arrived. Y2K projects made up about 7.5 per cent of revenue in the year to March 1997 and 23.3 per cent in the year to March 1998.39 In the nine months to December 1998 the share was 21.4 per cent.40 The often repeated figure of 23 per cent ‘in 1997’ is the same number, since the company’s fiscal year 1998 ran from April 1997 to March 1998.41 Y2K tripled as a share of the business in a single year and had already begun to shrink as a share by the end of 1998.

The company was frank with American investors about what kind of work this was. ‘The high demand for these time-sensitive projects results in pricing and margins that are favorable to the Company,’ the filing said. It expected demand to ‘begin to diminish rapidly after fiscal 1999’, and it could not promise to replace the work with other work at the same margins.42 Then came a sentence about people. ‘There is an additional risk that the Company may be unable to retrain and redeploy IT professionals who are currently assigned to Year 2000 conversion projects involving legacy computer systems after such projects are completed.’43 In a document filed under American securities law, the leading Indian firm said that a wave of demand had trained a cohort of its engineers for work that would soon end, and that it might not find other work for them.

For the industry, the figures come from the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry’s lobby group, as reported by the trade newsletter Computergram in June 1999. Indian software exports in FY1998-99 were $2.65 billion, up 51.4 per cent, and Y2K work accounted for $560 million of that, or 21.1 per cent. Nasscom said that 203 of the Fortune 1000 companies used Indian software services, and that 25 firms earned 63 per cent of all exports.44 Two weeks earlier the same newsletter had reported a larger Nasscom estimate: that Indian firms had earned $2.3 billion from the bug, might reach $3 billion by the end of 1999, and would earn only ‘a few hundred million dollars’ from it the following year.45 The cumulative figure is hard to reconcile with $560 million in the peak year unless it spans several years or other kinds of work; the share of a single year’s exports, about a fifth, is the safer number. The same report described the trap that the end of the work would set. Large companies around the world, it said, were holding back from buying new computer systems until the millennium had passed, and were no longer spending much on the bug either, because most of it had been dealt with. Indian firms that had relied on Y2K feared that their profits would fall.46

Nasscom already had an answer to the end of the work. Its president, Dewang Mehta, spoke of ‘three Es’, electronic commerce, enterprise resource planning (ERP, the integrated software that large companies were then installing to run their accounts, stock and staff) and euro conversion, and of an export target of $50 billion by FY2007-08.47 The euro was a second conversion job. The European Union announced in 1995 that the single currency would be used from 1 January 1999, and banks and other financial institutions had to change their programs before then. In Rajaraman’s words, this ‘was a labour intensive job and provided software conversion contracts to numerous Indian software companies’.48

The millennium arrived without a crisis. In early 2000 the news magazine Outlook asked in a headline whether the whole thing had been ‘The Rs 1,800 Cr Hoax?’. It put the world’s spending on the problem at $600 billion, about $150 billion of it in the United States. It reported Nasscom’s estimate that Y2K preparations had cost India Rs 1,800 crore across government and private industry, of which Rs 600 crore was for software and services.49 The magazine converted the total to $3.6 billion. At the 1999 average exchange rate it was about $420 million.50 The article also recorded what the night had been like for the firms. Rajeev K. Arora, resident manager of Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), a company that by the magazine’s account had written more than 650 million lines of code for Y2K correction, said TCS had bought overseas tickets and arranged visas so that it could fly its engineers to clients if anything went wrong, and had stocked diesel for its generators.51 Mehta’s own verdict, as Outlook quoted it, was that ‘Y2K was a godsend and might prove to be the turning point for the industry.’52

Was it the turning point? The export figures say not. In the series Rajaraman reproduces, Indian software exports had grown by between about a quarter and two-thirds a year in every year from 1990-91 to 1997-98, most of that before Y2K work became large.53 At its peak Y2K was about a fifth of the industry’s exports and about a fifth of the leading firm’s revenue, and that firm expected it to fade quickly. What Y2K did, on the best evidence, was open doors. Rajaraman writes that many firms ‘earned the goodwill of clients by not only fixing the Y2K bug but also improving the software (when appropriate) at no extra cost’, and that ‘This opportunity gave many software services companies entry to the Fortune 500 companies and enabled them to get software development contracts later.’54 N. Chandrasekaran, who had joined TCS in 1987 and became its chief executive in 2009, gave a similar account to the Computer History Museum in 2020, two decades after the event. The Year 2000 work, he recalled, ‘gave us scale, because everyone wanted a solution’, and clients who saw it done well began to ask what else the firm could do for them.55 He also gives part of the credit for the quiet rollover to the programmers ‘working diligently in India between 1993 and 1999’.56 That is his judgement, and the Outlook article shows that it was not universally shared at the time.57 The common belief that Y2K made the Indian industry is half right. It accelerated growth that had already begun, and it put Indian engineers inside the systems of large American companies, some of which kept them on for other work.

Two hundred thousand applications

The engineers on the bus in 2000 had been chosen from a very large crowd: in the year to March 2002 Infosys received about 193,640 applications and about 1,550 people joined, about one in 125, against about one in 55 four years earlier.58 These are the company’s figures, and it used them to advertise itself, but they also show a supply of graduates who wanted these jobs growing far faster than even the fastest-growing firm could take them. Infosys still selected by its own tests and taught its own methods.59

The firm explained its appeal in its own terms. In 1999 it told American investors that, because of its stock option plan, it had ‘become one of the most sought after employers for Indian engineering graduates’, with attrition ‘significantly below the industry average’. It added that ‘India is home to the world’s second largest population of English-speaking technical talent’.60

The education that mattered most happened after the degree, in the three-month course that every batch of new engineers took in the training centre the reporter had been shown.61 The firm selected from a vast pool, taught its recruits its own methods, and placed them into a documented process.

The people inside it were mostly engineers. The magazine counted 4,616 employees on the Bangalore campus in late 2000, and said that apart from the 840 people in the company’s staff functions, such as human resources, finance, marketing and administration, everyone was an engineer.62 The magazine noticed the strain as well as the perks. ‘Every time things get a little too much for me,’ said an employee who, it reported, ‘not infrequently puts in 16 hours a day at work’, ‘I go out for a smoke, or a walk, or play a few holes of golf.’ Muthamma Acharya, ‘a twenty-something marketing executive’, described the pace of hiring: ‘You come in one morning and find the breakfast line 400-people longer’.63 Suparna Shankar, an assistant manager in marketing for the Asia-Pacific region, summed up the place for the magazine: ‘You can create a role for yourself.’64 These were the voices a magazine chose for a story about the best employer in India.

Nasdaq

Infosys had listed in India in 1993, in an offering Murthy remembered as ‘a very tough time’.65 Six years later it went to New York. The final prospectus, dated 10 March 1999 and filed the next day, offered 1.8 million American Depositary Shares (ADSs), certificates traded in the United States that each represented half an Indian share, at $34.00 each. The offering raised $61.2 million before an underwriting discount of $1.68 a share. The shares were to be quoted on the Nasdaq National Market under the symbol INFY.66 The American price was set a little below the equivalent price in Mumbai, and until then, the prospectus noted, there had been no public market for the company’s shares in the United States.67

Murthy gave a specific reason for the listing. Infosys had prepared its accounts under American accounting rules voluntarily from 1995, so that clients could read them. ‘Later on, we said, “Look. We have to create an employee stock option plan in the U.S. currency.” Therefore, we wanted ADRs [American Depository Receipts], and that is how we got listed on NASDAQ in 1999.’68 In his account, the listing was as much about paying staff as about raising money.

Others followed. In June 1999 Mehta of Nasscom said he expected up to ten Indian firms to list in the United States over the next twelve to eighteen months.69 The next was not a software services firm but an internet company. Satyam Infoway, the internet subsidiary of Satyam Computer Services, priced its ADSs on 18 October 1999 at what the filing gives as $4.50 each, and they began trading on Nasdaq the next day.70 Satyam Computer Services owned about 52.5 per cent of it in 2002.71 A rival Indian content provider, Rediff.com, completed its own American offering in June 2000.72

Rupee millionaires

The listing turned paper into wealth for employees because Infosys had given them paper. ‘In 1994, the Company established the ESOP, one of the first employee stock option plans in India,’ the 1999 filing said. A trust set up for the purpose held warrants for shares, and employees received rights to buy shares at Rs 100 each, $2.35 at the exchange rate of February 1999, which the filing called ‘substantially below the market price’. The rights vested over five years.73 The filing explains the plan at length because under American accounting rules the discount was a cost, and under Indian rules it was not.74 Stock options were so unusual in India that the government’s information technology task force of 1998, among its 108 recommendations, proposed allowing ‘sweat equity’ and employee stock option plans, which Rajaraman describes as ‘alien to other industries in India’. That required a change in company law.75

A rupee millionaire, in the magazine’s count, held at least Rs 10 lakh (Rs 1 million), about $22,000 at the 2000 exchange rate.76 That was a large sum for an engineer in his or her twenties, but not a fortune. A dollar millionaire was another matter. Infosys’s sales had grown from Rs 88.97 crore in the year to March 1996 to Rs 884.35 crore in the year to March 2000.77 The new wealth reached the city. ‘Indeed, a couple of enterprising Bangalore-based real-estate developers make a living primarily from selling apartments to people who work for Infosys,’ the magazine wrote.78 In 2020 Murthy put the scale of the programme in his own words: Infosys had distributed 19 per cent of its equity to employees through stock options, worth about $9 billion at that time’s prices.79

The money was very unevenly spread. It went to those who had joined early, at the leading firm, and stayed long enough to vest. A graduate who joined a smaller firm, or who joined Infosys in 2001, was in a different position.

The bust

The bust looked different depending on where one stood. For investors in Indian internet shares it was a collapse. Satyam Infoway’s annual report for the year to March 2002 said that since the offering its ADSs had traded between ‘a high of $113 per ADS’ and a low of $0.87.80 The price table in the same report shows a low of $0.72 in the quarter to September 2001.81 On 5 November 2001 a securities class action was filed in New York on behalf of everyone who had bought its ADSs between 20 October 1999 and 6 December 2000. It named the company, some of its executives and some of the underwriters of its offering, and alleged that the underwriters had failed to disclose commissions and arrangements with investors to whom they allocated shares.82 In October 2001 Satyam Computer Services announced that it intended to sell its shares in the company.83 The parent’s own later history belongs to Chapter 16.

For the largest services firm the bust was a slower line, not a fall. From the year to March 1998 to the year to March 2002, Infosys’s revenue grew at a compound 68.1 per cent a year and its IT staff rose from about 2,200 to about 9,400.84 But its outlook for the following year was cautious. ‘The economic slowdown in the U.S. has impacted the growth prospects of companies that are in the insurance, banking and financial services and telecom segments,’ it said, and companies ‘may cut their IT spending or postpone decisions regarding new IT expenditures’.85 The industry’s exports kept rising. The Reserve Bank of India recorded software services receipts of $6.3 billion in the year to March 2001 and $7.6 billion in the year to March 2002.86

For new graduates, the bust arrived on campus. In January 2002 The Hindu Business Line reported that Anna University had placed ‘only around 320 students with various companies, down from 1,200 around the same time last year’. The year before had been a record: 65 per cent of the 70 companies that visited the university had been IT firms. This time about 35 companies came. Wipro and Satyam, which had taken 49 and 23 students the year before, did not come at all. TCS recruited 54 students against 177, Infosys 46 against 126, and Cognizant Technology Solutions 69 against 105.87 Dr N. Baluswamy, who directed the university’s Centre for University-Industry Collaboration, which ran placements, said that some of the previous year’s graduates had seen their offers postponed indefinitely, and that the centre had negotiated with the companies. ‘A few companies have delayed the date of joining for last year students by over a year,’ he said. Some IT companies, ‘except big players such as TCS, Infosys and CTS’, had cut the pay promised on campus ‘by 50-60 per cent’.88

At the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) in Madras (Chennai since 1996), a little over 400 of the 719 students registered with the placement centre had been recruited by 51 companies, 30 of them in IT, with ten companies still to come, against about 750 placed the year before. About 30 per cent of students stayed away from campus interviews that year, the paper reported, because of ‘shocking stories of lay-offs, delayed appointments and salary cuts’ among the previous year’s recruits. Most engineering students now wanted ‘to consider only engineering companies, rather than shift to IT’, and some said they wanted stable jobs.89 Companies from outside IT were coming back. Maruti Udyog returned after about four years and the Gas Authority of India after fifteen, and NTPC was expected for the first time. At a management institute in Tiruchi, the highest offer came from Citibank, at Rs 6.5 lakh a year, about $13,600.90

The pattern matters for the rest of this book. In the placement director’s account, the largest firms did not cut the pay they had promised; other firms cut salaries, and some put off joining dates by more than a year. The colleges went on producing graduates while the firms at the end of the pipeline cut their campus intake, the two largest at Anna University by more than half. Later chapters show the same pattern returning.

Hours and outcomes

One thread runs through the filings of these years and connects them to the end of this book. Most of Infosys’s revenue in the mid-1990s had come from time-and-materials contracts, under which the client pays for the hours or months worked, and by 1999 the company said it wanted more fixed-price work.91 Y2K added a lesson of its own. It paid well because it was urgent: the company said that ‘high demand for these time-sensitive projects’ brought favourable prices and margins.92 The risk ran the other way too: the 1999 filing counted ‘the accuracy of estimates of resources and time required to complete ongoing projects, particularly projects performed under fixed-price, fixed-time frame contracts’ among the causes of uneven results.93 Under a fixed price, a project that overran cost the firm and not the client.

By 2002 the company reported progress. ‘As compare [sic] to the previous year, we have increased the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-timeframe, rather than a time-and-material basis, which reduce client risk, and if effectively structured and executed, provide opportunities for enhanced margins.’94 A firm that sells hours earns less when its engineers work faster, unless it uses the saving to win more work. A firm that sells an outcome at a fixed price keeps what it saves. Before 2002 the leading Indian firm had decided that it wanted, where it could, to sell outcomes. When tools arrive that cut the hours needed for coding and testing, the work that Rajaraman says took 70 to 80 per cent of a project’s time and was done in India, the way a contract is priced decides who keeps the gain.95

In 2002 that question was still far off. The more immediate change was in the size of the machine. In 1991 Infosys had advertised for sixty-four engineers.96 In 2002 it employed about 10,740 people and had received nearly 200,000 applications in a year.97 The firms’ demand for new engineers had fallen for a season. The supply had not. The next chapter follows the graduates who kept coming, and the companies that learned to hire them by the thousand.
