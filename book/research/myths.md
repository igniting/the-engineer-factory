# Myths and contested claims

**Chapter numbers in this file refer to outline v0** (until re-tagged in Phase 2). Mapping to outline v1 (2026-10-12): v0 1-14 = v1 1-14; v0 15 (Satyam and Aadhaar) = v1 16; v0 16 (product question) = v1 17; v0 17 (pandemic) = v1 18; v0 18 (AI) = v1 19; new v1 15 (foreign centres/GCCs) draws on sources tagged 14 and 16.

Each claim: where it is commonly stated, what the evidence shows so far, a **provisional** verdict, and source IDs. Verdicts: TRUE / MOSTLY TRUE / MIXED / MOSTLY FALSE / FALSE / UNRESOLVED. All verdicts below are provisional (Phase 1c, 2026-10-11) and must be re-tested in the relevant chapter dossier against the sources themselves (most are still at 'located', not 'seen'). A myth may only be called FALSE in the book when two independent sources support that verdict.

Each entry also notes **what would change the verdict** so the dossier work is targeted.

## From the author's brief (M01-M12)

| M01 | Texas Instruments' 1985 Bangalore centre was India's first offshore development centre. | MOSTLY FALSE as worded; TRUE in a narrower form | TI's own claim is narrower: first *multinational* software design centre in India and first to use a satellite dish to export software (S0061). Rajaraman records DoE negotiating a dedicated TI satellite link with the telecom department in 1985 (S0001, p. 36); the World Bank notes TI's private Intelsat earth station (S0241, p. 98); Heeks calls TI's 1986 operation 'the first proper offshore software facility' (S0085, p. 13). Citibank's SEEPZ unit (1985) and TCS's 1970s remote work complicate 'first' (S0240; Ch. 4). Earlier offshore work by Indian firms existed: TCS's Burroughs conversion, 1973-74 (S0039, S0038), Patni's data entry from 1972 (S0044). Infosys's first client-dedicated offshore centre came in 1993 (S0089). | Evidence that the TCS/Burroughs or Patni work was done on-site abroad, not in India; a contemporary record of an earlier MNC centre. | S0061, S0001, S0039, S0038, S0044, S0089 | 8 |
| M02 | Y2K made the Indian IT industry. | MIXED | Exports grew fast before Y2K: $128m (1990-91) to $1.76bn (1997-98), 28-63% a year (S0001 table, citing Bhatnagar). Y2K was c. 23% of Infosys revenue in 1997 (S0001 citing S0005) and roughly a fifth to a quarter of exports around FY1998-99 on inconsistent Nasscom estimates (S0109). Y2K widened client access and the global delivery model (S0094 to check); it did not start the growth. | A consistent export series showing growth stalled without Y2K; firm-level revenue mix from 20-F filings (S0161). | S0001, S0109, S0089, S0094 | 11 |
| M03 | IBM was thrown out of India in 1978. | MIXED (revised 2026-10-12, Ch. 5 dossier) | FERA (1973) required dilution to 40%; IBM offered 60% Indian equity in cards and data centres but not in its core marketing and maintenance business, and chose to close in June 1978 rather than comply (S0001, S0056); ICL complied and stayed as ICIM (S0001). But the minister, Fernandes, by his own 2006 account told IBM 'You get out' (S0056), and Banerjee's 2026 history speaks of 'expulsion' (S0221). Nadar says 'IBM wasn't evicted' and blames a source-code rule (S0081; uncorroborated). 'Expelled' and 'chose to leave' describe the same event from the two sides. | Grieco's archival account (S0026); Lok Sabha record; PAC report. | S0001, S0055, S0056, S0081, S0221 | 5 |
| M04 | Rajiv Gandhi started India's IT revolution. | MIXED | The 1984 minicomputer liberalisation and 1986 software-export policy (duty-free imports, software as 'industry') came under him and mattered (S0001). But Rajaraman marks break points at 1970, 1978, 1991 and 1998, and IBM's exit (1978) had already opened space for private firms (S0001). His government's 1984 plan expected $258m of software exports by 1990; actual 1990/91 exports were about half that (S0241, p. 96; S0085). Murthy credits the 1991 reforms (S0034); TCS and Patni exports began in the 1970s (S0038, S0044). | Export or employment series showing a sharp break at 1984-86 versus other dates. | S0001, S0034, S0038, S0044 | 8 |
| M05 | Cray refused India a supercomputer, so India built PARAM. | MIXED | The US blocked the X-MP/24 sale in 1987 and a Cray Y-MP contract for IISc was not fulfilled (S0079, S0001); but a Cray X-MP/14 was imported in October 1988 for weather forecasting (S0078). C-DAC was set up in 1988 after a 1986 committee on high-performance computing (chaired by Rajaraman) and parallel-computer efforts already under way 1985-92 (S0001). The popular story traces largely to one 2017 feature (S0080). | C-DAC founding documents naming the Cray denial as the reason. | S0001, S0078, S0079, S0080 | 8 |
| M06 | TCS was India's first software company. | UNRESOLVED (definitional) | TCS began 1967-68 as a Tata group data-processing bureau (Tata Computer Centre 1967 per S0228; TCS 1968 per S0039), not as an exporter; Patni's Data Conversion exported data-entry work from 1972 (S0044); ISI and government computing centres sold computing services earlier (S0007). 'First software company' needs a definition (bureau services, software exports, product sales). | Evidence of any earlier commercial software firm in India. | S0039, S0044, S0007, S0228, S0229 | 4 |
| M07 | Only 5% (or 25%) of Indian engineers are employable. | MOSTLY FALSE as a statement about the labour market | Figures trace to Aspiring Minds, an assessment vendor: c. 18% passing its test thresholds for software-services roles, 3-4% for product roles, c. 40% for BPO-type roles (S0153; 2014 vs 2016 figures conflated in press). This measures test performance against the vendor's role benchmarks, not employability in the labour-market sense. PLFS graduate unemployment was 13.0% in 2023-24 (S0154). | Independent employer-side or labour-force evidence of skills gaps at similar magnitudes. | S0153, S0154 | 12, 18 |
| M08 | India produces 1.5 million engineers a year. | MIXED to MOSTLY FALSE | The 1.5 million figure has no traceable primary source; it appears to count intake or enrolment, or to include diplomas. The Duke study counted c. 215,000 Indian engineering/CS/IT graduates in 2004, about half sub-baccalaureate, against press claims of 350,000 (S0213). AICTE approved B.Tech intake was 23.7 lakh in 2019-20 but enrolment only 13.8 lakh (S0151), and graduates are fewer still. | AICTE/AISHE graduate (outturn) counts by year, separating degree from diploma. | S0213, S0151, S0152 | 12 |
| M09 | Indian IT created India's middle class. | MOSTLY FALSE as worded | The sector's direct employment is small relative to India's workforce (c. 5.95 million in FY26 per Nasscom, S0140; earlier estimates put IT-ITES at a very small share of total employment, S0215), and India's middle-class growth has many sources. IT did create a visible new urban professional class and shaped aspirations (S0095, S0117). | Household-survey evidence on middle-class composition by sector. | S0140, S0215, S0095, S0117 | 12, 16 |
| M10 | Indian IT is mostly low-value maintenance work. | UNRESOLVED (time-dependent) | 1990s exports were majority on-site services (57% onsite, FY2000, S0086); Infosys's Nortel work moved from maintenance to product development (S0089); ADM (application development and maintenance) dominated revenue mixes; GCC and ER&D work grew later (S0141, S0140). Needs a definition of 'value' (price per hour, work type, IP ownership). | Revenue-by-service-line series from 20-F filings and Nasscom segment data over time. | S0086, S0089, S0141, S0140 | 11, 14, 16 |
| M11 | H-1B visas mostly go to Indian outsourcing firms. | MIXED (conflates nationality and employer) | India-born share of H-1B approvals: 48.5% (Oct 1999-Feb 2000, S0104); 72% (FY2023, S0105). Employer shares need the USCIS employer data hub by year (S0159); US tech firms became large sponsors. | Year-by-year top-employer tables. | S0104, S0105, S0159 | 13, 18 |
| M12 | A specific share of Silicon Valley startups were founded by Indians. | MIXED (depends on measure) | Saxenian 1999: Indian CEOs ran c. 7% of Silicon Valley tech firms founded 1980-98 (S0097). Wadhwa et al. 2007: an Indian founder in 15.5% of Silicon Valley startups 1995-2005 (13.4% also cited) (S0098). Measures differ (CEO vs any founder; period; universe; surname-based identification). | Original reports' method sections. | S0097, S0098 | 13 |

| M13 | TIFRAC was India's first computer. | MIXED | First electronic digital computer *in* India: imported HEC-2M at ISI (working August 1955 per S0001; arrival dated 'early 1956' by S0007); ISI built an analogue machine c. 1953 (S0007, S0010). TIFRAC (completed 1959, commissioned 1960, named 1962) was 'the first fullscale electronic digital computer designed, built, and operated in India' in Narasimhan's words (S0013, p. 447); a TIFR pilot machine ran in 1956 (S0001). | - | S0001, S0007, S0010, S0013 | 1 |
| M14 | The IITs were modelled on MIT. | MIXED | The Sarker Committee (1945-46), including two MIT doctorates, proposed institutes training students 'to the standard of MIT' (S0003, p. 787), so MIT was the stated benchmark. In practice the first five IITs had different patrons and curricula: USSR (Bombay), West Germany (Madras, workshop-heavy), US consortium anchored by MIT (Kanpur), UK (Delhi) (S0003, pp. 787, 796-797; S0031). Kanpur (1960s) and Bombay (1972 curriculum reform) adopted American models (S0003, p. 798). | S0004's full argument. | S0003, S0031, S0004 | 2 |
| M15 | Infosys was founded with Rs 10,000 borrowed from Sudha Murty. | MOSTLY TRUE, with wording caveats | Sudha Murty says she gave Rs 10,000 of her Rs 10,250 savings and expected to write it off (S0210), i.e. seed money rather than a loan. Secondary sources give the company's starting capital as Rs 10,000 (to confirm in filings). Infosys had seven founders (S0089); whether others contributed capital is not established. | Infosys incorporation documents or F-1 history on paid-up capital. | S0210, S0089 | 6 |
| M16 | Indian IT grew because of low wages alone. | MOSTLY FALSE | Wage arbitrage was central, but sources point to other factors: an English-educated engineering supply (S0089 risk factors; S0001), state policy on imports and tax (S0001, S0088), process capability such as CMM driven by tight labour markets (S0094, S0001), the diaspora (S0099, S0100), and US demand. Infosys says it refused GE's price cuts in 1995 and charged 20-25% more than Indian rivals (S0034). | Econometric decomposition (Arora et al., S0093). | S0001, S0034, S0088, S0089, S0094, S0099, S0093 | 9, 11 |
| M17 | Bangalore was chosen for its climate. | MOSTLY FALSE as the main reason (revised 2026-10-12, Ch. 10) | Every account puts labour first: the defence public sector and engineering colleges (S0034, pp. 10-11; S0085, p. 13; S0255, p. 14). Murthy's reasons for Infosys's move were Maharashtra's tenancy law and Karnataka's engineers, with no mention of climate (S0034, p. 10). Heeks lists 'a good climate and social life' as part of a perceived quality of life, one of four factors, alongside labour, infrastructure and proximity to previous employers (S0085, p. 13); C.S. Murali also cites the 'salubrious climate' (S0249). Climate was an amenity, not the cause. | TI's own site-selection account. | S0034, S0085, S0249, S0255 | 10 |
| M18 | Women make up about a third of the Indian IT workforce. | MOSTLY TRUE for large firms; seniority gap untested | TCS 35.2% (Q4 FY25, S0160); Infosys 39% (Q2 FY25, S0161); Nasscom c. 37% (FY09) and 45% of fresher intake (S0107); a PLFS-based summary gives 34% with sharp fall at senior levels (S0211, unverified). | Seniority breakdowns; PLFS tables. | S0160, S0161, S0107, S0211 | 12 |
| M19 | Indian IT workers cannot unionise. | FALSE | KITU certified under the Trade Unions Act 1926 in November 2017 (S0132); NITES active in complaints to the labour ministry (S0133). Unions are weak: Karnataka exempted IT firms from the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act (S0132). | - | S0132, S0133 | 17 |
| M20 | AI caused the 2025 TCS layoffs. | UNRESOLVED | TCS's CEO denied AI productivity was the cause, citing skill mismatch and failed redeployment (S0135, a party's claim). Analysts describe AI-driven price deflation across the sector (S0142). TCS net headcount fell 23,460 in FY26 (S0139). | Utilisation, pricing and role-mix data; independent reporting. | S0135, S0139, S0142 | 18 |
| M21 | The 1984 Computer Policy cut import duties and 'opened' computing. | MOSTLY TRUE (revised 2026-10-12, Ch. 8 dossier) | The November 1984 policy removed capacity limits, allowed 32-bit machines and imported boards, cut duty on computers for software export (previously 'over 100%') and recognised onsite work as 'software export' (S0001, pp. 31-32). Duty-free import against a 250% export obligation came in December 1986 (S0001, p. 32; S0241, p. 106). 'Opened' needs qualifying: imports 'still took time' (S0240), and mainframes stayed reserved for ECIL. | Gazette text of the 1984 policy. | S0001, S0241, S0240 | 8 |
| M22 | The Software Technology Parks scheme began in 1991 as part of liberalisation. | MOSTLY FALSE (revised 2026-10-12, Ch. 9) | STPs opened at Pune (Aug 1990), Bangalore (Oct 1990) and Bhubaneswar (Dec 1990) (S0241, p. 97); STPI was registered on 5 June 1991, seven weeks before the 24 July budget (S0091). The budget's software measure was the s. 80HHC tax concession (S0088, p. 20). STP satellite links became operational around 1992 (S0254), so the scheme's effect fell in the reform years, but its origin predates them. | Founding notification of the STP scheme (1990-91). | S0241, S0091, S0088, S0254 | 8, 9 |
