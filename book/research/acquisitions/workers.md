# Acquisitions: workers (rank-and-file voices)

Phase 8 source acquisition, GROUP = workers. Access date for every item: 2026-10-15. All quotations below were copied from the downloaded text (pdftotext or HTML extraction), not from memory or summaries. Quotations are under 50 words. Page numbers are printed page numbers unless marked otherwise. Interviewees in the scholarly items are anonymous or pseudonymous as published; none is a composite created here.

Stand-in logic: the books on the wanted list (Upadhya 2016 [S0095]; Upadhya and Vasavi 2008 [S0096]; Taylor and Bain 2005; Noronha and D'Cruz 2009; Mirchandani 2012; Patel 2010; Xiang 2007 [S0057]) could not be read. Items 1-6 are open texts by the same authors on the same fieldwork; items 7-9 are reviews (reviewer's words only); items 10-11 are contemporary press with named workers.

## 1. Upadhya and Vasavi, NIAS-IDPAD final report (2006): the fieldwork behind *Reengineering India* and *In an Outpost*

- **Citation:** Upadhya, Carol, and A.R. Vasavi. *Work, Culture, and Sociality in the Indian IT Industry: A Sociological Study.* Final Report submitted to the Indo-Dutch Programme for Alternatives in Development. Bangalore: School of Social Sciences, National Institute of Advanced Studies, August 2006. 195 PDF pages (printed pages i-xx, 1-185).
- **URL:** http://eprints.nias.res.in/107/2/idpadfinalreport.pdf (record: http://eprints.nias.res.in/107/)
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship (project report; authors' institutional repository). Read in full text; quotations checked against the PDF.
- **Page note:** printed page = PDF page minus 10 (page number sits at the top of each page).
- **Method (p. 4-5):** fieldwork November 2003 to March 2006; "structured interviews with a sample of employees from a cross-section of companies, totalling 132 IT professionals and 34 BPO workers" (p. 4); informal interviews "with approximately 160 software engineers and BPO employees and their families" (p. 4); 80 interviews with Indian IT professionals in Europe (p. 5).

**Body shopping and on-site work (open question: body shopping)**
- p. 10, n. 13: STPIs let firms offer offshore services "rather than working through 'bodyshopping', which had been the primary form of outsourcing in the 1980s (Parthasarathy 2005:208)."
- p. 18: "The Indian software services industry started out with 'bodyshopping' – a system in which companies send teams of software engineers to the customer site". Y2K: the 'Y2K crisis' of 1998-99 "saw a revival of bodyshopping due to the heavy demand for programmers in the U.S."
- p. 19 (software exports 1990-91, secondary figure): "in 1990-91, 90 per cent of Indian software and service export revenues came from onsite work and only 5 per cent from offshore" (citing Basant and Rani 2004:5318).
- p. 21: the bench described: the Indian employer "pays a basic monthly maintenance allowance when he or she is between assignments ('on the bench'). The engineer is paid at the contracted rate only when s/he is actually working."
- p. 23: "We were told that onsite workers are usually able to save Rs 100,000 to 200,000 by working in the U.S. for three months."

**Who the engineers are (survey of 132)**
- p. 37: "80 per cent of respondents' fathers were graduates or above"; "84 per cent of the fathers in our sample were in occupations that can be identified as solidly middle class".
- p. 38: "71 per cent belong to the 'savarna' upper castes (about half were Brahmins)." With dominant agricultural castes, "fully 86 per cent of respondents come from upper castes and/or economically better-off communities."
- p. 36 (HR manager on hiring from lower-tier colleges): they require "guys who can just sit and code and not ask questions".

**Engineers in their own words (anonymous informants)**
- p. 56: "Everyone is focused on learning new skills because there is a high level of insecurity – everyone knows that at any time, the company might lose a project, or be taken over, and you could be let go any time."
- p. 56-57 (woman engineer): "Nobody really plans their careers. ... More than 50 per cent took up IT jobs simply because it's a good way to make money."
- p. 69 (woman tech lead, medium Indian company): "It is very hierarchical, it is not open. They say it is open but it is not. It is a very conservative place, more Indian".
- p. 69: "We are still supposed to call our bosses as sir or madam, although we were told when we joined that we should not address them as such…."
- p. 69 (engineer, large American ODC): "Managers who give orders feel superior to the techies who receive them, and it subtly comes out in their behaviour."
- p. 71: manager "always around, seeing who is there and who is not … You can't just leave the office when there is no work".
- p. 72: "Project managers get paid well in services companies because they are key people ... We need slave drivers to do that kind of work".
- p. 85: "One respondent referred to this as a system of 'time slavery'." Survey: "61 per cent" work nine to ten hours a day; average maximum hours "12.3" (MNCs) vs "13.8" (large Indian companies).
- p. 86: "They'll give out work at 5:30 in the evening and expect you to have it done by the next morning, which isn't fair".
- p. 87: "What's the point of working till late if you aren't seen working?" Director of a small firm on young engineers ('blue collar IT workers'): "working hard means being seen in the office till 2 a.m."

**BPO and call-centre workers (Ch. 14)**
- p. 129: HR officer would not hire a candidate with a "very good family background with high percentage of marks", as they would be "looking at call centres as a stopgap arrangement". One respondent falsified his c.v. to hide a postgraduate degree.
- p. 130: pre-process training "twenty hours each of accent, culture, grammar and customer service"; UK-process workers in one company had to understand "fourteen different British dialects".
- p. 133 (process trainer, third-party call centre): "most people who come to call centres are not looking for permanent employment, they see it as an in-between, for instance while studying further."
- p. 136-137 (young employee): "Call centres can only be used as stepping stones to learning. ... So, take this and move on."
- p. 151: "They enter like Gandhis and come out like Hitlers!" (informant on how call-centre work changes young women's dress and behaviour, as told to the researchers).
- p. 152 (female worker): "Since call centre employees live a life completely different from those in other professions, their social interaction gets confined to people in similar profession".
- p. 153: "When a girl gets a drop at some odd hour in the night, neighbours switch on the lights and gaze at her with shock and make comments".
- p. 153: "Initially I felt so ashamed that I worked in a call centre that I used to tell people that I'm working for an internet service provider without mentioning any details."

## 2. Upadhya, 'Employment, Exclusion and "Merit" in the Indian IT Industry' (EPW 2007)

- **Citation:** Upadhya, Carol. 'Employment, Exclusion and "Merit" in the Indian IT Industry.' *Economic and Political Weekly* 42, no. 20 (19 May 2007): 1863-1868.
- **URL:** http://eprints.nias.res.in/92/2/EPW_employment_exclusion.pdf (record http://eprints.nias.res.in/92/)
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship (journal article, author repository). Read in full.
- p. 1864: "Brahmins constituted 48 per cent of our sample." Upper castes "71 per cent"; with dominant agricultural castes "86 per cent".
- p. 1864: "Only one respondent said that he belonged to a scheduled community."
- p. 1864: "36 per cent of the sample were born in one of the five metros (including Bangalore), 29 per cent were from tier two towns and cities such as Mysore and Pune, and only 5 per cent came from rural areas." Tier-three towns: "31 per cent".
- p. 1864: contrasts this with MindTree's Subroto Bagchi, who cited a company survey finding "33 per cent of new recruits were from 'rural, agricultural backgrounds'".
- p. 1865: large Indian services firms prefer tier-three campuses "because the best students are not suitable for the kind of routinised work that is on offer". HR manager: "guys who can just sit and code and not ask questions".
- Use: published, peer-citable version of the IDPAD survey figures in item 1.

## 4. Taylor, D'Cruz, Noronha and Scholarios, union formation in Indian call centres (NTWE 2009; accepted manuscript)

- **Citation:** Taylor, Phil, Premilla D'Cruz, Ernesto Noronha and Dora Scholarios. 'Indian Call Centres and Business Process Outsourcing: A Study in Union Formation.' *New Technology, Work and Employment* 24, no. 1 (2009): 19-42. Accepted author manuscript.
- **URL:** https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/15272/2/Taylor_etal_NTWE2009_indian_call_centres_business_process_outsourcing.pdf (record https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/15272/)
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship (author manuscript). Read in full. **Page numbers are manuscript pages, not journal pages.**
- Method (ms p. 12): survey of UNITES members April-July 2007, "879 completed questionnaires from Bangalore (30%), Chennai (17%), Hyderabad (16%), Kochin (16%), Mumbai (14%) and New Delhi (8%)"; 45 taped interviews (ms p. 13).
- ms p. 14 (agent, Bangalore captive): "Every second company is willing to pay you very well irrespective of your experience. If things don't work out well in one company they don't care, they can go elsewhere."
- ms p. 17 (voice agent, Chennai captive): "At a stretch, a technician is supposed to take at least 50-60 calls ... People are stopped from going for their breaks ... Once they find that the person has shouted at the customer, he is thrown out of the job."
- ms p. 18 (Chennai domestic): "No time to freshen up. ... So no water, three hours continuous talking, without taking a glass of water. ... The leader will yell, come to each person and scream."
- ms p. 16: UNITES' first recruits followed its campaign after "the murder in December 2005 of a women working nightshift for Hewlett Packard in Bangalore by a man purporting to be her driver".
- ms p. 21-22 (UNITES member, third-party company, Noida, on unpaid salaries): training manager said "'No point in talking to me, you won't be getting your salaries so you can all go back'"; after a group visit to HR, "'Tomorrow you will be getting your cheques'. And the next day they did."

## 5. Taylor, D'Cruz, Noronha and Scholarios, Indian domestic call centres (2010 chapter; manuscript)

- **Citation:** Taylor, Phil, Premilla D'Cruz, Ernesto Noronha and Dora Scholarios. 'Domestic Labour: The Experience of Work in India's Other Call Centre Industry.' In *Work and Life in the Global Economy*, ed. Debra Howcroft and Helen Richardson, ch. 6. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2010 (Crossref lists the volume as DOI 10.1057/9780230277977). Pre-publication Word manuscript.
- **URL:** https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/15323/1/Taylor_et_al_for_Howcroft_and_Richardson.doc (record https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/15323/)
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship (manuscript). Read in full. **No page numbers**; cite by section ('Findings').
- Method: "618 usable questionnaires were completed between April and June 2008" by domestic-centre employees in six cities; 25 interviews.
- Pay: "Avendus (2008, 16) calculates annual average domestic starting salaries at $2,000, compared with $4,500 for international agents."
- Chennai telecoms agent: if they "do not meet their targets they [managers] cut our salary", which "could mean the difference between 4,500 and 6,000 rupees a month given incentives" (authors' paraphrase with quoted fragment).
- Chennai team leader: "I am a team leader and I cannot show that I am under pressure but I have to give this pressure to my agents".
- Woman agent, small Chennai outsourcer: "Now we can take a 2-minute break. There used to be nothing. We used to beg our team leaders".
- New Delhi call handler: "If you move from Gurgaon to Connaught Place, it will take about 90 minutes, so two ways becomes 180 minutes. So practically it becomes 12 hours a day."
- The authors report BelAir in Bangalore "summarily dismissed its 150-strong workforce in the summer of 2006 without compensation".
- Note: the Chennai 'No time to freshen up' testimony appears here in a longer version than in item 4; the wording differs slightly between the two manuscripts. Quote one version and cite it, not a blend.

## 6. D'Cruz and Noronha, 'Hope to Despair' (IJIR 2013)

- **Citation:** D'Cruz, Premilla, and Ernesto Noronha. 'Hope to Despair: The Experience of Organizing Indian Call Centre Employees.' *Indian Journal of Industrial Relations* 48, no. 3 (January 2013): 471-486.
- **URL:** https://www.publishingindia.com/storage/PDFBrochures/1602.pdf
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship (publisher-hosted PDF). Read in full. Draws on "fieldwork over a period of 8 years and in-depth interviews with union officials and more than 200 employees" (p. 471). Mostly the authors' synthesis; few verbatim worker quotes.
- p. 474: agents "could not log out of their systems or leave their seats even to go to the restroom (if it was an emergency, they had to seek permission from the team leader to do so)."
- p. 474: some employers went "to the extent of blocking bank salary accounts of those absenting themselves and refusing to give relieving letters to those who quit."
- p. 476: "Some call center agents stated that they had neither seen nor had a meal with their family members for several weeks."
- p. 478: agents "abhorred nomenclatures such as 'cyber coolies' and 'slaves on Roman ships'" (quoting Ramesh 2004).
- p. 479: agents "reprimanded for using prefixes such as 'sir' or 'madam'"; agents saw "sloganeering, picketing and striking" as "unbecoming of professional demeanor".
- p. 480-481: UNITES Professionals formed September 2005, backed by UNI; registered "only in the state of Karnataka"; members feared "having one's name blacklisted on NASSCOM's database".

## 6a. Noronha and D'Cruz, telemarketing agents (TQR 2007)

- **Citation:** Noronha, Ernesto, and Premilla D'Cruz. 'Reconciling Dichotomous Demands: Telemarketing Agents in Bangalore and Mumbai, India.' *The Qualitative Report* 12, no. 2 (June 2007): 255-280.
- **URL:** https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ800182.pdf (ERIC full text; original http://www.nova.edu/ssss/QR/QR12-2/noronha.pdf)
- **Kind:** open-access scholarship. Read in full. 18 telemarketing agents, unstructured interviews (p. 255).
- p. 261: "We have to make 6-7 sales per shift." Another: "The shifts affect our health – no sleep, appetite goes, loss of weight, fatigue. But even if you are not well, you cannot go home".
- p. 263: "So repeating 10 lines a day, I will get paid 10000-12000 rupees - amazing ... even a guy who works from morning to evening, say in a garment or textile shop, he wouldn't have been paid even more than 3000 rupees".
- p. 264: "everyone knows that for one non-performer, there are five guys outside waiting to replace him."
- p. 267: on using a pseudonym: "if I say that I am _____ (actual name) then they won't understand it and it will take time."
- p. 268: "you cannot react back, you just have to keep quiet and listen. Sometimes when people are a bit fed up, what they do is they put the person (customer) on mute and curse them back."
- p. 270: "I got into telemarketing because typical engineering jobs did not pay."

## 10. Rozen, 'Invasion of the bodyshoppers' (Dallas Observer, 1998): named H-1B workers and a body shop

- **Citation:** Rozen, Miriam. 'Invasion of the bodyshoppers.' *Dallas Observer*, 12 November 1998 (web timestamp 1998-11-11T22:30 UTC).
- **URL:** https://www.dallasobserver.com/news/invasion-of-the-bodyshoppers-6401366/
- **Kind:** primary-adjacent contemporary press with named people (first-person quotes). Read in full. Paragraph numbers not stable on the web page; cite by name.
- Body shop owner Mali Subbiah (Plano; from Madras) takes "a big chunk–sometimes as much as 40 percent–out of the workers' paychecks every week."
- H-1B cap: the INS "had implemented a moratorium on H-1B immigrants in May 1998" when "the congressionally set annual quota of 65,000 H-1Bs had already been reached"; issuing resumed 1 October; cap later raised "from 65,000 to 115,000"; new "$500 fee for each worker".
- Subbiah: "I am getting the cream of the crop from India and dumping them on America"; "I know there are workers who will tell you Mali runs a slave shop". He refused to make his contract workers available.
- Reedhar Gunda (electrical engineer from Bombay): "he found out his bodyshopper was taking $22 an hour out of his paycheck, leaving him with only $28." Leaving required a new H-1B petition taking "several months".
- Reddy Bandi (27, Hyderabad, son of a rice farmer, first in family at college; student visa then H-1B): "I don't like to apply to companies who get people from the outside ... I know what their tactics are." Also: "I try to live better than the locals."
- Subbiah's terms: salary "$45,000 to $60,000"; rival recruiters say he could charge "$90,000 to $200,000 a year"; INS data: Mastech sponsored "672 temporary workers" in six months to March 1998.
- A recruiter's memo: "They speak good English and know to smile" (Applisoft, Saratoga, CA).
- Bandi's friends' accounts of losing visa papers to body shoppers are second-hand; mark them as such.

## 11. Pink, 'The New Face of the Silicon Age' (Wired, February 2004): named Indian programmers

- **Citation:** Pink, Daniel H. 'The New Face of the Silicon Age: How India became the capital of the computing revolution.' *Wired* 12.02, February 2004 (online 1 February 2004).
- **URL:** https://www.wired.com/2004/02/india/
- **Kind:** contemporary press with named workers. Read in full (web text loses drop-cap letters at paragraph starts; wording below checked).
- Aparna Jairam, 33, project manager at Hexaware Technologies, Navi Mumbai; Pune engineering graduate of 1992; earlier two years at Patni in the late 1990s. "Jairam's annual salary is about $11,000".
- Jairam (quoting the Gita, on American programmers' fears): "Do what you're supposed to do. And don't worry about the fruits. They'll come on their own."
- Kavita Samudra, senior software engineer: "Back in the US, it's all about cheap, cheap, cheap. It's not only about India being cheap. It's quality services".
- Lalit Suryawanshi: "Don't you think we're helping the US economy by doing the work here?" (opening word restored from the text's lost drop-cap).
- Aditya Deshmukh, project manager at Patni, "worked in Baltimore and New Jersey for three years but has no desire to return to the States".
- Hexaware facts reported: average age "27"; canteen meal "22 rupees, about 50 cents".
