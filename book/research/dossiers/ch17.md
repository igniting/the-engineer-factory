**Chapter question.** Why did the country that supplied the world's software labour build so few software products of its own, and what changed when it did?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The early products.** India's products appeared early but stayed small beside services. Tally began in 1986 as an accounting package for Indian small businesses and grew through a network of partners (S0178, company account). FLEXCUBE, launched in 1997 by a unit Citicorp had spun off with $400,000, became the world's best-selling core-banking system by a British league table. Oracle bought Citi's stake in 2005 for about $592 million (S0176). Infosys built Finacle inside a services firm (S0260, S0271).

**Founders' explanations.** Rajesh Hukku blamed path dependence: after F.C. Kohli's TCS model succeeded, 'hundreds of companies that came after copied him' (S0176). In 2013 about 30 product firms broke away to form iSPIRT. Tally's Bharat Goenka said product companies lacked 'weightage' in NASSCOM, whose policies favoured services (S0188). NASSCOM's own roadmap that year imagined software products at about $10 billion of a $300 billion industry by 2020 (S0188).

**The SaaS generation.** Freshworks started in a 700-square-foot Chennai warehouse in 2010 and sold online to the world from 'day one'. It listed on Nasdaq at $36 in 2021 (S0173, S0284). By 2024 about 3,700 of its employees, 83%, were in India, but the company was headquartered in San Mateo (S0285).

**Boom, bust and platforms.** Funding fell from 2021's peak to $10.8 billion in 2023 (Tracxn; S0147†). Byju's ended in insolvency proceedings (S0185, press). UPI, a state platform, processed 16.73 billion transactions in December 2024 (S0286†).

**Myth M29.** The 'services mindset' explanation is the participants' own. It has not been tested against capital, market and talent explanations, and stays unresolved.

**Why it matters for AI.** A product firm keeps productivity gains; a services firm sells hours.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The question

By the 2010s India was the world's largest exporter of software services, and almost every chapter of this book has been about selling engineers' time. The question that runs through public debate in India is why the same industry produced so few software products of its own. A product here means software that a firm owns and sells many times, as opposed to software written once for a client who pays by the hour or by the project. The question matters for this book beyond national pride. A product company keeps the gains when its engineers become more productive. A services company must negotiate who keeps them. That difference decides how AI tools affect the two kinds of firm (Chapters 9 and 11, and Part V).

### 3.2 Tally: the domestic product

The longest-lived Indian software product grew from the home market. Tally's own history page records that in 1986 'Peutronics Financial Accountant' was founded by Bharat Goenka, 'inspired by his father, Shyam Sundar Goenka's vision to simplify business operations'. In 1988 it 'Launched Tally 3.0, pioneering code-less accounting'. In 1991 'Tally Solutions Private Limited was officially established' (S0178). Later versions tracked Indian tax law: Tally 7.2 in 2005 'introduced VAT compliance, aiding businesses with tax regulations' (S0178). The company now says it has 'helped millions across 100+ countries ... supported by 28,000+ partners' (S0178). These are company claims, and the drafting must say so.

Two dates conflict. The register records incorporation on 8 November 1991 and a rename to Tally Solutions in 1999. The company page says Tally Solutions Private Limited was established in 1991 (new dispute vv). The likely reconciliation is that the 1991 company took the Tally name later. Check the Registrar of Companies record.

Tally's model was the opposite of the export firms'. It sold a packaged product to Indian small businesses through local partners, and its updates followed Indian tax changes rather than foreign clients' needs. Its survival for four decades is evidence that a domestic software market existed even when the export industry was growing much faster. Its modest size relative to the services firms is evidence of how small that market was.

### 3.3 FLEXCUBE: the product from a captive

The most successful early Indian product for global markets came from inside a foreign bank. Knowledge at Wharton's 2007 interview with its founder gives his account (S0176). 'In 1992, Rajesh Hukku, then head of Citicorp Overseas Software, spun off Citicorp Information Technology Industries Ltd. (CITIL), now called i-flex solutions, from the giant financial institution. Citi had decided that software wasn't its business, but invested $400,000 in the venture'. 'Five years later, i-flex launched FLEXCUBE'. By 2007 it had been 'sold to more than 300 banks in 120 countries'. The company listed on Indian exchanges in 2002, and in 2005 'Oracle bought out Citicorp's 42% interest for $592 million' (S0176). The register and chronology had 41% and $593 million (new dispute ww). The Oracle and i-flex filings of 2005 should settle it.

Hukku had studied electrical and electronics engineering at BITS Pilani and worked for TCS before joining Citicorp's in-house software unit (S0176). His answer to the question of why there were so few Indian products is the most direct participant's explanation in the sources. 'In my opinion, almost all software companies followed the model of [Faqir Chand] Kohli, who pioneered the concept of the Indian software exports industry with TCS in the early 1970's, of exporting the Indian brain, or doing software services projects offshore. He, like every pioneer, had to work very hard ... The not-so-great news was that hundreds of companies that came after copied him. Basically it is a human tendency to take the route which is already a proven success' (S0176). Asked whether i-flex was 'Made in Spite of India', he replied: 'It's not in spite of India, but it's in spite of whatever was successful when we came into existence' (S0176).

How a product firm survived its first years is the most useful part of the interview for the drafting, because it shows services paying for the product. 'on day one we were selling professional services, we were doing consulting, and we were also trying to survive by spending very little money. We decided early on that we were not going to go to the U.K. or the U.S. because we couldn't even afford the airfare. We said, 'Let's go to Africa. That's all we can handle right now.'' The company took over Citicorp's legacy MicroBanker product, enhanced it and sold it in Africa. 'In 1995, MicroBanker was number 1 in the league tables, because we sold it to 117 banks, mostly in Africa. The money we were making on the services, consulting, MicroBanker, etc., was used to fund the creation of our dream product - FLEXCUBE' (S0176). Citi's support came with a condition: 'Please don't come back asking for business from Citibank' (S0176).

Hukku also made two choices that ran against the industry. On markets: 'in the services outsourcing business, America was the main destination ... We said that's a very big limitation for the whole industry to be dependent on one country.' On Y2K: 'We're perhaps the only IT company from India who made $0 from Y2K. ... We made a conscious decision to stick to the value-addition paradigm' (S0176). And on staff: 'Right from the beginning, we said 10% to 15% of the staff have to be bankers, because how else will I learn the domain?' (S0176). Set against Chapter 11, where Y2K work was a fifth of Infosys's revenue at its peak, this is a deliberate refusal of the easiest money of the decade. It is a founder's retrospective claim and should be presented as such.

He was also clear that i-flex was a hybrid: 'We are a product company. We're also a services company. We do technology deployment and we also do high-end consulting and domain specialist business process outsourcing' (S0176). He described Citi's terms as ideal: 'you get the money and you don't get controlled' (S0176). He cited an international league table placing FLEXCUBE 16th in its launch year, ninth the next year, second in 1999 and first 'since 2002' (S0176; the ranking is by International Banking Systems, as he reported it). And he made a prediction worth testing in the drafting: 'I can guarantee you that five years from now, there will be many famous product companies growing out of India' (S0176).

The FLEXCUBE story fits the pattern of Chapter 15. As with Cognizant, a foreign company's Indian captive became an independent firm. The difference was that this one sold a product. Its exit was to a foreign buyer.

### 3.4 Finacle: a product inside a services firm

Infosys built a banking product inside the largest services company. Its 2002 annual report mentions a 'software application, known as FINACLE(TM)' (S0260). By fiscal 2009 Finacle was described as 'our primary suite of software products', in a market that was 'competitive'. The company said it provided 'a core banking software solution, Finacle TM, for the banking industry and provide[s] customization and implementation services around this solution' (S0271). In fiscal 2012 Finacle was listed among 'Products, business platforms and solutions to accelerate intellectual property led innovation', and the company noted that 'India and Finacle TM business units continue to be organized as before' (S0266). The Tuck School case on how Finacle grew from a failing earlier product (S0177) has not been read. Finacle's share of Infosys revenue is not yet extracted; it is needed for the data appendix.

The pattern is a product nested in a services company and sold with services around it. Like i-flex, it was never a pure product business.

### 3.5 The break with NASSCOM

By 2013 the product firms had decided that the industry association did not speak for them. Knowledge at Wharton reported in March 2013: 'A group of 30 software product companies in India recently got together to form the Indian Software Product Industry Round Table (iSpirt)' (S0188). The industry NASSCOM represented had 'grown from US$100 million in 1992 to US$108 billion in 2012' (S0188; NASSCOM figures). Bharat Goenka of Tally, a founder member, told *Business Standard*: 'At Nasscom, the product companies do not have the same weightage as the services companies. The policy interventions Nasscom is trying to make are more in favor of the services industry.' Sharad Sharma, another founder member: 'We believe that with this think-tank, we will get a louder voice in policy making' (S0188).

NASSCOM's chairman, N. Chandrasekaran of TCS, played it down: 'When you are on a growth paradigm, you cannot do everything that you want to do. I don't see any conflict' (S0188). The association's own roadmap, released at the same time by a committee chaired by Narayana Murthy, shows the scale of priorities. It aimed for 'US$300 billion by 2020', with the Internet and mobile at US$100 billion, the domestic market at US$75 billion, and 'software products could contribute around US$10 billion' (S0188). Murthy said 'we need to look at new areas of growth like Internet and mobile content and commerce, software products and the domestic market' (S0188). Products were on the list, at about one-thirtieth of the target.

What iSPIRT did next, including its advocacy of 'India Stack', is in the register (S0189) but not yet read. The drafting must not attribute specific achievements to it without that source.

### 3.6 Freshworks: SaaS from Chennai, listed in America

The generation of software-as-a-service companies that followed sold subscriptions online to small businesses abroad. Freshworks is the best documented, because it listed in the United States. Its founder, Girish Mathrubootham, wrote in the 2021 registration statement: 'We started in a 700 square foot warehouse in Chennai in 2010. Our simple idea was to create a 'fresh' helpdesk in response to a poor customer service experience I had' (S0173). He listed the doubts he had heard, including 'You can't find talent in Chennai; you can't win unless you move to Bangalore' and 'You really can't build a global SaaS company from India' (S0173). The company was 'global from "day one" with our first few customers coming from four different continents', and its early sales 'came through search-driven online acquisition, powered by robust product-led growth' (S0173). This model needed no onsite engineers, no visas and no client billing by the hour.

The S-1 shows the business at listing: revenue of $172.4 million in 2019 and $249.7 million in 2020, net losses of $31.1 million and $57.3 million, and about 52,500 paying customers by mid-2021 (S0173). 'A substantial portion of our operations and employees are located in India, including a majority of our software engineering resources' (S0173). It disclosed that Zoho had sued it in California on 17 March 2020, alleging trade-secret violations; Freshworks said it had 'meritorious defenses' (S0173). This is an allegation, and its outcome is not yet sourced. The final prospectus set the price: 'The initial public offering price is $36.00' (S0284).

Its 2024 annual report shows where the company had settled. It was 'headquartered in San Mateo, CA ... with a majority of employees based in India, where we were founded'. 'As of December 31, 2024, approximately 3,700 of our employees reside in India, representing approximately 83% of our total employee population' (S0285). On 5 November 2024 it had begun 'a 13% reduction in force' (S0285).

The 2024 report also shows the company's revenue and its bet on AI. 'Our total revenue was $720.4 million, $596.4 million and $498.0 million in the years ended December 31, 2024, 2023 and 2022' (S0285). It described 'Our generative AI offerings, Freddy AI Agent and Freddy AI Copilot'. The first 'offers always-on, autonomous, personalized resolutions to customer and employee queries'; the second 'provides always-on contextual assistance for customer support, employee support, marketing and sales use cases, designed to boost productivity' (S0285). Among its risks it listed that 'Our competitors may be able to develop new AI offerings that disrupt our customers' needs and negatively impact demand for our products' (S0285).

This is the hinge to Part V. An Indian-built product company was selling AI agents designed to resolve customer and IT service requests autonomously. That is the work Indian BPO and IT services firms had been paid to do by the hour (Chapter 14). The same filing that records 83% of its staff in India records its own exposure to AI-driven disruption. Products did not escape the AI question; they met it from the other side, as sellers of automation.

Two observations for the drafting. First, the most celebrated Indian SaaS company was, legally and financially, an American one, with Indian engineers. The value it created was listed in New York. Second, the scale gap is stark. Freshworks's 3,700 employees in India compare with hundreds of thousands at each of the largest services firms (Chapters 14 and 15). Products created wealth with far fewer engineers. That is exactly why they matter for AI, and also why they could not absorb the engineer factory's output.

### 3.7 Boom and bust

The 2020s brought a venture-capital boom in Indian start-ups, most of them consumer internet businesses rather than software products in the old sense. According to Tracxn data reported by *The Week* in January 2025: 'In 2022, Indian startups had raised $25.7 billion, a 31 per cent decline from the previous year.' 'startup funding had plunged close to 58 per cent in 2023 to $10.8 billion.' Funding 'increased around 2.11 per cent to $11.1 billion in 2024'. Six unicorns were created in 2024 against two in 2023 (S0147†). Tracxn's figures imply about $37 billion in 2021. Other vendors put the 2021 peak at about $42 billion (Inc42) or $43.5 billion (register; new dispute xx). Different vendors define 'startup' differently, and their series must never be spliced.

The emblem of the bust is Byju's, the education company. Press reports, read only in summary, record that the National Company Law Tribunal admitted an insolvency petition by the Board of Control for Cricket in India over dues of about Rs 158.9 crore in July 2024. On 23 October 2024 the Supreme Court set aside an appellate order that had closed the insolvency after a settlement (S0185, press). The primary court records have not been read, and later developments are post-cutoff live checks. For this book Byju's matters less for its legal fate than as a marker: the boom had moved Indian technology investment from software to consumer services, and many of the firms it funded employed engineers in large numbers before cutting them.

### 3.8 Platforms built by the state

The most successful Indian 'products' of the 2010s by usage were public platforms, not private firms. Chapter 16 covers Aadhaar. The Unified Payments Interface, run by the National Payments Corporation of India, is the clearest case of scale. All India Radio reported in January 2025 that UPI 'has processed nearly 16.73 billion financial transactions in a single month in December' 2024, worth 'around 23.25 lakh crore rupees', with 39% growth in count over the year (S0286†). Press reports in search results (not read) give 1.15 billion in October 2019 and 10.58 billion in August 2023. NPCI's own statistics page could not be reached. The launch month (April or August 2016) remains disputed (register).

UPI fits the book's argument in a particular way. It is software built for Indians, used by Indians, and it created markets for private builders (payment apps, merchants) without itself being a product sold for profit. That is a different answer to the product question: the state as the product company, with the private sector building on top.

### 3.9 The scale gap in numbers

The most important single comparison for this chapter is employment. The product firms in this dossier employed thousands; the services firms employed hundreds of thousands. Freshworks had about 3,700 employees in India at the end of 2024, 83% of its total, so about 4,450 worldwide (S0285; total computed). In the same years Cognizant had 241,500 employees in India out of 336,800 (S0192). TCS reported 607,979 employees in its fiscal 2025 fourth-quarter fact sheet (S0160, register). Infosys had about 150,000 employees as early as March 2012 (S0266). Freshworks's revenue of $720.4 million in 2024 (S0285) was earned with roughly one-fiftieth of Cognizant's Indian workforce.

The gap cuts both ways for the book's argument. Products earn more revenue per engineer and keep the gains of productivity, which is why the debate in India treated them as the 'higher' path. But they could never have employed the millions of graduates that the engineer factory produced (Chapter 12). The services model was not only a mindset; it was the only model in which the industry's growth translated into mass employment. The AI chapters turn on this point. A shift from selling hours to selling products or outcomes may raise profits per engineer while cutting the number of engineers needed. That is the services industry's dilemma, and Freshworks's own AI agents (section 3.6) show the product side selling the automation.

These figures come from different years and sources and are given only to show orders of magnitude. The data appendix should build a consistent comparison.

### 3.10 Testing the 'services mindset' explanation

Myth M29 holds that Indian firms failed to build products because of a services mindset. The sources in hand give the participants' version of that explanation. Hukku: firms copied the proven Kohli model (S0176). Goenka: the industry body favoured services (S0188). NASSCOM's roadmap gave products a small place (S0188). They also give counter-examples: Tally (domestic), FLEXCUBE (spun out of a captive), Finacle (inside a services firm), Zoho (self-funded; register) and Freshworks (US-listed, India-built).

What the sources do not yet do is test structural explanations against the 'mindset' story:
- **Capital.** Venture funding at scale arrived only in the 2000s and peaked in 2021 (S0147†).
- **Market.** Indian businesses' willingness to pay for software was limited until digital tax and payment systems arrived (GST, UPI). This is a hypothesis to test.
- **Talent.** The services firms offered secure, well-paid jobs to graduates (Chapter 12).
- **Exits.** The largest product exits went to foreign buyers (Oracle for i-flex) or foreign listings (Freshworks).

A 'mindset' may be the participants' name for these conditions. The verdict stays UNRESOLVED. The chapter should present the competing explanations and say which evidence would decide between them.
