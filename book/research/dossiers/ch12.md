**Chapter question.** How did India come to mass-produce software workers between 1998 and 2012, who got in, and what kind of work were they produced for?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Colleges.** The engineer factory had two halves. The first was the colleges. Engineering and technology colleges grew from 486 in 1997-98 to 1,562 in 2005-06 (S0248), after forty years of slow growth. Private universities were allowed from 2002 (S0001, p. 51). Approved undergraduate engineering intake reached about 15.5 lakh in 2012-13 and peaked at about 17 lakh in 2014-15, with a third to half of seats empty (S0267, secondary). The often-quoted output figures confuse seats with graduates. Duke counted about 112,000 bachelor's graduates in 2004, plus 103,000 diploma holders (S0213, p. 5); Aspiring Minds wrote of 600,000 graduates a year by 2013 (S0153, p. 7).

**The firms.** The second half was inside the firms. Infosys made about 1,790 job offers in FY1998 (S0089, p. 6) and about 46,300 in FY2008 (S0264-S0265). It built a Mysore campus that could train 4,500 graduates at a time in 2005 and 14,000 by 2012, on a 14-week course (S0264, S0266). Firms hired 'all engineering graduates with aptitude in computing', whatever their branch, and trained them in 12 weeks to six months (S0001, p. 56). Utilisation of about 69-72% including trainees (S0266) shows the bench built into the model.

**Who got hired.** Selection by high marks and English favoured those already advantaged. In Chennai, anthropologists found IT staff drawn largely from salaried middle-class families and, by strong impression, from the Forward Castes, especially Tamil Brahmans (S0117, pp. 126, 141-143). About a fifth of recruits at their firm were women (S0117, p. 125).

**The work.** The same scholars reported that the work had 'progressively become more routinized and reliant on standard packages', so IIT graduates were 'over-qualified' (S0118, p. 189). The factory produced trainable generalists for routine work. That is the population most exposed to the AI tools of the later chapters.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 Counting the engineers

No number in this book is more abused than the annual count of Indian engineers, and the chapter must get it right before it says anything else.

**Institutions.** The firmest series counts colleges. The Ministry of Statistics' compilation gives engineering and technology colleges as 289 in 1990-91, 486 in 1997-98, 678 in 2000-01, 1,077 in 2001-02 and 1,562 in 2005-06 (S0248). The 1950s to the 1980s added roughly a hundred colleges a decade. The eight years after 1997-98 added more than a thousand. Rajaraman describes 'a rapid expansion of engineering colleges between 1998 and 2010' and 'a rapid growth of private engineering colleges and colleges offering MCA courses'. He notes that 'Private corporate bodies were allowed to set up Universities in 2002 with relevant acts' (S0001, pp. 51, 56). The legal history (Unni Krishnan 1993, TMA Pai 2002) is told in Chapter 7.

**Students.** Rajaraman gives the most quoted growth figure: 'In 1998 the number of engineering students was around 135,000 and in 2010 it grew to 1,300,000, a growth of almost 10 times' (S0001, p. 56). On p. 12 he gives the same figure differently: 'Currently India graduates around 1.3 million engineers per year with 32% in Computer Engineering and IT from about 3000 colleges.' The two passages describe one number as both students and annual graduates, and neither defines it. A four-year enrolment of 135,000 in 1998 would be far too small given 486 colleges, so 135,000 is most likely annual intake. By the same logic 1.3 million in 2010 is most likely intake, or approved seats, rather than graduates. **New dispute (ii).** The drafting should say 'about 1.3 million seats' and not 'graduates'.

**Graduates.** The Duke study of December 2005 is the best attempt to count output. Using NASSCOM projections built on government data with a 'three- to four-year lag', it put India's 2004 production of engineering, computer science and IT graduates at 215,000. Of these, 112,000 had four-year bachelor's degrees and 103,000 three-year diplomas (S0213, pp. 5, 8). It noted that 'the most recent official Indian graduation data are from 1993' (S0213, p. 8). That is a striking admission: India had no official annual count of its own engineering graduates during the boom. The study's purpose was to rebut American press claims that India produced 350,000 engineers a year against the US's 70,000. Its verdict was that these figures 'include not only four-year degrees, but also three-year training programs and diploma holders' and were compared against US four-year degrees (S0213, pp. 2-3). Per million people, it estimated the US produced about 750 engineering, CS and IT graduates, China 500 and India 200 (S0213, p. 6).

By 2013 the assessment firm Aspiring Minds was writing of 'the six hundred thousand engineers that graduate annually' (S0153, p. 7), without a source. If both the Duke and Aspiring Minds figures are roughly right, bachelor's output grew about five times in a decade. That is consistent with the tripling in college numbers and with rising intake per college.

**Seats.** An analysis of AICTE approved-intake data and AISHE enrolment by the education non-profit Avanti Fellows (S0267, secondary, dated August 2026) gives approved undergraduate engineering intake as 15.49 lakh in 2012-13, peaking at 17.05 lakh in 2014-15 and falling to 12.53 lakh by 2021-22. It finds that 37.6% of approved seats were empty in 2012-13, rising to 49.5% in 2016-17. The regulator's figures should be checked directly in Phase 3 (S0151). The pattern is clear enough: by the early 2010s the country had built more engineering seats than students wanted to fill.

**Myth M08 ('India produces 1.5 million engineers a year').** The figure matches approved seats around 2012-15, not graduates. The graduate figure was about 112,000 bachelor's in 2004 and perhaps 600,000 by 2013. Verdict stays MIXED to MOSTLY FALSE, with a firmer explanation.

### 3.2 Hiring by the thousand

The second half of the factory was inside the firms. Infosys's filings give the clearest run of numbers, though they are company figures.

In fiscal 1998 Infosys received about 70,870 applications, tested 21,350, interviewed 5,800, made 1,790 offers, and saw 1,300 accepted (S0089, p. 6). In fiscal 2002, about 193,640 applications led to 1,710 offers (S0260). In fiscal 2005 the company 'screened over 1,348,800 employment applications, tested over 122,000 applicants, interviewed over 36,600 applicants and made approximately 14,500 offers of employment' (S0264). In fiscal 2008 it screened over 885,000 applications, tested 227,600, interviewed 97,500 and made about 46,300 offers (S0265). In fiscal 2012 it received about 622,970 applications, interviewed 60,860 and made 41,460 offers (S0266).

The fall in applications between FY2005 and FY2008 alongside a tripling of offers suggests a change in what was counted (new dispute jj). Offers are the better measure. Offers grew roughly 26-fold in ten years, from about 1,790 to about 46,300. Infosys had about 9,400 IT professionals in March 2002 (S0260), about 91,200 employees in March 2008, of whom about 18,700 worked outside India (S0265), and about 150,000 in March 2012 (S0266).

Rajaraman describes the industry-wide pattern (S0001, p. 56). 'The IT companies' common complaint was that there were not enough employable graduates with computer science degrees. Instead they recruited all engineering graduates with aptitude in computing; however they could not be directly placed as members of project teams. All major IT companies had in-house training programmes ranging from 12 weeks to 6 months.' He adds that 'every major software company was recruiting around 8000 graduates each year' and that, with attrition of 15-20% and workforces 'of the order of 120,000', 'the replacement numbers themselves were over 15,000 each year'.

This passage explains the shape of the factory. The firms did not need computer scientists. They needed graduates who could pass an aptitude test and be trained in three months. A mechanical or civil engineer would do. That is why the firms recruited across branches, and why a degree in any engineering branch became a ticket into software. It also helps explain why colleges expanded so fast. The degree, not the branch, was the entry requirement for the best-paid jobs open to most graduates.

### 3.3 Mysore

Infosys's training campus at Mysore is the factory in physical form. In 2002 the company reported a 200-acre campus 'recently set up' (S0260). In its FY2005 filing it described 'an employee training facility in Mysore' that 'is able to house 4,500 trainees at any one time, and is able to provide the facilities required for the training of approximately 12,000 employees annually'. It noted that new employees were not billed during 'our 14-week training course' (S0264). By FY2008 capacity was 4,800 at a time and 15,200 a year, on a campus of 336 acres (S0265). By FY2012: 'We train the new engineering graduates that join us at our Global Education Center in Mysore. With a total built-up area of 1.44 million square feet, the Infosys Global Education Center can train approximately 14,000 employees at a time' (S0266). The company's press releases put the opening by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh in February 2005 (S0115, not read; confirm).

Business Today's earlier description of the Bangalore training centre, where 'fifty of the country's best software instructors put every batch of engineers recruited by the company through a gruelling three-month training program' (S0262), shows the model before Mysore. Mysore industrialised it.

The chapter needs a trainee's account of Mysore in their own words. None is in hand.

### 3.4 The pyramid and the bench

The shape of the workforce followed from the shape of the project. Infosys's 1999 description of 'two to five' professionals at the client and '10 to 50' in India (S0089, p. 45; Ch. 11) is the pyramid. Its base had to be refilled every year by new graduates. Promotion for those already inside depended on it: in 2000 Infosys's HR head said people with 'two to three years experience are getting into managerial roles' (S0262).

Utilisation measures how much of the pyramid is billed. Infosys defined it as 'the proportion of total billed person months to total available person months excluding support personnel' (S0264, S0265). In fiscal 2012 utilisation was 69.0% including trainees and 75.9% excluding them, down from 72.1% and 79.6% the year before. The company said the fall 'adversely affected our profitability' (S0266). In other words, at any moment a quarter to a third of the company's technical staff was not billed: in training, between projects, or waiting. This is the bench. It was not waste in the model's terms. Under time-and-materials pricing a firm must be able to staff a new project at once, so it keeps trained people ready. But it is a cost that grows with every hiring wave and is cut first in a downturn. Chapter 11 showed the reverse case in 2001-02 (S0263), and Chapter 14 covers 2008-09.

Attrition shows the other side of the pyramid. Infosys reported its own attrition at 10.5% in fiscal 2004-05 and 13.4% in fiscal 2006-08, against an industry estimate of 'approximately 20%' (S0264, S0265). Fuller and Narasimhan cite a senior Infosys executive giving 7% for 2003 (S0117, p. 136 n. 11). Their own evidence was that job-hopping was 'less prevalent in the IT sector' than people in Chennai believed (S0117, p. 137).

### 3.5 Who got in: marks, English and caste

The best evidence on who the factory selected is ethnographic. Between August 2003 and February 2005 the anthropologists Chris Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan studied IT professionals in Chennai. Most of their interviews were in one of India's six largest software companies, which they call 'Indian Computer Services' (ICS) (S0117, pp. 124-125). They interviewed nineteen ICS staff, a sample they call 'tiny' and 'the haphazard product of the problems of access', and 'All names used are pseudonyms' (S0117, p. 127).

The selection process was a filter on school marks. Students needed 'a consistent record of high examination marks before they can sit the company's aptitude test and proceed to an interview, where only one out of twelve is offered a job' (S0117, p. 125). Starting pay was 'around 15,000 rupees per month' for people in their early twenties and could 'easily double within a few years'. That put new recruits, individually, near the income of the top 6% of Indian households (S0117, pp. 125-126).

On caste, they write that ICS 'is said to be dominated by the high-status "Forward Castes" - especially Tamil Brahmans, who form about 2.5 per cent of Tamilnadu's population - and it is our strong impression that this is true'. They then add that 'no accurate figures exist' (S0117, p. 126). Their 2008 paper repeats that Tamil Brahmans are 'disproportionately well represented' in south Indian IT, and that 'no figures to confirm this assertion exist, but all the circumstantial evidence suggests that it is true' (S0118, p. 170). The drafting must keep both halves: a strong scholarly impression, without numbers.

Their explanation of the mechanism matters more than the impression (S0117, pp. 142-143):
- **Reservations.** Tamil Nadu reserved 68% of state posts and college seats for Other Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and 'many people say that this is why many private companies employ a lot of Brahmans. Yet reservations alone cannot fully explain upper-caste predominance.'
- **Urban background and English.** Brahmans are increasingly urban and more likely to have 'the social skills and cultural knowledge, including fluency in English, needed to work in global IT companies and to pass their selection tests in the first place'.
- **Marks.** 'Because ICS allows only candidates with consistently high marks to apply for posts, its selection policy in practice favours Forward Caste candidates who, from a tender age, have been motivated to achieve repeated scholastic success.'
- **Aptitude myth.** They dismiss as 'fanciful' the idea that Brahmans have a special aptitude for software from traditional learning.

A footnote shows how small the measurable gaps in marks were. Cut-off marks for electronics and communication at Anna University in 2004 were 293.28 out of 300 for Forward Caste candidates, 290.57 for Backward, 283.67 for Most Backward and 276.13 for Scheduled Castes (S0117, p. 143 n. 13).

This is the core of the 'who got hired' argument. A selection system that looked only at marks, aptitude tests and interviews in English reproduced the advantages of urban, educated, upper-caste families without needing to look at caste at all. It is one city and one firm. National data on caste in IT employment (S0211, S0212) have not been verified and must not be used until read.

Family background points the same way. 'Most younger people we have interviewed have fathers (and occasionally mothers) who are or were employed in professional or managerial positions in government, or public- or private-sector firms, and the only exceptional fathers are a few small businessmen or farmers' (S0117, p. 141). In this sample IT did not lift families into the middle class; it moved the children of the salaried middle class into a richer, private-sector version of it. That is direct evidence for myth M09.

Tier mattered as well as caste. Aspiring Minds, a vendor with an interest in the point, argued that '53% of employable candidates for IT services companies and 25% of employable candidates for IT product companies are studying beyond the top 750 colleges, and thus end up being invisible to most employers', because firms visit only high-ranking campuses and 'the college name is a key signal, and resumes from unknown colleges aren't shortlisted' (S0153, pp. 7-8). That is a claim about exclusion by college tier, which falls hardest on students from the new private colleges and small towns.

Region: Fuller and Narasimhan found that of twenty software engineers under thirty in their data, only three had studied outside Tamil Nadu. Most could stay because 'young men and women ... can nowadays obtain good engineering education in the state' (S0118, pp. 189-190). Saxenian reported that 60% of India's CS graduates came from Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu around 2000 (S0255, p. 14). The south's colleges fed the south's firms.

### 3.6 Women

At ICS 'Approximately one-fifth of new recruits are women', about the same as the industry. NASSCOM reported 79% of software professionals as men in 2001-02, projecting 65% by 2005 (S0117, p. 125 and n. 8). Current company shares (TCS 35.2%, Infosys 39%; S0160, S0161) belong to later chapters but show the direction.

Fuller and Narasimhan's findings on gender are more subtle than the 'empowerment' story the industry tells. Almost everyone they met in Chennai 'insisted to us that both sexes have equal technical skills', in contrast to English software firms where women faced a 'double bind' (S0117, p. 138). The difference lay in the family. 'Married women professionals consistently give their families higher priority than their work' (S0117, p. 138). Many men wanted 'home-loving' wives. Women preferred husbands in IT; men tended to prefer wives who were not (S0117, p. 138). One leading company reportedly started an in-house online matrimonial service to stop married women resigning to follow husbands elsewhere (S0117, p. 138 n. 12). Managers of two small firms 'admitted to reluctance about recruiting women, precisely because their family duties take them away from work' (S0117, p. 139).

Their conclusion: 'Plainly, the IT industry has not revolutionised the position of Indian women. Yet ... real change has occurred' (S0117, p. 140). Compared with Patricia Caplan's study of upper-middle-class Chennai women in 1974-75, for whom outside employment was 'not considered at all desirable', young women IT professionals 'appear to command greater respect and have more bargaining power within their families - particularly over their own marriages' (S0117, pp. 140-141).

Two women in their sample show the range. Anuradha (pseud.), a project manager in her early thirties, 'firmly believes that the IT industry has helped to empower women'. Her childcare was secured by both sets of parents (S0117, pp. 129-130). Jayashree (pseud.), about 35, found childcare 'an endlessly negotiated series of arrangements', joked that household chores had been 'outsourced', and later resigned to do contract work from home (S0117, pp. 130-131). The Britain sidebar (S0164) is not needed. The Indian evidence runs differently from Hicks's British story, because the skills of women were not doubted, and the chapter can say so without it.

### 3.7 What the work was

The factory's product was not a computer scientist. Fuller and Narasimhan report: 'Because software engineering in companies like ICS or TCS has progressively become more routinized and reliant on standard packages, IIT graduates are over-qualified and can secure more technically demanding, better-paid jobs. Nowadays, most new recruits in major software companies have only a bachelor's degree in engineering, usually from one of the leading engineering colleges in Tamilnadu' (S0118, p. 189).

Their informants said similar things. Shoaib (pseud.), 29, who ran ICS's intranet, claimed that 'any schoolleaver with reasonable intelligence could learn to do the job'. The authors say he was exaggerating, but 'we have met several IT professionals who are bored because their work is too easy' (S0117, p. 137). Raghu (pseud.), 22, a Tamil Brahman software engineer with an electronics and communication degree from Coimbatore, worked on production support for a British client. 'every day a new problem has to be solved', so he was never bored, but he thought 'he is not learning much - except how to say "no" to demanding, foreign clients', and wanted to do an MBA (S0117, p. 132). Parvati (pseud.), in her mid-twenties, found her work 'boring' after six months. A year later she was a team leader seeking a US posting (S0117, pp. 131-132). Sarojini (pseud.), her colleague, who joined as a trainee in 2002 after an electrical and electronics degree, found the work 'exciting and challenging' (S0117, p. 131).

The money was the clearest change. Raghu earned Rs 200,000 a year after one year, against his father's Rs 400,000 after thirty years as an engineer at a nuclear power station (S0117, p. 132). A personnel manager at a Chennai vehicle maker, Sampath (pseud.), said the industry had 'disturbed the social fabric' with 'quick promotions', 'fancy designations' and 'phenomenal compensation'. The authors report a colleague of his saying that at campus interviews by the late 1990s 'only fourth-rate men wanted to join' the firm (S0117, p. 134; their paraphrase). Shoaib turned down a state electricity board job because in the public sector you 'have to bow your head to somebody', but he also said 'there is no security in this job' (S0117, p. 130).

This is where the chapter meets the book's argument. The factory trained large numbers of graduates for documented, routinised work on standard packages, for clients abroad, billed by time. That is the work the later chapters show AI tools doing faster. The 2014 Aspiring Minds report already noted that 'the growth of entry-level jobs is diminishing' and that there was 'a slow but definite trend towards hiring candidates who already have decent expertise in programming' (S0153, p. 13). This is a vendor's observation, but it is the first documented sign in this research of the factory's demand curve bending.

### 3.8 'Employability'

Two figures dominate public talk: that only a quarter, or under a fifth, of Indian engineers are employable (M07). Both have traceable origins.

The '25%' comes from McKinsey Global Institute in 2005. As reported by the Duke study, 'McKinsey concluded that only 10% of Chinese engineers and 25% of Indian engineers can compete in the global outsourcing arena', citing 'limited language proficiency, educational quality, cultural issues' and other factors (S0213, p. 9). The McKinsey report has not been read.

The 'under 20%' comes from Aspiring Minds. Its 2014 report tested 'more than 1,20,000 engineering students from 520+ engineering colleges' graduating in 2013 on its own AMCAT test, against benchmarks it says it built with client companies, including 'six of the top-ten IT services companies' (S0153, p. 9). It found 'only 18.43% of them are employable for the Software Engineer-IT services role, while a dismal 3.95% are appropriately trained to be directly deployed on projects' (S0153, p. 7). Its own definition matters: the IT services figure measures whether a graduate is 'trainable into software engineers within a period of 3 to 6 months' under the firms' existing practice of hiring and then training (S0153, p. 12). For product companies the figure was 3.21%, among computer and IT branches only (S0153, p. 12).

These are estimates of suitability for particular roles by interested parties: a consultancy and a test vendor that sells the solution to the problem it measures. They are not labour-market measures. M07 stays MOSTLY FALSE as a statement about employability in general. The drafting can still use the Aspiring Minds report for what it shows about the factory's design: the firms' bar was trainability, not skill.
