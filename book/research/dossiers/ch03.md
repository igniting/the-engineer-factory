## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

Between the 1962 war and the 1977 election, India tried to make computers a matter of national self-reliance and, at the same time, to keep them from taking clerical jobs. Two kinds of scarcity shaped the period: foreign exchange and jobs. Computers were 'not considered a priority area that deserved foreign exchange outflow' (S0001, p. 18), so IBM built its Indian business by exporting key punches and using the foreign exchange to import used 1401s, refurbished in Bombay and rented at high prices (S0001, pp. 18-19; S0020, p. 33). By 1970 about 80 of these were India's most common computer (S0001, p. 19); by August 1971 India had 140 computers in all (S0020, p. 25).

The state answered with planning. The Bhabha Committee (1963-66) recommended a Department of Electronics and Electronics Commission, created in 1970, and local manufacture of all but the largest machines (S0001, pp. 20-24). The public-sector ECIL built TDC computers but sold only 98 between 1971 and 1978, four of them to private firms (S0001, pp. 25-26). The DoE became the gatekeeper for imports, with delays of one to three years (S0001, p. 23).

Labour pushed from the other side. LIC's decision to install a computer around 1965 set off an agitation by the insurance employees' union that spread to railways, oil companies and banks (S0020, pp. 2-4, 63). A tripartite Committee on Automation, chaired first by R. Venkataraman and then V.M. Dandekar, recommended in 1972 that every commercial computer require a justification report and the workers' prior agreement (S0020, pp. 7, 67-74).

Yet the period trained people. IBM trained 'a whole generation' of programmers and engineers (S0001, p. 19); ECIL trained over a thousand engineers (S0001, p. 26); NCSDCT ran evening software courses in Bombay (S0013, p. 451). And in 1970 Rajaraman argued that 'Foreign exchange can also be earned by software export' (S0001, p. 21). The first software-export import policy followed in 1974 (S0001, p. 28).

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 Computers without foreign exchange: IBM's Indian business

Before 1970 there was no computer policy beyond the 1958 Scientific Policy Resolution; computers fell under the Directorate General of Technical Development and the Department of Defence Supplies (S0001, p. 18). The governing rule was money: 'Foreign companies were permitted to manufacture and sell computers provided foreign exchange was not required. Computers were not considered a priority area that deserved foreign exchange outflow.' (S0001, p. 18). IBM and the British Tabulating Machine Company (later ICL) already sold punched-card equipment to firms, Indian Railways and statistics departments (S0001, p. 18).

IBM's solution, as both Rajaraman and the 1972 Committee on Automation describe it, was an export-for-import trade. It had a commercial outlet from 1950 and began manufacturing in 1963: punch cards in Bangalore, and 029 key punches and unit-record equipment at Kurla, Bombay, exported to IBM units in South-East Asia (S0020, para 2.54, p. 33 [check page]). The foreign exchange earned paid for imports of used, disassembled 1401 computers, bought at low inter-company prices, which were reconditioned, repainted and rented in India (S0001, pp. 18-19; S0020, p. 33). The government licensed IBM to recondition 68 1401s over three years (22 in 1968, 22 in 1969, 24 in 1970) and make up to 4,000 key punches; IBM had applied to recondition System/360s instead for sale in rupees (S0020, para 2.54, p. 33 [check page]). ICL's Indian company (formed 1963) used the same model, exporting card equipment made in Poona (S0020, para 2.54, p. 33 [check page]).

By 1970 the 1401 was India's most popular computer, with about 80 installations; Indian Railways had more than a dozen, most Tata companies and textile firms had them, TCS's first computer was one and ISI installed one (S0001, p. 19). A high-end 1401 rented for about Rs 850,000 a year (about $190,000 at the prevailing rate), and IBM 'was in fact accused of profiteering', since US rentals were lower; IBM replied that it was selling a service that included maintenance and a systems engineer who designed software (S0001, p. 19). Rajaraman, no admirer of IBM's pricing, credits it with training: 'A whole generation of maintenance engineers and programmers were trained and nurtured by IBM. In short IBM set a bench mark for professional operations in India' (S0001, p. 19). ICL had 48 machines assembled by Bharat Electronics between 1965 and 1975 (S0001, p. 19).

The Committee on Automation's count shows how small the field was: 30 computers in 1965, 88 in 1968 (S0020, p. 4), and 140 by August 1971: 10 in government departments, 39 in public undertakings, 55 in private firms and 36 in research institutions; 35 in Bombay, 20 in Delhi, 14 in Calcutta, 11 in Bangalore, 7 in Madras (S0020, p. 25). They were used mostly for payroll, billing and accounts; the State Bank of India used one to reconcile some 70,000 inter-branch transactions a day; Delhi Cloth Mills and Calico used linear programming to blend cotton (S0020, pp. 30-31).

### 3.2 The Bhabha Committee and the self-reliance plan

The 1962 border war persuaded the government that electronics mattered for defence; in 1963 it appointed a committee under Homi Bhabha (S0001, p. 20). The report was ready by 1966, but Bhabha was killed in an air crash before formally submitting it (S0001, p. 20); Gopalakrishnan and co-authors say S. Bhagavantam completed it and a 1966 committee under Vikram Sarabhai was set up to implement it (S0052†). Its main recommendations were a Department of Electronics and an Electronics Commission with wide financial and executive powers (S0001, p. 20). A computer subcommittee chaired by R. Narasimhan projected demand for ten years: one very large imported computer for a national facility, three to five medium-large imported machines as Regional Computer Centres run like utilities, and all smaller computers to be manufactured in India from local components, about 500 a year (S0001, pp. 20-21). Its report argued that 'attaining self-sufficiency in systems engineering and fabrication is of fundamental importance from the point of view of the defence and security of our country' (quoted in S0001, p. 24).

In March 1970 a National Electronics Conference at TIFR debated the plan (S0001, p. 21; S0052†). Rajaraman, then at IIT Kanpur, argued that India lacked the market volume to build up from components to circuits to systems, and should instead design systems and import components, paying for imports with exports: 'Foreign exchange can also be earned by software export.' 'However, my comment was not taken seriously' (S0001, p. 21, quoting S0024). Vikram Sarabhai, chairing, called computing 'far more fundamental, of wider significance than any other field of electronics' and of 'particular relevance to our developing country where resources are scarce'; multinationals had a role 'on honourable commercially rewarding terms, on ground rules which are acceptable to India', but not 'at the cost of a major national effort' (quoted in S0001, p. 22).

### 3.3 DoE, the Electronics Commission and the import gate

The DoE and Electronics Commission were set up in 1970, with M.G.K. Menon, TIFR's director, as DoE secretary and Commission chairman (S0001, p. 24; S0013, p. 447). In 1970 the DoE also took over import licensing for computers as a 'single window', judging requests by essentiality, whether an Indian equivalent existed and how much foreign exchange was needed (S0001, p. 23). The political economy pressed hard: banks were nationalised in 1969, the rupee was not convertible, the trade deficit was permanent, and 'obtaining foreign exchange for import of equipment required elaborate justification' (S0001, p. 22). The 1973 oil shock made foreign exchange scarcer; unless UNDP or a foreign government paid, clearance took one to three years, hurting universities, laboratories, industry and would-be software exporters (S0001, p. 23). The 1971 war with Pakistan brought US embargoes on electronics and computers, and the 1974 nuclear test an embargo on advanced electronic equipment and scientific software (S0001, p. 23). The DoE 'became very unpopular' and was seen as a regulator protecting ECIL; 'no private Indian company was allowed to manufacture computers' (S0001, p. 27).

### 3.4 ECIL: building computers in a 'cottage industry'

ECIL was set up at Hyderabad in 1967 under the Department of Atomic Energy to commercialise BARC's electronics; the DoE funded a computer division (S0001, p. 22). It was led by S. Srikantan, who had designed BARC's TDC-12, a 12-bit real-time machine similar to DEC's PDP-8 (S0001, p. 25). India's own statement to the IAEA called the TDC-12, commissioned at BARC on 21 January 1969, capable of 250,000 additions and subtractions a second and 'the first real time digital computer developed in the country', to be produced by ECIL (S0040, via Phase 1 search summary†). ECIL had no commercial computer on which to write software, so it outsourced work to TIFR, IIT Kanpur and IIM Ahmedabad; an operating system, assembler, BASIC and FORTRAN were built (S0001, p. 25). The TDC-312 followed in 1974 and the 16-bit TDC-316, with a COBOL compiler, in 1975; the microprocessor-based Micro 78 in 1978 (S0001, p. 25). Typical prices were Rs 600,000 for a TDC-12, Rs 1 million for a TDC-312 and Rs 3 million for a TDC-316 (S0001, p. 25, Table 1).

From 1971 to 1978 ECIL sold 98 computers (21 TDC-12, 32 TDC-312, 35 TDC-316, 10 Micro 78), plus 75 ruggedised TDC-316s for defence (S0001, p. 25). Rajaraman's assessment: it 'worked more like a cottage industry'; buyers were mostly atomic energy establishments, government departments and universities 'constrained to buy' from it; 'Only 4 machines were sold to the private sector'; its machines were overpriced and short of software (S0001, pp. 25-26). Its real achievements were elsewhere: the Air Defence Ground Environment System, with TIFR designing the system and software under P.V.S. Rao, three TDC-316s per system, Rs 30 million each, 25 deployed by the Air Force between 1969 and 1984 (S0001, p. 26); and machines for atomic energy, satellite telemetry, engine testing and steel-mill control where imports were embargoed (S0001, p. 26). And people: ECIL trained 'over a thousand engineers in designing systems and developing systems software', many of whom later led groups in private firms (S0001, p. 26). The DoE gave ECIL grants and loans of Rs 37.44 million (S0001, p. 26, citing S0025, p. 30).

The DoE delayed a minicomputer policy that would let private firms (many already making calculators) build computers, partly to save foreign exchange on imported components and partly to protect ECIL; a panel chaired by P.V.S. Rao said minicomputers could be made without foreign collaboration; the policy came only in 1978, after the Janata government replaced Menon (S0001, p. 24). A semiconductor committee formed in 1971 reported in 1973 but nothing happened until 1978 (S0001, p. 28).

### 3.5 Labour: 'a painful luxury'

Opposition to office computers grew from a template set in 1957, when the Indian Labour Conference's Model Agreement on rationalisation required no retrenchment or loss of earnings, equitable sharing of gains and prior consultation with unions (S0020, pp. 1-2). The Committee on Automation dates organised opposition to computers from 1965, when LIC's management decided 'unilateral[ly]' to introduce one; the All India Insurance Employees' Association began an agitation and the central trade union organisations took up the issue (S0020, pp. 2, 63). A union-side biography dates insurance employees' 'struggles against computerisation' earlier, to 1959-61, and says computers were installed step by step and the agitation died down (S0226). The unions, in the committee's summary, saw automation 'as a painful luxury, and as a labour saving device which has no place in a developing economy marked by labour surpluses and scarce capital resources' (S0020, p. 2); Bassett notes they called computers 'job eaters' (S0003, p. 799).

Parliament heard the dispute. In November 1966 the Finance Minister reportedly told the Lok Sabha that LIC's switch to computers would take about three years and would cut 383 jobs while creating 225 (S0227†, not read). On 23 March 1967 George Fernandes asked how many computers LIC had installed, at what cost, and how many employees had lost their jobs; Morarji Desai replied that LIC was autonomous, that talks should be with its chairman, and that 'there is going to be no retrenchment on this account' (S0019†, not read). Fernandes would be Industry Minister when IBM left India eleven years later (Ch. 5).

Labour conferences in July 1966 and May 1967 failed to agree; union conventions in 1966 and 1967 demanded an outright ban; employees in LIC, oil companies and railways stepped up opposition (S0020, pp. 3-4). The screening that did exist missed most machines: imports needing foreign exchange required Labour Ministry clearance, but most computers were locally assembled 1401s, so between 1967 and 1971 only five of 94 new computers were referred (S0020, p. 4). At a special Standing Labour Committee meeting on 18 July 1968, workers' representatives argued that 'since the country had an enormous volume of unemployment and was short of technological and capital resources the general orientation of policy should be against automation', with a freeze pending guidelines; employers argued computers were necessary for large concerns and created more jobs across the economy (S0020, pp. 5-6). The meeting ended without consensus (S0020, p. 6).

### 3.6 The Committee on Automation (1969-1972)

The government set up a tripartite Committee on Automation on 19 July 1969 under R. Venkataraman, a Planning Commission member; members included the trade unionist G. Ramanujam, industrialists such as Naval H. Tata, B.D. Somani and Babubhai Chinai, the economist V.M. Dandekar and the computer scientist V.R. Rao (S0020, pp. 7, front matter). Venkataraman resigned on 22 March 1971 and Dandekar, director of the Gokhale Institute in Poona, became chairman on 24 July 1971 (S0020, p. 7); the report was signed on 18 May 1972 (S0020). (This resolves the Phase 1 dispute over the chair: both are right; Rajaraman also notes the change, S0001, p. 22.)

The committee found that office computers mostly did routine work or sat under-used (S0020, p. 68). The committee was candid about its own evidence. It was 'greatly handicapped because of the inadequacy of available data' and found no national study of technological change; it relied on testimony, a questionnaire and case studies (S0020, p. 35). Computer use was very new: 'As many as 124 of the 140 computers in India have been installed only since 1965', and of 94 in commercial use all but three dated from after 1965 (S0020, p. 37). Planning a computer took about two years and integrating it two or three more, so the committee thought it would be at least five years before effects showed (S0020, p. 37). It noted the charge that some firms bought computers as status symbols, 'to keep up with the Joneses' (S0020, p. 37). Its own survey of 40 public and private organisations is revealing about the gap between the two sides: managements gave quality of service (36), faster tabulation (37), better decisions (36) and output (29) as motives, while only five named reduction of labour cost (S0020, p. 41, Table VI). Unions, as the committee recorded, opposed computers even when managements offered 'no retrenchment' guarantees, on the ground of lost potential employment (S0020, p. 63). Managements argued that installing a computer was a normal management decision requiring no prior consultation, while conceding that unions had a legitimate interest in its effects on jobs and conditions (S0020, p. 63).

Its appendix illustrates 'loss of potential employment' in one unnamed establishment: branch-clearance and accounts staff fell from 380 (November 1966) to 304 (January 1970) while volume rose; the old methods would have needed 516 people (S0020, p. A69). It recommended that computers be freely allowed in education, research, government statistics and defence (para 4.1); that commercial and industrial users, public or private, be examined case by case by experts (4.2); that shared computer service centres, 'in the nature of a public utility', preferably in the public sector, meet most needs (4.4-4.5); and that 'all use of computers by commercial and industrial establishments ... should be subject to a prior agreement with the workers' (4.7), through a Justification Report, two jointly chosen experts, negotiation and a National Bipartite Machinery (4.11-4.17) (S0020, pp. 67-73). It called its approach 'positive' and urged that India's needs be met 'as far as possible from indigenous sources', saving foreign exchange and creating jobs (4.20, S0020, pp. 73-74). Rajaraman judges that the report 'constrained the introduction of computers in banks, insurance companies and many private companies' (S0001, p. 22). A 1975 Public Accounts Committee report added that in a country of large-scale unemployment labour-saving computers 'may not be desirable or may even be detrimental' (S0001, p. 23, summarising S0022).

### 3.7 What the state built instead

The DoE's promotional work, which Rajaraman lists despite its reputation, laid foundations: NCSDCT at TIFR (1972; Rs 2.85 million and $2 million from UNDP), which installed DEC systems, sent scientists abroad and later became NCST (S0001, p. 27); the National Informatics Centre (1975; Rs 31.72 million and $4.4 million from UNDP) for government data processing (S0001, p. 27); Regional Computer Centres at Jadavpur (1977, with free student access to a Burroughs mainframe), Pune and Chandigarh (S0001, p. 28); and the Computer Maintenance Corporation (1976, Rs 53 million) to maintain imported machines, anticipating IBM's departure (S0001, p. 28). Narasimhan, who directed NCSDCT from 1975 to 1985, records that a UNDP mission in May 1973 recommended it be placed at TIFR, that its National Computation Centre served petroleum companies, dockyards, power and space agencies, state governments and town planners, and that it ran a one-year evening course in computer science at VJTI in central Bombay, over a telephone data link to TIFR's PDP-10, for 60 to 80 students at a time, preferring people already working in software and persuading employers to pay (S0013, pp. 450-452). 'Formal educational channels are wholly inadequate to cope with a demand of this magnitude,' he wrote (S0013, p. 451).

### 3.8 The first software-export opening

The DoE allowed computers to be imported exclusively for software export; the first policy paper on this appeared in 1974 (S0001, p. 28). TCS used it to import a Burroughs B6700 and convert other manufacturers' software for Burroughs, and also sought work in Iran, Switzerland, South Africa and Canada (S0001, p. 28; detail in Ch. 4). This is the earliest documented instance in this book's sources of the state tying computer imports to export earnings for software, the mechanism the working thesis puts at the centre of the profession.

### 3.9 Interpretations

Rajaraman, a participant, judges that it was 'strategically incorrect' for a poor country with low demand to try to design computers from the component level and hope to be self-reliant (S0001, conclusions, p. 60 [check page]). Narasimhan's 1999 view (Ch. 1) is that second-generation computers required production know-how only large manufacturers had (S0013, p. 449). Banerjee (2026), from the publisher's summary and a news report, argues that Cold War politics, trade imbalances, US corporate monopolies and an elite preference for profitable services blocked a self-reliant industry (S0221†, S0222†). The Committee on Automation shows a fourth force: organised clerical labour, whose opposition made computers politically costly in exactly the white-collar sectors (insurance, banking, railways) that would later be the largest users (S0020). The chapter should present all four, and note that the sources agree on the facts (few machines, slow imports, ECIL's tiny sales) while disagreeing on why.

### 3.10 Education and profession in this chapter

*Education.* The people who learned computing in this period learned it from IBM (systems engineers, maintenance engineers; S0001, p. 19), at ECIL (over a thousand engineers; S0001, p. 26), at TIFR and NCSDCT (including the VJTI evening course; S0013, p. 451), at IIT Kanpur's ten-day courses (S0003; Ch. 2) and on defence projects such as ADGES (S0001, p. 26). Formal degrees remained rare (Ch. 2).

*Profession.* Computing jobs were in public-sector firms (ECIL, BEL, CMC from 1976), government (NIC, DoE, railways), research institutes, IBM and ICL, and the computer departments of a few dozen large firms and banks (S0020, p. 25; S0001). Clerical workers, not programmers, were the organised workforce of the period; their unions shaped where computers could go (S0020). How programmers were paid is not documented in sources read.

## 6. Disputes

1. **Committee on Automation chair.** Resolved: Venkataraman (1969-71), then Dandekar (1971-72) (S0020, p. 7; S0001, p. 22).
2. **When union opposition began.** 1965 (S0020, pp. 2, 63) vs 1959-61 (S0226). Possibly two waves (LIC's early unit-record mechanisation vs the 1965 computer). Present both; prefer S0020 for the computer agitation.
3. **Bhabha Committee report date.** 'Ready by 1966' (S0001, p. 20) vs Narasimhan's computer report cited as part of a 1968 report (S0001, p. 24) vs S0001 ref. 32 'Bombay, 1960'. Obtain S0018.
4. **ECIL as first commercial maker.** TDC-12 'first real time digital computer developed in the country' (S0040†) vs TIFRAC (Ch. 1). Different claims (real-time; production); word carefully.

## 7. Documented scenes

1. **Repainting old 1401s in Kurla, late 1960s.** Used IBM computers, bought cheaply abroad with foreign exchange earned by exporting key punches, are dismantled, repaired, repainted and rented to Indian firms at prices critics call profiteering (S0001, pp. 18-19; S0020, p. 33).
2. **LIC's computer, 1965.** LIC management decides to install a computer without consulting workers; the insurance employees' union begins an agitation that the central unions take up (S0020, pp. 2, 63).
3. **Question time, 23 March 1967.** George Fernandes asks how many LIC employees have lost their jobs to the computer; Morarji Desai promises no retrenchment (S0019†; verify text).
4. **The special Standing Labour Committee, 18 July 1968.** Workers ask for a freeze on automation; employers say computers create jobs; the meeting ends without agreement (S0020, pp. 5-6).
5. **TIFR, March 1970.** At the National Electronics Conference, the young Rajaraman proposes designing systems, importing components and earning foreign exchange through software export; he is ignored; Sarabhai calls for a national effort (S0001, pp. 21-22).
6. **ECIL's customers.** Of 98 computers sold in seven years, four go to private companies; most go to state bodies obliged to buy them (S0001, pp. 25-26).
7. **The committee signs, 18 May 1972.** A tripartite committee of union leaders, industrialists and economists recommends that no commercial firm use a computer without its workers' prior agreement (S0020).
8. **The evening class at VJTI.** Sixty to eighty working programmers in central Bombay study at night over a telephone link to TIFR's PDP-10, their employers paying (S0013, p. 451; date of first course not documented).
9. **Radar screens on the border, 1969-84.** TDC-316 computers built in Hyderabad run air-defence systems because importing them would expose secrets and risk embargoes (S0001, p. 26).
