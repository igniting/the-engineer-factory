## S0001 Rajaraman, pp. 31-42 (read in full earlier). Ch. 8 material, with corrected pages.

### Asian Games, 1982 (p. 31)

- Rajiv Gandhi 'was technically savvy, being an amateur electronics buff'. He decided computers should handle schedules, records, results and 'all other clerical chores'.
- DCM computers served as terminals, linked to an HP server. NIC engineers built the software 'in a short period of six months'.
- 'The computerization of the games was a resounding success.' It brought N. Seshagiri (NIC Director General) close to Rajiv Gandhi.

### 1984 policy (pp. 31-32)

Rajiv Gandhi had heard from private entrepreneurs, while organising the Games, of 'their difficulties in manufacturing computers due to myriad rules and regulations'. Seshagiri was 'his informal advisor'. 'A liberalized policy on minicomputers was announced in 1984 with the following main highlights':

- any manufacturer could make 32-bit machines;
- capacity limits were removed;
- assembled boards could be imported, which 'changed manufacturing to assembly and system integration ... Thus local manufacturing of boards slowly disappeared';
- import of application software allowed, with duty reduced;
- mainframe imports simplified.

Protections kept for the state firms:
- superminis and mainframes reserved for ECIL;
- compulsory maintenance of imported computers by CMC or the users themselves.

Export measures:
- software recognised as an 'industry', which brought bank loans and duty exemptions; excise exemption;
- customs duty on computers imported for software export reduced: 'Earlier it used to be over 100%';
- onsite work and its profits 'recognized as "software export"'.

### 1986 (p. 32)

- Duty-free import of computers for export, against an export obligation of 250% of cost in 4 years. Where no foreign exchange was spent, 150%.
- Duty-free software tools.
- Export allowed 'using communication systems such as satellite and cable'.
- CMC's Indonet (IBM 4342 network) available to exporters at concessional tariff.

### Telecoms (p. 33)

- 'In 1982 there was a waiting time of over 2 years to get a land line telephone connection.'
- MTNL corporatised for Bombay and Delhi; VSNL set up in 1986.
- C-DOT formed in 1984 under Sam Pitroda, with M.V. Pitke and G.B. Meemamsi. It developed a rugged rural digital exchange 'in a record time of 3 years'.
- PCO (public call office) booths with microprocessor billing: 'Every nook and corner of India was connected within 5 years. The power of microcomputer was visible to all'.

### Other initiatives (pp. 34-35)

- MCA and IIT CS (pp. 34-35; Ch. 7).
- 1981 export committee (p. 35).
- CMC's Project INTERACT ($2.75m UNDP).
- CAD programme 1984 ($1.5m UNDP plus Rs 340m) at IIT Kanpur, IIT Bombay, IISc and Jadavpur. 'During the mid 80s the US Government had banned the export of CAD tools in electronics to even educational institutions in India.'
- Computer-aided management programme at the IIMs (1984).

### TI (p. 36)

'The DoE negotiated with the Department of Telecommunication (DoT) in 1985 to allow Texas Instruments (TI) to set up a dedicated satellite communication link to its centre at Dallas, USA. TI's centre in Bangalore developed software tools and sent them to its Dallas centre via a dedicated satellite communication link. This was the first step in breaking the monopoly of the DoT in communications and had far reaching consequences later in the growth of "off shore" software development centres and the Software Technology Parks programme of the DoE.'

### Banks (p. 36)

- The RBI Rangarajan committee: all banks to have EDP cells; back-office work by computer; Unix as standard OS. Bank demand gave 'an impetus to private computer manufacturing companies'.
- 'The increase in computer use by banks was a red flag to the trade unions and they observed 1984 as "anti-computerization year"'.
- 'This perception changed in 1986 when a passenger reservation system was implemented by CMC for the Indian Railways.'
- Also p. 36: Sampath teacher-training committee (1985); KBCS programme (1985, $5.2m UNDP).

### SERC and Cray (pp. 36-37)

- The Ministry of HRD granted IISc Rs 500 million (at Rs 12 per USD) in 1984 for a Supercomputer Education and Research Centre.
- A committee chose a Cray Y-MP. 'Cray was confident of getting a licence'. A US State Department team negotiated conditions of use.
- 'As the chairman of SERC of the Indian Institute of Science between 1982 and 1994, I participated in the entire exercise.' The US 'did not give an export licence to Cray before the date specified in the contract'. The order was cancelled.
- IISc instead bought a CDC Cyber 992, two CDC 4360s, a VAX 8810 and IBM RS6000 clusters. 'The denial of Cray was a blessing in disguise'.
- By 2010 the US had loosened controls.

### C-DAC (p. 37)

- The Science Advisory Committee to the PM (C.N.R. Rao) set up a committee in 1986, chaired by Rajaraman, 'as a consequence of the difficulties encountered by India in buying supercomputers from the USA and Japan'.
- It proposed 'a mission mode project to build parallel computers with a speed of a giga-flop or above'.
- DoE established C-DAC at Pune in 1988 with Rs 300 million. 'The project was successfully completed in 1991.'
- Also: PARAM PADMA ranked 171 in 2007; the GIST chip for Indian scripts.
- Other parallel machines, 1985-92: FLOSOLVER (NAL), PACE (DRDO), ANUPAM (BARC).

### Railway reservations (p. 38)

- Began 1984, completed 1986.
- 1984: over 5 million passengers, over 600 long-distance trains, about 50,000 reservation requests. 'Clerks kept numerous ledgers, one for each train'.
- CMC's proposal was accepted. 'by 1986 a reservation system was developed and implemented at the New Delhi booking office. It had 50 counters'.
- 'This was perceived in 1986 by the general public as almost a miracle.' Confirmed reservations within 30 minutes.
- Clerks reconciled cash 'within 30 minutes after closing time whereas in the manual system it used to take at least two hours'.
- **'There was also no reduction of staff; in fact there was an increase as more counters were operated with more clerks working simultaneously.'**
- Built on a VAX cluster in FORTRAN. 'It was the first time in the world that such a system was developed for on-line transaction processing on a VAX cluster' (an unverified claim; flag it). 'The entire software effort was by Indian software engineers'.
- 'There was an attitudinal change among both the general public and the white collar workers about computerization.' Some bank unions then accepted computerisation.
- Dispute (g): S0001 says 1986. Search summaries give a 15 November 1985 pilot (IMPRESS, Northern Railway). The Ministry of Railways says computerised reservation was introduced at New Delhi in 1986. Use 'pilot 1985, live 1986'.

### ERNET (pp. 38-39)

1986; UNDP $6m plus Rs 41m. Five IITs, IISc, NCST. Brought the Internet to India under S. Ramani.

### STPs (p. 39)

- 'Software Technology Parks provided infrastructure such as buildings, work stations and continuous uninterrupted power supply'.
- Satellite links let firms 'develop software on the computers of their overseas customers from access terminals located in their respective premises'.
- 'As the investment required to set up a company in STP was low, this initiative allowed many small entrepreneurs to enter the software services export business. The first STP was established in Bangalore in 1990.'
- Conflict with S0241 and S0091: Pune first (August 1990).

### EVMs (p. 39)

1982 by-election in Kerala.

### Consequences (pp. 39-42)

- 1984 liberalisation: computers grew 100% and cost fell 50% within two years. 'screw driver' manufacturing (p. 40).
- Sterling's Shiva PC at Rs 29,000 (1987). The Taiwan/Singapore/Hong Kong 'consultant' anecdote. Self-reliance 'truly and completely buried' (p. 41).
- Exports from insignificant (1978) to $131m (1990).
- 'Breaking the monopoly of VSNL ... providing satellite communication through VSATs at a much lower cost, and establishing Software Technology Parks allowed many entrepreneurs to enter the software export business.'
- 'Permitting Texas Instruments (TI) to open an off shore centre in Bangalore ... was a trend setter.'
- GE, Citibank, American Express and Cadence set up centres 'to take advantage of the lower cost and the high quality of software developers' (p. 41).
- p. 42:
  - MAIT 1982; NASSCOM 1988, 'fortunate in employing Dewang Mehta as its President in 1991. Mehta was a superb lobbyist'. 38 members in 1988, 464 by 1999.
  - DoE run by technocrats 1970-88. In 1989 'A difference of opinion between two technocrats' led to an IAS officer taking over.
  - N. Vittal (from 1990) 'was instrumental in setting up STPs and obtaining a host of fiscal concessions to the software industry by persuading the bureaucrats of the Ministry of Finance'.

## S0241 Hanna (World Bank 1994), Annexes 2-3 (corrected pages)

### p. 96: plans against performance

- 'when it announced the Computer Policy Plan in November 1984 and expected software exports to reach US$258 million (or 0.5 percent of the world market) by 1990'. Later targets: '$400 million and US$800 million by 1992 and 1995 respectively'.
- Actual 1990/91 exports were $131.2m (S0085), about half the 1984 target.
- 'policy making and implementation have fundamentally focused on ways to make export figures rise'. Weaknesses: 'troublesome delays exist for import of computers and spare parts, local communications links are poor ... high export commitments must be made to import foreign hardware'.
- 'On the positive side, it has pushed firms into exporting. On the negative side, this pushing has been short-term oriented'.

### p. 96: export zones

- SEEPZ (Bombay), NOIDA and MEPZ account for most electronics and software exports.
- Incentives: duty-free import and export; wholly owned foreign firms allowed; profit repatriation; single window; tax exemptions; 5-year tax holiday; 25% domestic sales.

### p. 97: STPs

- Objectives: infrastructure, export promotion, training, design improvement. 15 STPs planned.
- 'Currently, the DOE has established 7 STPs in Pune (August 1990), Bangalore (October 1990), Bhubaneshwer [sic] (December 1990), Hyderabad (February 1991), Thiruvananthapuram (July 1991), Gandhinagar (July 1991), and Noida (July 1991).'
- 'Approximately 160 companies have been approved'; their estimated exports '$7.28 million by 1994'.
- 'it may be too early to judge'.
- Piracy: Lotus estimated 140,000 of 150,000 copies of 1-2-3 in India pirated.

### p. 98: foreign collaboration

- '100% foreign equity holdings have been permitted since 1986 in export oriented projects, but only 40% foreign equity is allowed for companies producing software for the domestic market.'
- 'TI obtained a private Intelsat Business Service earth station so that its programmers can communicate with other TI operations using satellite links.' TI's approvals 'helped make India more attractive' to HP, DEC, British Telecom, Philips and Westinghouse.

## S0061 TI press release (8 August 2005). Full text.

- 'TI was the first multinational to establish a software design center in India' (company claim).
- Milestones: '1985 TI begins operation in India as the first multinational to establish a software design center in India and the first to install and use a satellite dish for exporting software.' 1989 mixed-signal IC design centre and ASIC CAD libraries; 1990 application-specific products.
- By 2005, 'close to 300 patent applications' from Bangalore (citing Dataquest July 2005).
- TI calls it a 'software design center'. Its later work was chip design (DSP, mixed-signal), which is relevant to the hardware question of Ch. 5 and Ch. 17.

## S0249 Roye, 'Revolution on a Bullock Cart', *Little India* (23 August 2013). Full text.

This is an eyewitness memory written 28 years later.

- 'On a memorable day in 1985, my Ambassador car got stuck behind a heavily laden bullock-cart outside the office on Miller Road. The cart was negotiating a steep incline at the gate leading to the office complex, Sona Towers.'
- Sam Pitroda (C-DOT 'located one floor above my office') among spectators: 'This can happen only in India.' (A second-hand quote, recalled after 28 years. Use as 'Roye recalls Pitroda saying'.)
- 'The consignment addressed to Texas Instruments' Bangalore office, was to enable ... two-way satellite link between India and the US'.
- 'Texas Instrument's 64 kbps dish antenna was like a whale surfacing.'
- About 12 years earlier (early 1970s): Vinay Deshpande in the basement of the Classic Building, Richmond Road (Processor Systems India), with 'a soldering iron in hand ... putting together hi-tech firmware, bound for the US'.
- C.S. Murali (IISc): 'It turbocharged the IT revolution ... and TI became the first major IT multinational in Bangalore.'
- Vijay Chandru: 'Bangalore was a tech city well before the arrival of the MNCs'.
