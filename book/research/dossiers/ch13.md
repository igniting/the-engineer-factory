**Chapter question.** What did the American temporary work visa do to Indian software, to the engineers who used it, and to the workers on both sides of it?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The visa.** The H-1B was created by the Immigration Act of 1990 with a cap of 65,000 a year. The cap 'is the same now as when it was established in 1990' (S0105, IF12912). It was first exhausted in 1997. Congress raised it to 115,000 and then 195,000 between 1998 and 2003. When it fell back to 65,000 in fiscal 2005, the limit 'was reached on the first day' (S0105, RL30498).

**Indians and the visa.** India's share of the visa rose from about 47% in 1999 (S0255, p. 12) and 49% of new arrivals in 2005 (S0105) to about 70% by 2014 (S0254, p. 11). By 2013 ten of the eleven largest sponsors had their main workforce in India. They included American-owned Cognizant, Accenture and IBM India as well as Infosys, TCS and Wipro (S0254, Table 1). So 'Indian outsourcing firms' is too narrow (M11). The sponsors were the offshore model, whoever owned it.

**Effects at home.** Khanna and Morales argue that the visa pulled Indian students into computing, while the cap kept most of them at home. Their model estimates a 4.9% larger Indian computing workforce in 2010 (S0254, pp. 1-3).

**Silicon Valley.** Saxenian found Indian-surnamed CEOs at 7% of Silicon Valley technology firms founded in 1980-98 (S0097, p. 23). Through the 1990s the 'brain drain ... continued unabated' (S0097, p. 65). Circulation is a story of the 2000s.

**The B-1 channel.** In 2013 Infosys paid $34 million to settle US allegations that it had used business visitor (B-1) visas for skilled work. It agreed that it had kept inaccurate employment-eligibility records and denied the rest (S0268).

**Displacement.** In 2016 a displaced Disney IT worker told the Senate how his team recorded its own jobs for replacements before leaving (S0269). That is a documented case of tacit work being codified for transfer, a process the AI chapters return to.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 A visa with a cap

The H-1B is a creature of the Immigration Act of 1990. The Congressional Research Service's current summary: 'The H-1B program traces its roots to the Immigration Act of 1990 (P.L. 101-649), in which Congress amended the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) to provide for the admission of foreign nationals on H-1B visas to work temporarily in the United States if hired by a U.S. employer to perform work in a specialty occupation' (S0105, IF12912, p. 1). A specialty occupation requires 'theoretical and practical application of a body of highly specialized knowledge' and a bachelor's degree or its equivalent. Status is valid for three years and renewable for three more. 'Current law generally limits the number of individuals who are annually provided H-1B status to 65,000'. That cap 'is the same now as when it was established in 1990', although Congress raised it temporarily in the late 1990s and early 2000s (S0105, IF12912, p. 1).

The safeguard for American workers was an attestation. Before petitioning, the employer files a labour condition application with the Department of Labor, attesting that it will pay 'the greater of the actual wages paid to similar employees or the prevailing wages for that occupation' and that working conditions of other employees will not be harmed. The CRS adds a sentence that should be in the book: 'The LCA is a statement of intent rather than a documentation of actions taken' (S0105, IF12912, p. 1).

The cap began to bind within seven years. The CRS's 2007 history records that the 65,000 limit 'was reached for the first time prior to the end of FY1997, with visa numbers running out by September 1997', and the FY1998 limit was reached in May 1998 (S0105, RL30498).
- **1998.** Congress raised the ceiling to 115,000 for fiscal 1999 and 2000, through the American Competitiveness and Workforce Improvement Act, but 'that annual ceiling of 115,000 visas was reached months before FY1999 and FY2000 ended'. The INS 'admitted in autumn 1999 that thousands of H-1B visas beyond the 115,000 ceiling were approved in FY1999'.
- **2000.** The next Congress raised the ceiling 'to 195,000 for three years'.
- **FY2005.** When it fell back to 65,000, 'that limit was reached on the first day'.
- **FY2008.** The cap was reached in the first two days of filing, 2-3 April 2007, with about 119,193 petitions (S0105, RL30498).

Since the mid-2000s a lottery has allocated the visas (S0105, IF12912, p. 2). One date in the CRS text (the FY1999 ceiling 'reached in June 2002') is a typographical error and must not be quoted.

The fees in 2013 show how Congress tried to tax the programme. Base fee $325; a $500 fraud fee; $1,500 under the 1998 Act 'to fund the training of United States citizens'; and $2,000 for firms with more than 50 US employees of whom more than half held H-1B or L-1 status (S0268, para II.B.1). Firms with at least 15% of their workforce on H-1Bs were classed as 'H-1B dependent' and had to attest that they had tried to recruit Americans (S0105, RL30498).

The H-1B replaced an earlier, looser route. In 1993, write Khanna and Morales, 'the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work' (S0254, p. 5). Chapter 9 argues this pushed work offshore. The B-1 business visitor visa did not disappear from the industry, as section 3.6 shows.

### 3.2 How Indian the visa became

The share of the visa going to Indians rose from about half to nearly three-quarters. The measures differ and must be named each time (new dispute ll):
- **1999:** 47% of H-1B visas, 55,047 in number (Lowell 2000, via S0255, p. 12).
- **October 1999 to February 2000:** 48.5% of approvals with a known birth country were India-born (INS, via S0104; original not read).
- **FY2005:** 'India was the leading country of origin for newly arriving H-1B workers, comprising almost half (49.0%) of all of the new arrivals in FY2005', with China a distant second at 9.16% (S0105, RL30498). The same CRS report repeats an undated DHS estimate that 'nearly three-fourths of all of the systems analysts and programmers are from India'.
- **2014:** about 70% (USCIS, via S0254, p. 11). China received only 8% in 2012 (S0254, p. 1 n. 2).
- **FY2023:** 72% India-born and 12% China-born; 71% male; median age 33; 65% in computer-related occupations; median pay $118,000 (S0105, IF12912, p. 2).

Who sponsored them is a separate question, and the answer is more interesting than the myth. Khanna and Morales tabulate USCIS approvals by firm for 2012 and 2013 (S0254, Table 1):
- Infosys 5,600 and 6,298;
- Tata Consultancy Services 7,469 and 6,258;
- Cognizant 9,281 and 5,186;
- Accenture 4,037 and 3,346;
- Wipro 4,304 and 2,644;
- HCL 2,070 and 1,766;
- IBM India 1,846 and 1,624;
- Mahindra Satyam 1,963 and 1,589;
- Larsen & Toubro Infotech 1,832 and 1,580;
- Deloitte 1,668 and 1,491;
- iGate (Patni) 1,260 and 1,157.

'10 out of the top 11 H-1B firms have Indians as their primary employment base' (S0254, p. 11). Three of those ten were headquartered in the United States or elsewhere: Cognizant, Accenture and IBM. Deloitte was the only top sponsor whose workforce was mainly American.

**Myth M11 ('H-1B visas mostly go to Indian outsourcing firms').** Two claims are run together. By nationality, the visa went overwhelmingly to Indians from the late 1990s. By employer, the largest sponsors around 2012-13 were firms built on the offshore services model, Indian- and American-owned alike. Whether they took 'most' of all approvals cannot be tested without USCIS totals for the same years (S0159). Verdict stays MIXED, with the ownership point added.

### 3.3 What the visa did at home

The most useful single argument for this book is economic. Khanna and Morales argue that 'the H-1B program induced Indians to switch to computer science (CS) occupations, increasing the CS workforce in India by 4.9% in 2010' (S0254, abstract). The mechanism runs through the cap. Students and workers chose computing because the visa offered a route to American wages. Those 'who could not join the US workforce, due to the H-1B cap, remained in India, enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector'. Those who returned after their visas expired brought 'acquired human capital and technology' (S0254, p. 1). As they put it, those unable to get a visa 'or return to India after their visa expires are a ready supply of labor for firms in India' (S0254, p. 11). The 4.9% is a model counterfactual, not a count, and the drafting must say so. The direction of the argument fits the evidence of Chapter 12. The American prospect helped fill the engineering colleges, and the colleges filled the Indian firms.

The same paper gives a snapshot of the workforce this produced, from NASSCOM data for 2002: a 'median age of 26.5 years', with '58% of them' having 'less than three years of experience'. A NASSCOM-Hewitt survey found that '88% of firms visited engineering colleges to recruit, and 47% recruited only there'. Meanwhile engineering PhDs fell from about 675 in 1987 to 375 in 1995 (S0254, p. 11, citing Arora and Athreye 2002). The visa and the factory drew the best students toward early, well-paid industry jobs and away from research.

### 3.4 Silicon Valley's Indians

The Indian engineers of Silicon Valley were mostly a product of the period before the H-1B. Saxenian's analysis of the 1990 census found 28,520 Indians in the region's workforce, 98% of them immigrants. Of Indians in high-technology industries, 87% had arrived after 1970 and 60% after 1980 (S0097, p. 13). Most had come for graduate study. National Science Foundation data cited by Saxenian show that 79% of Indian science and engineering doctoral recipients of 1990-91 were still in the United States in 1995 (S0097, p. 3 n. 3). Another NSF figure shows 'over 95%' of Indian and Chinese doctorate holders planned to stay (S0256, n. 1). By 2000, 53% of Silicon Valley's scientists and engineers were foreign-born, about 20,000 of them Indian (S0256, p. 2).

Saxenian's count of Indian entrepreneurship is the origin of a much-repeated figure (M12). Using a Dun & Bradstreet database of 11,443 technology firms founded in Silicon Valley between 1980 and 1998, she identified 'immigrant-run businesses' as 'all of the companies with chief executive officers (CEOs) with Chinese and Indian surnames' (S0097, p. 7). She was explicit that this was a proxy: 'we are using immigrant-run businesses as a proxy for immigrant-founded businesses'. It undercounted founders who had hired non-Asian chief executives, which her interviews suggested was common because venture capital 'has often been tied to the requirement that non-Asian senior executives be hired' (S0097, pp. 8, 23). On this measure, 774 firms (7%) were run by Indians and 2,001 (17%) by Chinese. The Indian-run firms had $3.6bn in sales and 16,598 jobs in 1998 (S0097, p. 23). The Indian share of new start-ups rose from 3% in 1980-84 to 9% in 1995-98 (S0097, p. 24). The later and larger figure of 15.5% (Wadhwa et al. 2007) counts any immigrant founder over a different period (S0098, not read). M12 stays MIXED, and the drafting must give the measure with the number.

Why did they start firms? One quoted answer, recorded by the *Los Angeles Times* in 1996 and reproduced by Saxenian: 'Why do you think there are so many Indian entrepreneurs in Silicon Valley? Because they know that sooner or later they will be held back' (Prabhat Andleigh, via S0097, p. 43). Indian engineers 'complained about a glass ceiling in the region's established companies, and responded by starting their own businesses' (S0097, p. 43).

The networks came next. The Silicon Valley Indian Professionals Association was founded by Prakash Chandra, whom Saxenian describes as 'a typical Silicon Valley engineer'. He had worked at AMD from 1984, then Intel, MIPS and Cadence, before returning to India in 1992 to work for Wellfleet, later Bay Networks (S0097, p. 43 n. 15). The Indus Entrepreneurs (TiE) began the same year, among 'an older generation of Indian immigrants'. Its founders included Suhas Patil of Cirrus Logic, Prabhu Goel of Gateway Design Automation and Kanwal Rekhi of Excelan (S0097, pp. 44-45).

Saxenian's account of its origin is a scene in itself. 'This core group came together in response to a visit from India's Secretary of Electronics to Silicon Valley in 1992, but when the minister's flight was delayed they began to informally share complaints about the difficulties of running a business' (S0097, p. 45). Patil, quoted in the *San Jose Mercury News* in 1994: 'as individuals we are brilliant, but collectively we amounted to nothing' (S0097, p. 45). An early member, Satish Gupta: 'When some of us started our businesses we had nobody we could turn to for help' (S0097, p. 45). The first annual conference, in 1994, 'attracted over 500 people'; that of 1997 drew more than 800. There were forty charter members by invitation, paying $1,000 a year. The founders chose 'Indus (rather than Indian)' to include other South Asians (S0097, p. 46). This fits the register's date of December 1992 for the founding and 1994 for the first conference (S0116; new dispute kk, resolved as compatible).

### 3.5 Drain, then circulation

'Brain circulation' is Saxenian's phrase, and it is often read back into the 1990s. Her own evidence for India in that decade points the other way. 'few Indian engineers choose to live and work permanently in India. Unlike the Taiwanese immigrants who have increasingly returned home ... Indian engineers - if they return at all - typically do so on a temporary basis' (S0097, p. 63). 'Software companies in Bangalore report turnovers of 20-30 percent per year, primarily because so many workers jump at the first opportunity to emigrate' and 'the "brain drain" of skilled workers to the United States continued unabated throughout the 1990s' (S0097, p. 65). Writing in 2005 she repeated: 'few U.S.-educated emigrants returned to either India or China during the 1980s and 1990s' (S0256, p. 6). It was the technology recession of 2001 that 'triggered an upsurge in cross-regional entrepreneurship' (S0256, p. 6).

The exception was the returnee who set up an American company's Indian operation. Radha Basu left 'her conservative South Indian family' for graduate study at the University of Southern California in the early 1970s and joined Hewlett-Packard. In the mid-1980s she spent four years setting up HP's software centre in Bangalore. It employed 400 people when she went back to California in 1989 (S0097, pp. 62-63). The first office 'consisted of a telex machine on her dining room table'. For five years, because customs officials could not accept that software could be exported over a satellite link, she had to copy HP's data to tapes and ship them physically so that the exports could be recorded (S0097, p. 64 and n. 19, from an interview on 1 October 1997). When officials spoke of a 'single window of clearance', she would ask 'where is the window?' (S0097, p. 64). Saxenian's assessment was that Silicon Valley's Indians played 'an important, but largely arm's-length, role' in the 1990s. Wages for programmers were 'ten times lower in India', and 'the fully loaded cost of an engineer is 35-40 percent what it is in the United States' (S0097, p. 66).

The Chennai ethnography adds a twist from the 2000s. By 2005 offshoring had reduced long-term migration: 'the very logic of "offshoring" means that flows of people may diminish as the work travels to the site of labor, instead of the reverse' (S0118, p. 190). Most young engineers in that study went abroad for months, not years (S0118, p. 190). The H-1B became, for many, a stint rather than a one-way ticket.

### 3.6 The B-1 channel: the Infosys settlement

The clearest documentary evidence on how visas were actually used comes from a legal settlement. On 30 October 2013 Infosys filed with the SEC a Settlement Agreement with the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Texas, the State Department and the Department of Homeland Security. Under it, 'Infosys agrees to pay a full settlement amount of $34,000,000' (S0268).

The agreement separates what both sides accepted from what the government alleged. Both sides accepted that 'Infosys failed to maintain accurate I-9 records for many of its foreign nationals in the United States in 2010 and 2011', including 'a widespread failure to update and re-verify the employment authorization status of a substantial percentage of its foreign national employees' (S0268, para II.D).

The government alleged more, and Infosys denied it (S0268, paras II.E and II.G). The allegation was that Infosys 'knowingly and unlawfully used B-1 visa holders to perform skilled labor in order to fill positions in the United States for employment that would otherwise be performed by United States citizens or require legitimate H-1B visa holders'. Its purposes were said to include 'increasing profits, minimizing costs of securing visas, increasing flexibility of employee movement'. The examples cited:
- **Invitation letters** for B-1 travellers described the purpose as 'meetings' or 'discussions' when the work included 'coding and programming'.
- **A 'Do's and Don'ts' memorandum** told travellers: 'Do not mention activities like implementation, design & testing, consulting, etc., which sound like work'; 'do not use words like, [sic] work, activity, etc., in the invitation letter'; and 'Please do not mention anything about contract rates.'
- **Stated destinations.** Workers were allegedly told to give consular officers the destination on the labour condition application when they were going elsewhere, for example Seattle instead of Bentonville, Arkansas.
- **A contract conversion.** On 29 September 2009, according to the government, emails directed staff to convert a 'time and materials contract', which disclosed the names and billing rates of all individuals, into a 'fixed price contract', which disclosed only a total, in order to conceal B-1 holders doing skilled work.

Infosys's reply, in the same document, was that its B-1 use 'was for legitimate business purposes and not in any way to circumvent the requirements of the H-1B program'. It said the letters were accurate, that the memorandum 'was not intended to deceive' and was withdrawn in 2011, and that contract changes were 'made to comply with internal Company guidelines'. It 'denies any allegations of civil or criminal wrongdoing and/or liability' (S0268, paras II.G-I).

The drafting must keep this structure: agreed facts as facts; allegations as allegations, with the denial alongside, every time. Two points are safe to make. First, the B-1 route that Khanna and Morales describe as replaced in 1993 was still in use, legitimately or not, two decades later. Second, the government's allegation about the 2009 contract shows the two pricing forms doing different work. A time-and-materials contract names the people and their rates. A fixed-price contract names only the price. That difference is at the centre of the book's argument about who captures productivity gains (Chapters 9, 11 and the AI chapters).

### 3.7 The other side of the counter

The American worker displaced by a visa holder is part of this story and must not be left to politicians' summaries. Leo Perrero testified to the Senate Judiciary Committee on 25 February 2016 (S0269). He had worked for Disney in Orlando 'in some capacity for well over ten years', on the point-of-sale system through which visitors' payments flowed. In the 2014 holiday season he and about two dozen colleagues were called to a meeting. An executive told them: 'All of you in this room will be losing your jobs in the next 90 days. Your jobs have been given over to a foreign workforce.' Severance depended on training their replacements. Only '4 people would be directly rehired by Disney of the hundreds that were lost'. His last day was 31 January 2015.

He describes the handover in three phases. In the first, 'Everything was recorded with audio and video. Then the very next day the recordings were put down on paper and read right back to us.' In the second, the incumbents worked 'side by side with our replacements'. In the third, they watched 'the foreign workers completely take over our jobs' (S0269). Of taking the severance he said: 'Sadly, I choose the money over America.'

Three cautions apply. His is one witness's account, given in support of reform. He does not name the contractor or the replacements' nationality, so the book must not name them from this source. And the episode falls at the end of the chapter's period. With those cautions, it is valuable for two reasons. It is the only first-person account in hand from a worker on the receiving end of the model. And its first phase is a documented instance of 'knowledge transfer': tacit work recorded, written down and handed over so that someone cheaper can do it. That is the same codification on which, a decade later, AI tools are trained, and the AI chapters can point back to it.

### 3.8 Families

Migration also reached into Indian homes. In Chennai, Fuller and Narasimhan heard the city described as 'full of lonely old folk whose sons and daughters send them dollars'. Others who visited their children ended up as 'bored, exploited childminders marooned in American suburbia under "house arrest", to quote one elderly grandfather' (S0117, p. 146). Their informants felt that overseas migration 'damages the proper reciprocal relationship between parents and children in a way that migration within India does not' (S0117, p. 146). Ravi (pseud.) cut short a stay in England to return to his elderly father. Balaram (pseud.) took his family to Australia in 1995 and came back in 2003 because the work in Chennai had become 'far more interesting and challenging' (S0117, pp. 128-129).
