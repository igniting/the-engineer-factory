# Ch. 3 source notes (working file)

## S0001 Rajaraman, History of Computing in India (2012), pp. 18-28 (full text read 2026-10-12)

### Before the DoE: IBM, ICL and foreign exchange (pp. 18-19)
- No government computer policy beyond the 1958 Scientific Policy Resolution; computers controlled by the Directorate General of Technical Development and the Department of Defence Supplies. Five IITs and 13 Regional Engineering Colleges (one per state) set up under the Plans.
- 'Foreign companies were permitted to manufacture and sell computers provided foreign exchange was not required. Computers were not considered a priority area that deserved foreign exchange outflow.' (p. 18)
- IBM and BTM/ICL already sold unit-record equipment (key punches, sorters, tabulators) to firms, Indian Railways, statistics departments.
- IBM sold a 1401 to ESSO in 1961 as a showpiece. IBM's scheme (c. 1965): make 029 key punches with 80% local components for export, and use 80% of the foreign exchange earned to import used 1401s at low inter-company prices; dismantled, refurbished, painted and rented in India from a Bombay factory; also made punch cards.
- By 1970 the 1401 was India's most popular computer, about 80 installations. Annual rental of a high-end 1401 c. Rs 850,000 (c. $190,000). IBM 'was in fact accused of profiteering'; IBM said rental included maintenance and a systems engineer who designed software and helped programme: 'selling a computing service rather than just renting a computer'.
- Rajaraman: IBM's service excellent; 'A whole generation of maintenance engineers and programmers were trained and nurtured by IBM. In short IBM set a bench mark for professional operations in India' (p. 19). [Profession: IBM as India's first trainer of programmers.]
- ICL: 48 ICL 1901A machines assembled by BEL 1965-75. Indian Railways had over a dozen 1401s; most Tata companies and textile firms had them; TCS's first computer was a 1401; ISI also installed one. IBM ran data centres in big cities for smaller firms.

### Bhabha Committee to DoE (pp. 20-22)
- 1962 war showed electronics' importance for defence. 1963 committee under Bhabha; report ready by 1966; Bhabha killed in air crash before formal submission. Main recommendations: a Department of Electronics and an Electronics Commission with wide financial and executive powers.
- Computer subcommittee chaired by R. Narasimhan projected demand for ten years: one very large imported computer for a national facility; three to five medium-large imported machines as Regional Computer Centres run like utilities; all smaller computers made locally with local components; about 500 a year for ten years.
- 1970 National Electronics Conference at TIFR debated the report. Rajaraman's own contribution: 'We do not have a large enough internal market of sufficient volume to justify the chronological development: components -> circuits -> systems. We should go backwards and design systems first and import the needed components... Foreign exchange can also be earned by software export.' (S0034 as cited: S0024, p. 395; via S0001, p. 21). 'However, my comment was not taken seriously.'
- Vikram Sarabhai, chairing: computing is 'far more fundamental, of wider significance than any other field of electronics' and of 'particular relevance to our developing country where resources are scarce'; international companies have a contribution 'on honourable commercially rewarding terms, on ground rules which are acceptable to India... not ... at the cost of a major national effort' (via S0001, p. 22).
- ECIL started at Hyderabad 1967 under the Department of Atomic Energy to commercialise BARC electronics; DoE funded a computer division.

### Political economy (pp. 22-23)
- Congress governed 1947-1977; banks nationalised 1969; perpetual trade deficit; rupee not convertible: 'obtaining foreign exchange for import of equipment required elaborate justification' (p. 22).
- **Automation committee:** set up 1969 by the Ministry of Labour, Employment and Rehabilitation under R. Venkataraman (Planning Commission member); 'later chaired by V.M. Dandekar' and known as the Dandekar Committee on automation; in 1972 prescribed strict controls on computers in industry and government; made prior agreement with labour mandatory; constrained banks, insurance companies, private firms (p. 22). [RESOLVES dispute (f): both are right.]
- Reason for negative attitude: IBM 1401s used mainly for payroll, accounting, stores; vocal white-collar workers saw them as labour-saving, unlike older unit-record machines that needed many operators (pp. 22-23).
- PAC report of 1975: efficiency gains only in the long run; with large-scale unemployment, labour-saving computers 'may not be desirable or may even be detrimental'; urged accounting for the social cost of computerisation (p. 23; S0022).
- 1970: import licensing transferred to DoE as 'single window clearance'; rules based on essentiality, availability of Indian equivalents, foreign exchange needed. 1973 OPEC oil shock worsened forex. Delays of one to three years for import clearance unless UNDP or a foreign government paid; hurt software exporters, industry, labs, universities (p. 23).
- 1971 war: Nixon favoured Pakistan; embargos on US electronics and computers. 1974 nuclear test: embargo on advanced electronic equipment incl. high-end computers and scientific software (p. 23).

### Government policy and ECIL (pp. 24-26)
- 1970 DoE and Electronics Commission established; M.G.K. Menon (TIFR director) secretary and ex-officio EC chairman; relied on Narasimhan's report (cited as Bhabha Committee report of 1968): 'attaining self-sufficiency in systems engineering and fabrication is of fundamental importance from the point of view of the defence and security of our country' (p. 24).
- Private firms (calculator makers) wanted to make computers; DoE delayed a minicomputer policy because of forex for imported components and to protect ECIL. P.V.S. Rao's minicomputer panel said minis could be made without foreign collaboration; recommended standardisation, which private firms rejected. Janata government (1977) replaced Menon; minicomputer policy 1978 (p. 24).
- ECIL computer division under S. Srikantan (designer of BARC's TDC-12, 12-bit, PDP-8-like). No commercial computer to develop software on; software outsourced to TIFR, IIT Kanpur, IIM Ahmedabad; OS, assembler, BASIC, FORTRAN built. TDC-312 (1974), TDC-316 (1975, 16-bit, ECOBOL), Micro 78 (1978). Sold 1971-78: 21 TDC-12, 32 TDC-312, 35 TDC-316, 10 Micro 78 = 98; plus 75 ruggedised TDC-316s for defence (p. 25).
- Table 1 typical costs: TDC-12 Rs 600,000; TDC-312 Rs 1,000,000; TDC-316 Rs 3,000,000 (p. 25).
- ECIL 'worked more like a cottage industry'; poor sales; buyers mostly atomic energy, government, universities 'constrained to buy'; 'Only 4 machines were sold to the private sector'; overpriced, short of software. But ECIL trained 'over a thousand engineers in designing systems and developing systems software', many of whom later led groups in private firms (p. 26). [Key for profession: the state as trainer.]
- ADGES air defence: system design and software by TIFR under P.V.S. Rao; three TDC-316s per system; displays co-developed with Tata Electric; deployed 1969-1984; Rs 30 million each; 25 systems for the Air Force; 'In the published literature this important contribution of ECIL is not mentioned' (p. 26).
- Other ECIL uses where export controls blocked imports: atomic energy data acquisition, satellite launch monitoring, telemetry, gas-turbine testing, steel rolling-mill data logging. DoE grants/loans to ECIL Rs 37.44 million (S0025, p. 30).

### Other DoE initiatives (pp. 27-28)
- DoE seen as regulator protecting ECIL; 'became very unpopular' for delaying imports by universities, labs and industry; 'no private Indian company was allowed to manufacture computers' (p. 27).
- NCSDCT 1972 (Rs 2.85m DoE, $2m UNDP); trained many scientists; sent many abroad; renamed NCST 1983.
- NIC 1975 (Rs 31.72m DoE, $4.4m UNDP); central government data processing; later VSAT network.
- AREN army radio network (TIFR, LRDE, Army signals; M.V. Pitke's switch later used for rural telephone exchanges).
- **Software export:** 'Recognizing the potential of software export DoE allowed import of computers exclusively meant for software export. The first policy paper on this was published in 1974.' TCS imported a Burroughs B6700, converted other makers' software to Burroughs; scouted work in Iran, Switzerland, South Africa, Canada, USA; Tata-Burroughs manufacturing company 1975, closed after four years (p. 28). [Ch. 4]
- Regional Computer Centres: Jadavpur 1977 (Burroughs 6738, free student access), Pune, Chandigarh.
- CMC 1976 (Rs 53 million) to maintain imported and post-IBM machines.
- Semiconductor committee 1971, report 1973, no action until 1978; investment sub-critical (p. 28).

## S0020 Government of India, Ministry of Labour and Rehabilitation, *Report of the Committee on Automation* (Delhi: Manager of Publications, 1972), 168 pp. Full text (OCR) read 2026-10-12 from GIPE DSpace PDF. Page numbers are the report's printed pages; OCR errors corrected in paraphrase only.

- Signed 18 May 1972. Chairman Prof. V.M. Dandekar; members G. Ramanujam, Ram Desai, Satish Loomba, B.D. Somani, Babubhai M. Chinai, Naval H. Tata, Prof. V.R. Rao, Brig. B.J. Shahaney, Dr B.S. Garud; secretary M.A.M. Rao (front matter).
- Ch. I (pp. 1-8): 'few subjects have sparked off such a controversy as automation' (p. 1). Earlier 'rationalisation' dispute: 1957 Model Agreement (15th Indian Labour Conference) - no retrenchment or loss of earnings, equitable sharing of gains, prior consultation with unions (pp. 1-2).
- 1960s: automation in manufacturing (textiles, petroleum, steel, etc.) caused little opposition, but computers 'particularly for office work, evoked serious misgivings among the employees' (p. 2).
- 'The issue attracted all India attention because of the unilateral decision of the Life Insurance Corporation (LIC) management in 196[5] to introduce a computer.' The All India Insurance Employees' Association started an agitation; central trade unions took it up. Unions saw automation 'as a painful luxury, and as a labour saving device which has no place in a developing economy marked by labour surpluses and scarce capital resources' (p. 2) [year digit garbled in OCR; para 3.48 confirms opposition 'since 1965' after a big public-sector undertaking introduced a computer without consulting workers].
- Indian Labour Conference 24th session, July 1966: regulate pace of change; apply Model Agreement (p. 3). Standing Labour Committee 26th session, May 1967: no conclusion; clerical automation 'evoked sharp controversies'; labour and some state labour ministers opposed (pp. 3-4).
- Agitation continued in LIC, oil companies, railways; union conventions against automation in 1966 and 1967 demanded an immediate ban (p. 4).
- Screening: imports needing foreign exchange required Labour Ministry clearance, but most computers were locally assembled (IBM 1401s) and escaped screening: 1967-71, only five proposals referred though 94 computers were introduced (p. 4). Computers rose from 30 in 1965 to 88 in 1968 (p. 4).
- Special SLC meeting 18 July 1968: workers wanted policy 'against automation' with exceptions and a freeze pending guidelines; employers said computers were necessary and created more jobs economy-wide; inconclusive; government continued 'selective' automation (pp. 5-6).
- Committee set up by Resolution No. 31/7/68 LRIV dated 19 July 1969 with R. Venkataraman (Member, Planning Commission) as chairman; first meeting 17 Sept 1969; Venkataraman resigned 22 March 1971; Dandekar (Director, Gokhale Institute, Poona) appointed chairman 24 July 1971 (p. 7). **Resolves dispute (f).**
- Table I (pp. 24-25): 140 computers in India by August 1971 (Government departments 10, public undertakings 39, private undertakings 55, R&D 36). By year: 1961: 2; 1962: 2; 1963: 3; 1964: 9; 1965: 14; 1966: 16; 1967: 22; 1968: 20; 1969: 19; 1970: 10; 1971 (partial): 23. [Sums to 140.] Cities: Bombay 35, Delhi 20, Calcutta 14, Bangalore 11, Madras 7, Hyderabad 6, Jamshedpur 5, Poona and Kanpur 4 each, Ahmedabad 3. Maharashtra 39 (27.9%) (p. 25).
- Uses: Railways (wagon movements, accounting, payroll); LIC (policy-holder records); State Bank of India reconciling some 70,000 inter-branch transactions a day; Delhi Cloth Mills and Calico Mills linear programming for cotton blending; TELCO production control; ACC PERT (p. 30). Mostly routine payroll, billing, invoicing (p. 31).
- IBM (para 2.54, p. c. 33): commercial outlet in India since 1950, manufacturing from 1963; punch cards in Bangalore; 029 key punches, unit-record equipment and reconditioned 1401s at Kurla, Bombay; key punches exported to South-East Asia, earning foreign exchange used to import disassembled 1401s; licensed to recondition 68 1401s (22 in 1968, 22 in 1969, 24 in 1970) and make up to 4,000 key punches; applied to recondition 360s instead for sale in rupees. ICL (ICIM) formed 1963, card equipment at Poona, same export-for-import model.
- Para 3.16 (pp. 43-44): office computers reduce jobs or potential jobs; new jobs fewer than displaced, so net local effect adverse; wider effects uncertain.
- Para 3.48 (p. 63): until 1965 little opposition; serious opposition since 1965 after a big public-sector undertaking introduced a computer without consulting workers; unions opposed even with no-retrenchment guarantees, 'on grounds of loss of potential employment'.
- Appendix XV (p. A69): Establishment A (unnamed): branch clearance and government accounts staff fell from 380 (Nov 1966) to 304 (Jan 1970) while volume rose; old system would have needed 516 for the 1970 volume. [A worked example of 'potential employment' loss.]
- **Recommendations (Ch. IV, pp. 67-74):** computers for education, research, science, government statistics and defence permitted with protection for existing staff (4.1); for commercial and industrial firms, public or private, case-by-case scrutiny by experts (4.2); computers often used for routine work or under-utilised (4.3); prefer shared computer service centres, 'in the nature of a public utility', ideally in the public sector (4.4-4.5); 'all use of computers by commercial and industrial establishments ... should be subject to a prior agreement with the workers' (4.7); Justification Report listing jobs, benefits, employment effects and safeguards (4.11-4.13); two experts chosen jointly (4.14); negotiation; disputes to a National Bipartite Machinery whose unanimous recommendations bind (4.17); same procedure for expansions and replacements (4.19). Conclusion: approach 'positive'; meet needs 'as far as possible from indigenous sources', saving foreign exchange and creating employment; DoE 'seized of the matter of ensuring self-reliance' (4.20, pp. 73-74).
