# Ch. 2 source notes (working file)

## S0003 Bassett, 'Aligning India in the Cold War Era', Technology and Culture 50(4), Oct 2009, 783-810 (full text read 2026-10-12)

p. 783 (opening scene)
- July 1963: chartered DC-7 lands at a military airbase in Kanpur carrying an IBM 1620 for IIT Kanpur, an institution supported by a nine-university US consortium; 'a group of young Indian men muscled the machine into place'. Harry Huskey (consultant on ENIAC, worked with Turing, past president of the US computing society, head of Berkeley's computer centre) greets it; with two Princeton experts gets it running; informal course for 25 'specially selected' first-year students who stayed long into the night programming. Sources in n. 1: Forman Acton letter (2005); Huskey autobiography (S0032); MIT-KIAP records (S0053); IIT Kanpur Annual Report 1963-64, pp. 88-89.
- Fig. 1 photo of delivery, from Norman C. Dahl, 'Revolution on the Ganges', Tech Engineering News, April 1967. Fig. 2 students at the 1620 c. 1964 with IBM 'Think' sign (IIT Kanpur 1965 Convocation publication).

p. 786 (argument)
- 'A close look at the Kanpur computer center reveals an American-oriented Indian technical elite which played a central role in the establishment of computing education in India, and in the development of the Indian IT industry.' Elite 'aligned technically rather than politically'. Unexpected cost: 'Indian students used an American-oriented system of education in India as an express path to the American educational system' (p. 786).

p. 787
- 1944: A.V. Hill, British Nobel laureate advising the Government of India, noted absence of an MIT-type institution; recommended one or two technical institutes 'of the highest possible standing'.
- 1945: Sarker Committee (N.R. Sarker) appointed; included two Indians with MIT doctorates; proposed four regional institutions that 'would train students to the standard of MIT'. [Relevant to M14: the founding blueprint invoked MIT as the standard.]
- First IIT at Kharagpur 1951 (support from US, UK, USSR, UNESCO); then national patrons: USSR for Bombay (1958), West Germany for Madras (1959), US for Kanpur (1960), UK for Delhi (1963, originally Delhi College of Engineering).
- n. 8: 'brain drain' term originated with British scientists moving to the US; Churchill 1949 at MIT lamented Britain had nothing of 'comparable stature'.

p. 788
- Before WWII, 1,000-2,000 Indian students in Britain each year; maximum in US 208. 1944: British Indian government began paying for Indian students in US universities; independent India continued. 1949-50: 1,680 Indian students in US universities vs just under 700 in UK (Fig. 4, 1947-1960 series from Open Doors etc.).
- 1949 Indian government survey: most sponsored students in US studied engineering or agriculture.

p. 789
- By 1958 informal US-India agreement on aid for Kanpur; India started the institute in 1960 without US help because of delays.
- P.K. Kelkar: first director; electrical engineer, doctorate 1936 Liverpool; taught at VJTI Bombay; chief planning officer for IIT Bombay working with Soviet sponsors.

p. 790
- Kelkar's unpublished memoir: epiphany reading the proceedings of the 1957 American engineering deans' conference on reorienting curricula around engineering science; drawn to a 'complex and challenging future'. Bassett: US engineering-science triumph driven by desire to share military research funding; its rhetoric broad enough to attract Kelkar.
- January 1961: MIT team of three professors visits; recommends support based 'almost exclusively' on partnership with Kelkar; consortium informally anchored by MIT. 1961: India, US and nine universities agree to establish KIAP.
- Sources on Kelkar: S. Ranganathan obituary, Current Science, 10 Feb 1991, 185-86; IIT Kanpur Alumni Association Newsletter Jan 1991; Arawind Parasnis, 'IITK, Kelkar and I: Reminiscences of a Bygone Era' (undated; Parasnis one of the first professors).

p. 790 n. 12
- Nine KIAP universities: MIT, Caltech, Carnegie Institute of Technology, Case Institute of Technology, Princeton, UC Berkeley, Michigan, Ohio State, Purdue. Stanford asked and declined, 'a profound irony' given its later benefit from IIT Kanpur graduates. MIT team report: Buechner, Dahl and Smullin, 'An Opportunity for United States Participation in Indian Technical Education', 3 March 1961 (MIT-KIAP box 5).

p. 791
- A computer was 'fundamental' to Kelkar's plans; before KIAP he planned to buy an IBM computer with American funds and have Delhi University physicists train faculty. D.S. Kothari to Kelkar, 27 Dec 1960, on 'Purchase of Digital Computer' (Board of Governors, 5 March 1961).
- The 1957 deans' conference had two themes: engineering sciences and the computer. S.N. Alexander called the computer 'the embodiment of a new attitude toward the utilization of ideas'.
- Late 1950s: Kelkar with F.C. Kohli (MIT-trained control engineer at Tata Electric) set up a master's in control engineering at VJTI Bombay. Gordon Brown (MIT dean, Kohli's teacher) defined the modern engineer as one who 'builds mechanisms to program, to control or to compute with lightning-like speed'. [Kohli link to Ch. 4.]
- Five undergraduate branches (electrical, mechanical, metallurgical, chemical, civil), often American textbooks. 1620 delivery scheduled for earliest possible date, as soon as a building existed; IIT Kanpur first taught in borrowed space at an existing technological institute.

p. 792
- 1620 arrived July 1963; Huskey with Forman Acton and Irving Rabinowitz (Princeton) had it running for a demonstration to the Board of Governors in late August [reconciles S0001's 'installed August 1963']. US visiting faculty for about a year, then Gio Wiederhold (Berkeley) for another year.
- Kelkar hired mostly Indians with US doctorates, often working in the US. H.K. Kesavan (MS Illinois, PhD Michigan State, professor at Waterloo) arrived Jan 1964 to head EE; first head of computer centre May 1964. V. Rajaraman arrived 1963 (MS MIT, PhD Wisconsin; analog computation). H.N. Mahabala and T.R. Viswanathan (PhDs Saskatchewan, ex-Waterloo).
- n. 15: IBM 1620 cost roughly $320,000 before a 60 percent educational discount.

p. 793
- Equipment open to students around the clock by April 1964; from spring 1964 all students trained in computing; undergraduates worked at the centre as programmers. 'some Kanpur students' lives were transformed by their early encounters with the computer' (p. 793).
- By 1965 plans for EE master's with computer-science concentration; 1969 catalogue full set of UG and PG computer courses incl. software engineering. Teaching load largely Rajaraman and Mahabala, who learned computing 'on the fly, without formal training'; later spent time at Berkeley (Rajaraman) and MIT (Mahabala) under KIAP. Focus teaching; Rajaraman's 1969 programming textbook; later FORTRAN and COBOL books.
- n. 18, students who worked at the centre (Bassett interviewed them): Ravi Sethi (later senior manager, Bell Labs communications sciences); Jayadev Misra (later head of EE, UT Austin); Raj Kanodia (member of the group that connected MIT to the ARPANET in early 1970s); Hari Sahasrabuddhe (interviewed). [Rank-and-file voices exist only as Bassett's paraphrase of his interviews; look for their own published recollections.]

p. 794
- 1620 fully used within a year; IBM 7044 arrived 1966, one of the most powerful computers in India. n. 21: list price $3 million; IBM gave the 60 percent educational discount 'and allowed it to pay in rupees - a major concession to a country for which foreign exchange was precious' (Spark, 31 Oct 1965). [Thesis-relevant: foreign exchange.]
- 7044 obsolescent on arrival; IBM phasing it out by 1969; neither IBM India nor IBM US helped with software. 1970: IIT Kanpur undergraduates produced 'the first compiler written in India'; students had been learning FORTRAN II, 'almost a dead crow on the world market' (student newspaper Spark, April 1970).

p. 795
- Kelkar, 1968 Berkeley talk: 'we soon became aware of the compelling power of the computer. It exercised a unique fascination over the minds of not only post-doctorals, but also freshmen' (S0050 via S0003, p. 795). And: 'there is no better instrument for generating a scientific and quantitative attitude of mind.'
- 'Considering computing at the IITs as a whole during the 1960s, Kanpur was the aberration.' US contribution twice the Soviet one at Bombay or the West German at Madras in equipment and guest faculty; three times the British at Delhi (Sebaly 1972).

p. 796
- IIT Bombay: some faculty with US degrees; students read National Geographic, Saturday Evening Post, Mad, Playboy (dormitory survey, Pragati 1963-64).
- First director Brigadier S.K. Bose (ex-commandant, college of military engineering): cautious; Aug 1961 told TIFR (letter to M.G.K. Menon, 7 Aug 1961) he had declined a Soviet Ural-I as too costly; asked about training staff at TIFR; one computer could serve several Bombay institutions.
- IIT Bombay ordered a computer three years later; Soviet aid via UNESCO; Minsk II delivered 1965, Soviet technicians arrived only July 1967 after two years of funding negotiations; machine damaged in storage; ran from January 1968. Centre head J.R. Isaac (MS Carnegie Tech; worked at IBM Poughkeepsie). Minsk a one-off in India; ran Soviet ACE language, not FORTRAN; programs had to be translated; poor for training general users.

p. 797
- IIT Madras (West German): curriculum focused on practical manual training (blacksmithing, woodworking). First computer 1963: surplus Bell Labs analog machine from a US university; students could use the IBM 1620 at neighbouring Guindy (US aid).
- 1971-72 worst years in Indo-US relations (Nixon-Indira Gandhi; Bangladesh; 1971 Indo-Soviet treaty; USS Enterprise to Bay of Bengal). Kanpur's planned second stage, including a new computer, made impossible; KIAP ended at original 1972 date. n. 32: no US money; Kanpur kept the obsolete 7044 until 1979; replaced by a DEC-10 (IIT Kanpur Annual Report 1978-79, 62-64).

p. 798
- 1971: West Germany agreed to give IIT Madras a computer; Mahabala and two Kanpur associates recruited to run it; Mahabala persuaded Germans to drop German machine and experts and buy IBM. November 1973: IIT Madras computer centre inaugurated with IBM System 370/155, the most powerful computer in India then; Madras supplanted Kanpur as leading centre. ('Computers in India', 1975, S0041, p. 25: IBM 370/155 'the largest and most modern computer system in the country'.)
- 1970: Kelkar becomes director of IIT Bombay; March 1972 curriculum committee report introduces US practice incl. compulsory computing course for all undergraduates; Suhas Sukhatme (MIT-trained; later IIT Bombay director and brain-drain author) put quotations from MIT's William Barton Rogers and Karl Compton at the head of the report. 'The faculty at IIT Bombay had made its own tilt.'
- Bassett: national enthusiasm favoured atomic power, steel, dams; the labour-saving computer 'was a problematic technology in a country that had a surplus of labor, prized self-sufficiency, and put little stock in international trade' (p. 798).

p. 799
- Early 1960s IBM installing computers at labs and large firms for record-keeping; unions called computers 'job eaters' [Ch. 3]. Government looked at labour issues only late 1960s-early 1970s.
- Kelkar 1968: 'there is hardly a computer installation where there is nobody who had been to Kanpur at least once.'
- Acton, Huskey, Rabinowitz lectured at TIFR, IIT Madras, ISI, Roorkee, Bombay University, PRL; special session for fifteen members of the Planning Commission. Ten-day courses for potential computer users: intro to computers, programming, numerical methods, hands-on labs.

p. 800
- By August 1965, 600 people from fifty institutions had taken the ten-day course (IITs, engineering colleges, labs, government agencies, private firms). Faculty taught 'with an almost religious sense of mission', driving ten miles to the railway station to collect participants while their wives cooked. Mahabala later met many professionals introduced to computing on these courses. Source: Mahabala, 'Early Computer Education in India - a Reminiscence', in Banerjee (ed.), *Computer Education in India* (1996), 41-46 (S0016) [another participant memoir to obtain]. Kohli arrived by cycle rickshaw from the station (n. 43).
- 1974 survey: 217 computers in India: 65% IBM, 13% ICL, 11% ECIL. 'an American-oriented program of engineering education in India and an American computing firm did reinforce each other.'
- No IT industry in Kanpur itself. Tata 'established a computing enterprise in 1967'; in 1969 Kohli moved from Tata Electric to head it, by then called TCS [dates differ from S0039 (TCS 1 April 1968) and S0038 (Kohli asked in 1969; director-in-charge 1974)]. Kohli had worked with Kelkar; sat on Kanpur's EE faculty-selection committee; learned computing on Kanpur's 1620; introduced computing to Tata Electric; first Indian on IEEE board (1973). Kohli, *The IT Revolution in India: Selected Speeches and Writings* (New Delhi, 2005) [exists: register].

p. 801
- Mahabala summered at TCS as consultant; Kohli often hired Kanpur's computing master's graduates.
- Narayana Murthy: University of Mysore EE; came to Kanpur for an M.Tech in EE and got 'hooked' on computing; then worked with MIT-trained Jashwant Krishnayya at IIM Ahmedabad on a Ford-funded HP computer (Krishnayya came to Kanpur to recruit); brief stint in France; Krishnayya's RAND-like think tank; then Narendra Patni's Patni Data Systems; 1981 left with six other Patni employees to found Infosys.

p. 802
- Murthy had computing ability from Kanpur but lacked US business connections; Patni had them; American-oriented elites (Patni, Kohli) 'gave a special potency to the computing work of IIT Kanpur graduates'.
- Students 'partially frustrated' the plan for an Indian institute solving Indian problems by remaking Kanpur into 'the Indian MIT'. Foreign students in US: 26,759 (1948) to 100,262 (1966); almost 7,000 Indians in US by 1965-66; MIT 1966: 82 Indian students, second only to Canada (Open Doors; MIT President's Report 1967).

p. 803
- Before 1965 a discriminatory quota limited Indian immigration; 1965, the year of Kanpur's first graduating class, Lyndon Johnson signed reform making permanent residence easier. After 1965 many IIT graduates stayed. IIT Bombay study: slightly over 40 percent of its EE graduates of 1973-1977 ended up abroad, mostly US (Sukhatme and Mahadevan, EPW 18 June 1988, 1285-93 = S0046). n. 50: email list of IIT Kanpur's first batch (1965) shows roughly one-third resident in the US in 2005.

p. 805
- RFC 176 (Network Working Group, 14 June 1971) authors: A. Bhushan (MIT), R. Kanodia (MIT), R. Metcalfe (MIT), J. Postel (UCLA). Abhay Bhushan: first IIT Kanpur graduating class 1965; MIT EE grad school; Project MAC; chaired the working group that defined FTP. Raj Kanodia: Kanpur 1968; Dartmouth then MIT Project MAC.
- 2008 count: 38 faculty in the top ten US CS departments (1993 NRC ranking) had Indian undergraduate degrees.

p. 806 (Table 1)
- IIT Madras 11, IIT Kanpur 10, IIT Delhi 5, IIT Bombay 4, Guindy 2, Jadavpur 1, IISc 1, Loyola 1, BIT 1, Mysore 1, BMS College for Women 1.
- Before 1978 no CS undergraduate major at Kanpur; 1978 B.Tech in CS, 'the first such degree offered at any IIT'. Last of 20 CS seats filled by JEE rank 44: close to half the top forty students in India chose it. (1988: 14 seats filled by rank 38.)

p. 807

- First CS graduates, 1983: 19; of 17 traced in 2007, one deceased, 14 in the US, 1 Czech Republic, 1 India; nine PhDs; five US CS academics (Stanford, Syracuse, Minnesota, Nebraska, UT Arlington); six in the Bay Area; at least three founded companies. Rajeev Motwani (Stanford) an early academic mentor to Larry Page and Sergey Brin; died June 2009.

- n. 56: Kishor Trivedi (Duke) graduated IIT Bombay 1968 with no computing exposure, worked for IBM India on accounting machines, then Illinois PhD and stayed in the US (interview).

## S0031 Fay, 'Report from Kanpur', Engineering and Science (Caltech), June 1966, pp. 11-17 (full text read 2026-10-12)

**Handling note:** a visiting American's account, valuable and vivid but written from inside the aid programme and with condescending passages about Indian staff ('peons', 'seeming stupidity'). Quote facts and his own position; do not adopt his judgements as the book's voice.
- p. 11: IIT Kanpur took its first 100 undergraduates July 1960 and graduated 66 in May 1965; nine universities began sending faculty and 'a great deal of Uncle Sam's money' in 1962; AID contracted the nine early in 1962 through Educational Services Incorporated. Americans lived as AID contract personnel: commissary flour, sugar and Scotch from New Delhi, APO mail, green AID jeeps, duty-free cars, AID housing; paid normal salaries in dollars by home universities.
- p. 12: Program leader Robert Green (Ohio State civil engineer); steering committee chaired by Paul Chenea (Purdue). AID India head John Lewis called KIAP 'the brightest jewel in AID's Indian crown'; Fay: also 'the greatest thorn in its side'. Americans 'neither servants nor masters, but associates'; about thirty at peak (c. 1965), twenty in 1966 and declining. Russians at Bombay and Germans at Madras simply faculty; British at Delhi ran departments themselves.
- p. 13: Americans sat on selection committees without a vote; seldom consulted on promotions; never department heads unless no Indian faculty existed. A first-year student from Delhi said he chose Kanpur over the IIT near home because 'you Americans run this one'; Fay: 'we have influence; we do not have power.' Visiting Berkeley mathematician's failed attempt to modernise the maths department. During the September 1965 war Kanpur was blacked out and Americans in the Punjab ordered to Delhi.
- p. 13-14: Plan: five-year B.Tech in aeronautical, chemical, civil, electrical, mechanical, metallurgical engineering; 1,500 undergraduates in five hostels; common first three years (English, social science, maths, physics, chemistry, engineering science, 'technical arts'); final two years in the branch 'which the boy or the boy's father has chosen' [all-male student body implied]; faculty of about 250; air-conditioned IBM 1620 for instruction and IBM 7044 for research (Phase 1 just arrived); library c. 130,000 volumes; 1,100 acres six miles northwest of Kanpur on the Grand Trunk Road; community of about 4,000 besides students.
- p. 14: IITs created because government could not reform entrenched university engineering departments; 'To expect, then, that American-aided IIT/Kanpur would not grow to resemble an American school would have been silly.' American features: common core, courses with grades, quizzes and midterms, problem-solving as 'a habit of mind', fewer class hours, lots of equipment. Of about 75 faculty at assistant professor or above, all but four under 40; all with PhDs or equivalent: a dozen from UK/Europe, over half from US and Canada, the rest Indian. In Delhi's mechanical department reportedly one of fifteen had a PhD; in Kanpur's only four of twelve did not. Kanpur's director an academic, not a civil-service engineer.
- p. 15: Language: institute operates in English, but 'only a fraction of the students feel thoroughly comfortable' in it; English in the common entrance exam has a deliberately low pass mark so as not to exclude promising engineers; first-year course largely English-language teaching; support staff's English 'poor to nonexistent'. Hindi ruled out by national politics and lack of technical literature; half the faculty (Bengal, Gujarat, the south, eight of ten department chairmen) might leave if it switched. [Key for central question 3: language as a filter.]
- p. 16: Premature tenure (after one year for associate lecturers, even for graduate assistants) threatened quality; Fay fears replacement of a staff 'recruited at American insistence on the basis of ability only'. Good technicians hard to find at institute pay; equipment poorly maintained. By November 1965 more than $4 million of equipment and books requisitioned from the US; 'already the best equipped engineering and science school in India', which helped attract faculty. The 1620, 'operating since the summer of 1963 and a very great success', began 'as an idea in an American's head'. 'For we have always been in a hurry. The program is to run for only ten years, and individuals never stay for more than two.'
- p. 17: Ordering was easier than building; some equipment idle (wind tunnels 'toys to please visiting firemen'). April 1966 heat; children kept indoors till sundown.

## Rajaraman S0001 on Kanpur (pp. 16-18, from scratchpad text)
- IBM 1620 installed August 1963; first computer with a FORTRAN compiler in an Indian educational institution; imported with USAID funds through KIAP (p. 16).
- Ten-day courses from 1963, three times a year, continued by IITK faculty until 1975 after the Americans left in 1965 (p. 17).
- 1964: compulsory course in programming and numerical methods for all engineering students (p. 17). 1620 used round the clock with free student access; 1966 IBM 7044 bought with Government of India funds (p. 17).

## S0223 Ravi Sethi, 'Our Experiences on the 1620', The Spark, Oct 2021 (via WebFetch; verify quotes)
- Took an evening programming course after the fall 1963 midterm, taught by Forman Acton. 'Six of us were selected to operate the IBM 1620.' 'I recall being authorized to turn the machine on, which was an event in itself.' Acton and Irving Rabinowitz taught machine language. Summer job: 'I spent the summer working for Harry Huskey, who handed me a box of cards with a compiler for HH-1'.
- Workflow: 'To run a program, you punched the code, line by line, on a deck of cards, one line per card'; operator ran jobs; output decks to line printer. 'From the blinking lights on the console, we could tell if a program was in a loop'. Rabinowitz challenge: a card-copy program on a single card, six instructions or less. Power: generators, then air-conditioning, then the 1620. Programs in FORGO (FORTRAN variant), numerical analysis.
- Classes then at Harcourt Butler Technological Institute.

## S0225 Abhay Bhushan, Vox Populi, Parts I-III, 2014 (via WebFetch; verify quotes)
- Part I: 'landed at Kanpur Central with a few Naya Paisas in my pocket'; met by a truck with 'dreamy-eyed boys who arrived from all parts of the country'; lived at the Government Central Textile Institute hostel in Gwaltoli until moving to Hall 1 in March 1963; classes and labs at Harcourt Butler Technological Institute. Early professors 'borrowed from the archaic U.P. educational system' who 'believed in memory and rote, even for math problems'; second-year faculty largely new, many with doctorates from 'the United States, Canada and UK'. Kelkar 'was always prepared to meet any of us students at a moment's notice.' 'There were no girls, not even one among the hundred who started.'
- Part II: roll no. 60001; fewer than 70 in the hostel, two to a room; contractor food 'generally of poor quality'; 'English was our first humanities subject.' The 1961 batch of almost 100 lived in rented houses in Kanpur city.
- Part III: first classes on the new campus held in the Workshop (1963); American professors' novelties (Zimmerman's Polaroid; Holt Ashley of MIT brought basketballs); 'As there were no girl students, the boys dressed up as girls for the plays'; film society; 'I was fortunate to have been accepted at M.I.T. and given a Research Assistantship.'
