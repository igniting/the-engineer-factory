## S0088 Manmohan Singh, Budget Speech 1991-92 (24 July 1991). Full text read earlier.

PDF page = printed page.

### The crisis

- **p. 1, para 2.** 'The new Government, which assumed office barely a month ago, inherited an economy in deep crisis. The balance of payments situation is precarious.'
  - Despite IMF borrowing in July 1990 and January 1991, reserves fell. 'We have been at the edge of a precipice since December 1990 and more so since April 1991.'
  - 'The foreign exchange crisis constitutes a serious threat to the sustainability of growth'.
  - 'We have not experienced anything similar in the history of independent India.'
- **p. 2, para 5.** External debt; debt service 'about 21 per cent of current account receipts in 1990-91'; 'the Gulf crisis'.
  - **'The current level of foreign exchange reserves, in the range of Rs.2500 crores, would suffice to finance imports for a mere fortnight.'**
  - The forex thesis at its most acute.

### The reforms (p. 4)

- **Para 11, trade.** Import substitution 'has not always been efficient and has some times been indiscriminate'.
  - 'The exchange rate adjustments on 1st and 3rd July 1991 and the enlargement and liberalisation of the replenishment licence system constitute the two major initial steps'.
  - 'the beginning of a transition from a regime of quantitative restrictions to a price based mechanism'.
- **Para 12, foreign investment.** 'we should welcome, rather than fear, foreign investment. Our entrepreneurs are second to none. Our industry has come of age.'
  - Foreign equity up to 51% in priority industries, given prompt approval.

### Software (p. 20)

**Para 78:** 'Our software industry has made considerable progress in recent years. However, there is still a vast unexploited potential for growth. It is time we make all-out efforts to capture the overseas software market. With this objective, I propose to extend the tax concession under section 80HHC of the Income-tax Act to export of software.'

- Para 80: professionals' skills 'have an increasing capacity to earn foreign exchange for the country'.
  - Software is framed explicitly as a forex earner.

### Close (p. 31)

Victor Hugo: 'no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come'; 'India is now wide awake. We shall prevail. We shall overcome.'

## S0001 Rajaraman, pp. 43-48 (read 2026-10-12)

Printed page at the foot of each page.

### p. 43

- 1991: near-default; IMF and World Bank bail-out; conditions 'forced India to liberalize'.
- 'The Rupee was devalued (from Rs.17.5 to Rs.26 per USD)'.
- Exports 'around USD 128 million in 1991 increased to USD 1759 million in 1997'; '160,000 software engineers' employed by 1997.
- Growth 'hovering around 3.8% from 1950 to 1990 suddenly went up to around 7% within two years'.
- Factors listed: Y2K, the Euro, 'a technically savvy influential Indian diaspora', satellite links, 'human resources with good knowledge of English', quality consciousness, project management.

### p. 44: policy

- 'The import duty on computers used for software export was abolished. Export earnings of software companies were made tax free for 10 years.'
- MNCs 'could be 100% equity companies'.
- Murthy's three changes:
  - convertibility, which let firms open offices abroad, pay marketing costs, 'send software engineers to clients' sites at short notice', and hire foreign consultants;
  - abolition of the Controller of Capital Issues: 'Many software companies floated IPOs';
  - duty on software tools abolished.
- 'the government also permitted private software companies to have dedicated satellite links with their overseas customers.'
- National Telecommunication Policy 1994 allowed private telecom firms.
- 'The devaluation of the rupee enabled Indian software companies to be competitive in selling their services'.

### pp. 44-45: consequences

- Exports '$128 million during 1990-1991 went up to around USD 1.76 billion in 1997-1998 ... an average growth of 45% each year'.
- 'Indian software companies invented what is known as the Global Delivery Model (GDM) and the 24 hour work day' (ref. 80, Murthy).
  - GDM: about '20 to 30%' of project time with customers onsite; '70 to 80%' coding and testing in India in 'secure areas ... devoted entirely to a particular client' with a dedicated satellite link (p. 45).
  - 24-hour day: India's prime shift is US night; bugs fixed overnight. The same model was used for medical transcription and call centres, 'called IT enabled Services (ITeS)' (p. 45).
- **'The salary of software engineers in India was about 20% that of an equivalent western engineer.'** (p. 45)
- ISO 9001. SEI CMM: 'The Motorola (India) software team was the first team in the world to attain this maturity level in 1993' (pp. 45-46).
  - **Dispute (cc):** Motorola India's CMM Level 5 date. The STATE batch 3 dispute ('first Indian CMM Level 5 unclear') stays open. Rajaraman says 1993; other sources (not yet registered) commonly give 1993 for assessment or later. Verify.
- 2012: 166 of 250 CMM-5 firms worldwide in India; 'Correct statistics are hard to obtain' (p. 46).
- **Process discipline:** 'The process orientation allowed companies which were recruiting large numbers of employees each year to quickly train them to become productive members of a development team. Staff attrition also could be managed better' (p. 46).
  - Process as a way to make labour interchangeable. Key for the thesis and for Ch. 19 (AI and process).

### p. 46: Y2K

- Y2K was 'labour intensive' and 'had to be outsourced'. 'This was a godsend opportunity to many fledgling software services companies in India.'
- Some firms fixed Y2K 'and also improving the software ... at no extra cost'.
- '23% of the revenue of INFOSYS ... in 1997 was for fixing the Y2K bug' (via Sharma S0005, p. 349).
  - Contrast F-1: Y2K was about 7.5% of fiscal 1997 revenue (S0089). **Dispute (dd).** Probably different periods: fiscal 1997 vs calendar 1997 or fiscal 1998. Check.

### pp. 46-47: diaspora

- '"brain drain" of the 60s and the 70s became "opportunity gain" in the 90s'.
- The diaspora gave 'small contracts at the beginning'.
- NRIs set up firms with relatives.

### p. 47

- STPs offered offices and workstations 'at low rentals'.
- VC: TDICI 1988 (UTI with ICICI), then Canbank VC and state funds.
- The Euro (announced 1995) brought conversion work.
- FDI centres: American Express in Mumbai 1994 (back office); Tata-IBM 1992, 50%; HP, Oracle, GE Capital.
- 'over 200 software services companies' set up private dedicated satellite links.
- 'the voice of NASSCOM was strong and it got the ear of the government'.

## S0034 Murthy CHM part 2, pp. 8-9 (read earlier)

- 1991 reforms:
  - 'First, it abolished licensing for computers. As I pointed out earlier, it would take us fifty visits to Delhi and about three years of elapsed time before we got a license'.
  - Second, 'current account convertibility'. Before it, 'if I wanted to travel abroad even for a day, I had to apply to the Reserve Bank of India ... wait for about 15 days'. The Paris-Frankfurt show-cause notice.
  - Third, the Controller of Capital Issues 'had no idea what the capital markets were'. IPOs were usually at par, so 'most entrepreneurs were uncomfortable about going public'. After abolition, 'the industry took off'.
- 'there is absolutely no doubt that for an industry to take off and demonstrate rapid growth you need the blessings of the government' (p. 8). Self-account.

## S0034 Murthy CHM part 2, pp. 16-20 (IPO and GE)

### IPO (pp. 17-18)

- The firm wanted to list in 1991 and postponed after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination (May 1991).
- 1992 brought the Harshad Mehta scam; 6 December 1992 the Babri Masjid demolition.
- 'So, we went public on 24th of February 1993. It was a very tough time. We barely managed it. Nobody had any idea what a software company was.'
- 'We were the second software company to go public', after Mastek (December 1992).
- IPO 24-27 February; 'listing was in June'; 'It opened pretty well at about 50 percent premium.'
- US GAAP voluntarily from 1995; NASDAQ ADRs 1999.

### GE (pp. 18-20)

- After a Jack Welch presentation to Rajiv Gandhi in the 1980s and Jairam Ramesh's counter-presentation, GE 'selected four Indian companies - Infosys was one of them'.
- Infosys started work for GE in 1990.
- '1995 May was when they came to renegotiate. At that point of time, General Electric formed about 25 percent of our revenue.'
- 'They sequestered the vendors in different rooms at a five-star hotel in the city. They would go from room to room and would negotiate to get better and better terms.' The negotiation ran Thursday and Friday.
- 'All other competitors of ours were ready to sign on the dotted line.'
- Murthy refused: 'if we reduced our prices to them we would have to reduce prices to our other customers too'. That would leave less money for 'compensation to our employees', tools and productivity.
- The work was transitioned to competitors; 'my colleagues were unhappy with me'.

### Pricing claim (p. 16)

'at least until 2007, 2008, probably 2009, Infosys always got 20-25 percent higher price compared to our competitors in India'. Founder claim; unverified.

## S0089 Infosys F-1 (1999), 1990s facts

- 'The Company completed its initial public offering on the Bangalore Stock Exchange in 1993.'
- From fiscal 1994 to fiscal 1998: revenue CAGR 63.6%, net income 46.8%; staff from about 480 to about 2,200 IT professionals.
- 1995: 'the Company chose to reduce significantly the services provided to its then-largest client rather than to accept the price reductions and increased Company resources sought by such client' (Risk Factors).
  - Largest client fell from 21.5% of revenue (fiscal 1996) to 6.7% (fiscal 1997) (MD&A).
  - The client is not named in the F-1. Do not name it without a source.
- Fiscal 1997 revenue $39.6m (+48.8%). Y2K 'approximately 7.5% of revenues for fiscal 1997'.
- Fixed-price share: 24.0% (fiscal 1996) and 37.0% (fiscal 1997). Time-and-materials: 76.0% and 63.0% (MD&A).
- p. 44: 'the Company is seeking to increase the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-time frame rather than a time-and-materials basis'. Fixed price offers 'the potential benefit of enhanced margins for projects that are performed efficiently'. 'The Company's policy is to decline or discontinue projects that do not offer the potential to meet the Company's profit margin targets.' (Key for Ch. 19: who captures productivity gains.)
  - Time-based billing dominant, and declining slowly.
- Visas (as of 31 December 1998): 231 on H-1B, 111 on L-1 in the US.
- NASSCOM via F-1: professionals about 56,000 (fiscal 1990) to 200,000 (fiscal 1998).

## S0254 Khanna and Morales (2016), pp. 5-6

- Late 1980s: '90% of software revenues came from on-site work (NASSCOM, 2003)'. Exports from about $50m (late 1980s) to about $200m by 1993.
- 'in 1992, satellite links were set up in Software Technology Parks (STP) negating the need for some kinds of on-site work'.
- **'In 1993, the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work, as they were to be paid the prevailing market wage.'**
- 'The fraction of on-site work went from 90% in 1988 to 66% in 1995 and 56% in 2000.'
  - **Dispute (bb):** onsite share in 1988. 90% of revenue (NASSCOM via S0254) against 'just under 75%' of development (Heeks S0085, p. 6). Different measures: revenue against effort. In 2000, 56% (S0254) and 57.25% (IDE via S0086) agree.

  - A pay datum, second-hand.
