**Chapter question.** What happened to the engineer factory when the pandemic sent its workers home, demand surged and then collapsed, and the workers began to organise?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The swing.** Infosys's offers of employment went from 48,029 in fiscal 2021 to 222,500 in fiscal 2022. They fell to 114,374 in fiscal 2023 and 26,975 in fiscal 2024 (S0288-S0291). Its headcount rose from 242,371 in March 2020 to 343,234 in March 2023, then fell to 317,240 (S0287-S0291). Cognizant reported 'unprecedented attrition' of 30.8% in 2021 and 31.7% in 2022, and hired 'over a hundred thousand new employees in each of 2021 and 2022' (S0293).

**Remote work.** Work went home almost overnight. Infosys had enabled 93% of its employees to work remotely by 20 March 2020 (S0287). A year later 96.5% were working remotely (S0288). That proved the offshore model did not need its secure campuses.

**Moonlighting.** It also made moonlighting possible. In September 2022 Wipro's chairman said the company had found 300 employees 'working directly for one of our competitors' and dismissed them (S0131). Infosys added moonlighting to its risk factors (S0290). Mohandas Pai answered: 'No, moonlighting is not cheating' (S0131).

**Delays and dismissals.** When demand fell, campus recruits waited. Freshers told *Business Today* in 2022 of joining dates postponed into 2023 (S0134). In 2024 the Union Labour Ministry directed Karnataka to act on a union complaint about more than 2,000 Infosys recruits from 2022-23 still waiting (S0295). In February 2025 Infosys dismissed trainees at Mysuru who failed assessments: over 300 by its count, 700 by the union's (S0133).

**Unions.** The unions that raised these cases were new. KITU was registered in Karnataka in November 2017 (S0132). Its founders spoke of 'layoffs to automation' (S0294).

**AI and the contract.** By fiscal 2024 Infosys reported that, 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies', clients had demanded higher productivity commitments in contracts (S0291).

**Caution.** The 2023-24 slowdown coincided with generative AI's arrival. The sources do not show that one caused the other.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 Sent home

The industry had been built on secure buildings. In the 1990s Infosys described 'secure areas ... devoted entirely to a particular client', linked by dedicated satellite (Chapter 11). Its campuses at Bangalore and Mysore were the factory's physical form (Chapter 12). In March 2020 the work left them. Infosys's annual report for the year to March 2020 states: 'By March 20, 2020, we had enabled 93% of our employees to be able to work remotely' (S0287). A year later: 'As of March 31, 2021, we had enabled 99.3% of our employees with a secure remote working environment and 96.5% of our employees were working remotely' (S0288). The following year's report summarised the first weeks: 'we were able to transition 99% of employees globally to a work from home arrangement' (S0289).

The filings record the strains as risks. In 2020: 'Our ability to continue to deliver service delivery obligations while our employees work from home are sometimes constrained by contractual terms with our clients' (S0287). The secure-floor model was written into client contracts, and remote work needed clients' approval. In 2021: 'The productivity of our employees may be negatively impacted due to isolated remote working from home, quarantine requirements, negative social sentiment and personal anxiety' (S0288). From 2023 the company described 'Our transition to a hybrid working model' as a source of risk (S0290, S0291).

The book's argument needs one point from this. The pandemic proved that the work of the Indian services firm could be done from a bedroom in a small town as well as from a campus in Bangalore. That weakened the case for the city and the campus (Chapters 10 and 12). It also opened two doors the industry had kept shut: an employee could work for two employers at once, and the work, once shown to need no particular place, could be examined again for whether it needed a particular person.

### 3.2 The boom

Demand returned faster than anyone had planned for. The clearest numbers are Infosys's recruiting statistics, which it reports every year in its 20-F (S0287-S0291):
- **FY2020:** 2,141,373 applications, 150,130 interviews, 83,319 offers.
- **FY2021:** 1,427,618 applications, 135,216 interviews, 48,029 offers.
- **FY2022:** 5,866,636 applications, 523,385 interviews, 222,500 offers.
- **FY2023:** 5,342,299 applications, 389,183 interviews, 114,374 offers.
- **FY2024:** 2,436,929 applications, 194,367 interviews, 26,975 offers.

Offers rose more than fourfold in one year and then fell by about 88% over two. Infosys's headcount went from 242,371 in March 2020 to 259,619 in 2021, 314,015 in 2022 and 343,234 in 2023, before falling to 317,240 in March 2024 (S0287-S0291). Wipro's annual reports give rounded figures: more than 200,000 employees in March 2021, 240,000 in 2022 and 250,000 in 2023, then 234,000 in 2024 (S0292).

Cognizant's annual reports explain the mechanism in their own words. 'In 2021, we experienced unprecedented attrition, which was considered industry-wide. As a result, we hired over a hundred thousand new employees' (S0293, 10-K FY2021). Its attrition rate, voluntary and involuntary combined, was 20.6% in 2020, 30.8% in 2021 and 31.7% in 2022. On an annualised basis it reached 34.6% in the last quarter of 2021 (S0293). 'Our attrition is weighted towards our more junior employees. In 2021, voluntary attrition constituted the vast majority of our attrition for the period'. By contrast, in 2020 voluntary exits had been only about half, because the company's own 'Fit for Growth Plan' had increased dismissals (S0293). Its 2023 report adds that it 'hired over a hundred thousand new employees in each of 2021 and 2022, and over sixty thousand in 2023' (S0293, 10-K FY2023). Its workforce rose from 289,500 at the end of 2020 to 355,300 at the end of 2022, of whom 258,500 were in India (S0293).

Attrition at this level meant that, for a large firm, the pyramid had to be refilled almost from scratch every three years. The cost appears in Cognizant's own risk language: 'While we strive to adjust pricing to reduce the impact of compensation increases on our operating margin, we may not be successful' (S0293, 10-K FY2021). This is the time-and-materials model under stress (Chapters 9 and 11). When labour costs rise, the firm must persuade clients to pay more per hour.

The comparable attrition rates for TCS and Infosys are published in their quarterly fact sheets, which could not be retrieved (S0160, S0161). The data appendix must fill this gap. Cognizant's are the only primary rates in hand.

### 3.3 Moonlighting

Remote work made a second job easy to hide. In August 2022 Wipro's chairman, Rishad Premji, wrote on Twitter that there was 'a lot of chatter about people moonlighting in the tech industry', and that 'it is cheating- plain and simple' (S0131, quoting the tweet). On 21 September 2022, at the All India Management Association convention, he went further: 'The reality is that there are people today working for Wipro and working directly for one of our competitors and we have actually discovered 300 people in the last few months who are doing exactly that' (S0131). They had been dismissed. He called the practice a 'complete violation of integrity in its deepest form' (S0131).

The industry was divided. *Business Today* reported that Infosys had sent 'a strong and firm message to employees last week that moonlighting is not allowed', and that TCS's chief operating officer warned that employees 'might lose out in the long term' (S0131). T.V. Mohandas Pai, a former Infosys director, disagreed: 'No, moonlighting is not cheating.' 'Employment is a contract between an employer who pays me for working for them for 'n' number of hours a day. During that time, I have to abide by their conditions, including client confidentiality, and I'm paid for that. At that time, I can't work for anybody else. Now what I do after that time is my freedom' (S0131).

Infosys then wrote the issue into its annual risk factors: 'Continued remote working exposes the company to the risk of some employees working for multiple employers simultaneously (moonlighting) without obtaining required prior approvals and this may result in potential conflict of interests, confidentiality breach, and reduced productivity' (S0290, S0291).

For the book's argument, the moonlighting dispute is the time-based contract seen from the employee's side. The firm sells its engineers' hours to clients. Pai's defence, in which the employer buys 'n number of hours' and the rest belong to the worker, is the same logic applied in reverse. When work was done on a client's secure floor, the question did not arise. When it was done at home, an engineer's capacity was no longer visibly bounded by the office day, and both sides discovered how much of the relationship depended on that boundary.

### 3.4 Offers delayed, offers withdrawn

The turn came in 2022. On 5 October 2022 *Business Today* reported complaints from freshers that Accenture had delayed their onboarding, after earlier reports about Wipro, Infosys, HCL Tech, Tech Mahindra and Capgemini (S0134). The freshers it quoted are anonymous:
- One had been 'placed in Accenture as Associate Software Engineer (ASE) via campus placement in November 2021. I received my offer letter in the last week of April this year. My onboarding is being pushed time after time, the latest date was September 30 and now it has been pushed to somewhere in 2023.'
- Another, whose date had been moved two days before joining: 'I had packed my bags and booked all tickets to move to the new city, just two days before joining, they postponed the date again. When will they finally make me join, who will pay for my expenses in the meanwhile?'
- One had turned down other offers: 'I am riddled with depressive feelings because I rejected TCS, Wipro, and HCL for a job in Accenture.'
- Another feared the gap: 'It's a year since I have been waiting to get onboarded. It is going to reflect as a gap year in my CV' (S0134).

A graduate of CV Raman Global University in Bhubaneswar said the college placement cell 'should have helped us in this tough time'. One from Galgotias University in Greater Noida said the placement office stopped responding once students had graduated (S0134).

The pattern repeats the bust of 2001-02, when Anna University's placements fell from 1,200 to about 320 and joining dates were deferred 'by over a year' (Chapter 11). It also repeats the Satyam recruits of 2009 (Chapter 16). This was the third time in twenty years that the factory's newest products bore the first shock of a turn in demand.

In 2024 the delays at Infosys reached the state. *The News Minute* reported that on 20 August 2024 Harpreet Singh Saluja, president of the Pune-based Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), filed complaints alleging 'the exploitation and unprofessional treatment of over 2,000 fresh engineering graduates selected by Infosys' in its 2022-23 campus drive, for System Engineer and Digital Specialist Engineer roles (S0295). On 3 September the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment directed the Karnataka Labour Commissioner to take 'appropriate action'. The directive, signed by Regional Labour Commissioner O.P. Singh, said labour-law enforcement in the matter fell to the state (S0295). Infosys had already issued about 2,000 final offer letters with joining dates in October 2024. About 700 candidates were still waiting (S0295).

The sequel came at Mysore. In February 2025, *Deccan Herald* reported, 'the IT services giant admitted to laying off over 300 freshers, who had undergone foundational training at the Infosys Mysuru campus for months'. NITES said the number was 700 (S0133; new dispute zz). The union's complaint said: 'Infosys Ltd has resorted to forcibly terminating recently onboarded campus recruits who had already suffered a delay of two years in their joining after being issued offer letters' (S0133). Infosys said it 'did not forcefully terminate anyone'. Its 'rigorous hiring process' required trainees to clear internal assessments 'within three attempts, failing which they will not be able to continue with the organisation', and the process had 'been in existence for over two decades' (S0133). The Union Labour Ministry again directed Karnataka to take 'urgent necessary actions' (S0133).

The assessment at the end of training was part of the factory from the start (Chapter 12: the 14-week course at Mysore). In 2025, after a two-year wait, it became the exit door. Both sides' accounts must be given.

### 3.5 The unions

The organisations that took up these cases were young. A law firm's note of November 2017 records: 'On 7th November 2017, the Commissioner of Karnataka Labour Department certified the formation of the Karnataka State IT/ITES Employees Union (KITU) under the Trade Union Act, 1926' (S0132). It called KITU 'the first trade union in India, officially registered and recognised by the labour department, which is dedicated to the information technology ("IT") sector'. KITU 'claims to have about 250 IT industry employees as its members' (S0132). 'First' is the law firm's word and the union's claim. *The News Minute* mentions an earlier, unregistered 'IT Employees Centre' (S0294), so the claim needs care (new dispute yy).

The same note explains why a union mattered legally. The Industrial Disputes Act's requirement of prior approval for retrenchment 'does not cover IT industry'. 'by a notification published on January 25, 2014 the Karnataka government exempted IT companies from applicability of Standing Orders Act for a period of five years' (S0132). The industry had grown under exemptions from the labour rules that governed factories. The engineer factory was not, in law, a factory.

The founding meeting, on 20 August 2017 at a hall in Koramangala, Bengaluru, drew about 200 people (S0294). Its general secretary, Vineeth, a testing lead at the Bengaluru software firm MindTree and a member of an earlier, unregistered IT Employees Centre that had handled complaints about layoffs and maternity leave for eight years, said: 'What is currently happening is that if an employee is facing a problem, they have to fight in their individual capacity and the companies may not listen to them. The trade union will help them negotiate and bargain with the management, and even fight' (S0294). Sethumadhavan, a Wipro employee: 'As per our Constitution, we have the right to join a union. Today the industry is facing so many challenges - from layoffs to automation. This is the right time to form an organisation' (S0294). The president, Amanullah Khan: 'Trade unions are generally seen as anti-social or anti-national ... But we are not individuals anymore. We are a group which gives us the strength to meet the challenges of our time' (S0294). The report notes that 'Several voices in the past, including Mohandas Pai, have opposed the idea of a union for the IT sector' (S0294).

By 2024-25 a second organisation, NITES, was bringing complaints to the Union Labour Ministry and getting directions issued to the state (S0295, S0133). Neither union's membership in these years is documented in the sources in hand.

### 3.6 Restructuring and hiring abroad

The bust brought restructuring as well as slower hiring. Cognizant's 2023 annual report describes a 'NextGen program aimed at simplifying our operating model, optimizing corporate functions and consolidating and realigning office space to reflect the post-pandemic hybrid work environment', begun in the second quarter of 2023. 'In connection with the NextGen program, in 2023 we incurred $115 million of employee separation costs and $114 million of facility exit and other costs totaling $229 million' (S0293, 10-K FY2023). It warned that 'Our NextGen program and the associated reductions in headcount and consolidation of office space could disrupt our business' (S0293). The pandemic's hybrid working thus became a reason to give up offices as well as staff.

The filings also show the factory's intake moving partly abroad, in line with the visa politics of Chapter 13. Infosys recorded that 'In fiscal 2021, we recruited over 7,280 employees locally in our markets, of which 1,941 were fresh graduates'. In fiscal 2022 the figures were 14,805 and 3,650, and in fiscal 2023 10,169 and 2,216 (S0288-S0290). These are small beside the hundreds of thousands hired in India, but they show the firms building local workforces in client countries, partly as insurance against visa restrictions.

### 3.7 The first sign of AI in the contract

The most important single sentence for the book's final part appears in Infosys's annual report for fiscal 2024, filed in mid-2024. In its risk factors the company wrote: 'due to competitive market conditions and pricing pressures, we are committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts with our clients. Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements. Any failure to realize such anticipated productivity improvements either due to our inability to identify areas to automate, optimize processes, leverage technology, effectively address service delivery risks or manage client requirements may impact our profitability' (S0291).

This is the pricing question this book has followed since Chapter 9, now stated by the company in a sworn filing. Clients had read about AI tools that write code. They asked their Indian suppliers to commit, in contract, to doing the same work with less effort. The supplier bears the risk if the promised gains do not materialise. Under time-and-materials billing, a client capturing productivity gains means fewer billable hours. Under fixed-price or outcome contracts, it means a lower price for the same outcome. Either way, the gain from the new tools flows first to the client. The firm keeps a share only if it beats the commitment.

The same filing records the company's own investment in the technology: 'Over the last few years, we have made significant investments in building our capabilities and training our employees in emerging technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI), generative AI and cloud technologies' (S0291). It also names 'Generative AI' among 'emerging areas of demand' where larger competitors might win (S0291). And among the conditions of fiscal 2024 it lists that 'clients may become cautious and reduce their discretionary spending in the short term or delay their decision-making process' (S0291).

The drafting must keep two things separate. The filing documents that AI changed what clients asked for in contracts by 2024. It does not document that AI caused Infosys's offers to fall from 222,500 to 26,975. The company's own explanation for the slowdown in the same document is clients' caution about discretionary spending. The causal question belongs to Part V and needs more evidence than one risk factor. As a marker, though, the sentence is precise: the first time in this research that a leading Indian services firm stated in its annual report that AI was changing the terms on which it sold its engineers' work.

### 3.8 What the boom and bust showed

Three lessons from these years matter for the final part of the book.

First, the factory's intake is set by clients' discretionary spending, not by the supply of graduates. A fourfold swing in Infosys's offers in a single year, followed by an 88% fall over two (S0288-S0291), shows how little control the colleges or the graduates had.

Second, the boom was a labour-market event as much as a demand event. Cognizant's attrition above 30% (S0293) and Wipro's moonlighting dismissals (S0131) show engineers using a tight market. That was the first time since the late 1990s that individual engineers held the advantage.

Third, the bust began just as generative AI tools arrived, in late 2022 and 2023. The sources in hand do not show that AI caused the fall in hiring. Cognizant dates the decline in attrition to the second half of 2022 (S0293), and the filings attribute the slowdown to clients' spending. The drafting must keep the coincidence and the causation apart, and leave the argument to Part V.

Fourth, by the end of the period AI had entered the contract even where it had not yet entered the headcount. Infosys's statement that clients 'have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements' because of AI's 'code generation capabilities' (S0291) shows the pressure arriving through pricing, exactly where the time-based model was weakest. The firms that had sold hours for forty years were now being asked to promise that the hours would shrink. That is where Part V begins.
