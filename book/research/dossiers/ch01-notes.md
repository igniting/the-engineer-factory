## S0013 Narasimhan, 'Men, machines, and ideas', Current Science 76(3), 10 Feb 1999 (scanned PDF, read as page images 2026-10-12)

p. 447
- Expands TIFRAC as 'TIFR Automatic Computer' (not 'Calculator') and calls it 'the first fullscale electronic digital computer designed, built, and operated in India'.
- At TIFR 36 years, 1954-1990. Three benefactors: Bhabha, Sarabhai, M.G.K. Menon (founder-chairman of Electronics Commission, founder-secretary of DoE). NCSDCT set up at TIFR at Menon's initiative with UNDP help.
- Footnote: abridged version due in *Seminar*.

p. 448
- Narasimhan: B.E.(Hons) 1947, College of Engineering, Guindy, Chennai; mentor K. Sreenivasan (head of telecom engineering) helped him get a part-loan scholarship from the J.N. Tata Endowment for US postgraduate study; six years in the US, 1948-1954.
- P.P. Gupta: founder MD of Computer Maintenance Corporation (CMC), set up as public-sector corporation in 1976; Narasimhan was its founder-chairman 1976-1979.
- Computing activity at TIFR began in 1954 in the Instrumentation Group; end of 1954 decision to build 'a fullscale, general purpose, electronic digital computer using contemporary technology'.
- Team at start 'about six', most with an M.S. in physics specialising in electronics. Quote: 'Except for one member (namely, myself), none had been outside India, and none of the participants in the project, including myself, had any experience in either using a computer or operating one, let alone building one.' (p. 448) [check length: c. 40 words, OK]
- Two phases: pilot machine as proving ground and on-the-job training; then full-scale machine.
- Dates: pilot design started early 1955, operational by October 1956 [Table 1 on p. 449 says commissioned Nov. 1956: internal discrepancy]; full-scale design started early 1957; fabrication completed early 1959; for lack of 'a suitable airconditioned area' commissioned after testing only in February 1960; named TIFRAC in 1962 when the new TIFR building was inaugurated by Nehru.
- Context: von Neumann's IAS machine (1946-1952); ILLIAC I at Illinois.

p. 449
- 'The TIFRAC control logic design benefited crucially through design details kindly made available by the ILLIAC team.'
- By 1960 commissioning, TIFRAC was already 'an obsolete first generation machine'; other Princeton-type machines had had about 5 years' useful life by then. Second-generation machines needed production know-how only big manufacturers had; so in 1962-63 TIFR looked to the world market for a commercial system (-> CDC 3600).
- TIFRAC in operation 1960 to early 1964; by the end run 2 shifts daily.
- Quote: 'many of the computer users in India, handling sophisticated and advanced computational techniques, had their first introduction to programming through the use of TIFRAC.' (p. 449)
- Users: TIFR, Atomic Energy Establishment, some universities (crystallography group in Chennai); cosmic-ray air-shower data analysis depended on it.
- Computer section grew from half a dozen to some two dozen (academic, scientific, technical). Built CRT display with character generation (Memotron tube), magnetic tape back-up; academic work on automata, coding theory.
- Table 1 (p. 449): IAS (1946-52; 2,300 tubes; 1,024 words CRT memory; 40-bit); IBM 701 (1950-52; 4,000 tubes; 13,000 diodes; 2,048 words); TIFR pilot (1955 started; Nov 1956 commissioned; 256 words ferrite core; 12-bit; 10 kW); TIFRAC (1957 started; Feb 1959 completed; Feb 1960 commissioned; 2,700 tubes; 1,700 germanium diodes; 12,500 resistors; 2,048 words ferrite core; 15 microsec cycle; 40-bit; add 45 microsec; multiply 500 microsec; paper tape, teleprinter, CRT display, tape added later; 20 kW).

p. 450
- TIFRAC software: a 3-address interpretive routine for floating-point arithmetic, popular with users; storage too small for compilers.
- Maintaining TIFRAC trained a group in preventive maintenance of a complex electronic system; valuable later.
- **Early brain drain:** 'Between 1959 and 1962 some half-a-dozen of the scientists who had been actively concerned with the design and construction of TIFRAC had gone to various universities in the USA on a variety of visiting appointments. Three of these decided to settle down permanently in the US' (p. 450) [quote c. 45 words: trim if used]. One returned 1962 after two years on a second-generation project at Illinois; others returned later. Narasimhan himself at Illinois Digital Computer Laboratory 1961-1964.
- Narasimhan chaired TIFR's Expert Technical Committee that compared IBM 7094, CDC 3600, Philco 211, Univac 1107; chose CDC 3600 though only the first was operating, at the Minneapolis plant. Bought with 'a US Aid Loan'; CDC-3600-160A commissioned at TIFR mid-June 1964; National Computation Centre from October 1964, maintained and run entirely by TIFR staff. Narasimhan head of the new Computer Group.
- [Ch. 3 material] Early 1970s: government decisions for self-reliance: develop small/medium computers in India; centralise procurement via global tenders; CMC set up 1976. UNDP mission May 1973 recommended NCSDCT at TIFR. NCSDCT informal from late 1974; formally launched 1975 with UNDP contract for a DEC 1077 and PDP-11/40 graphics system (installed mid-1975). National Computation Centre used by petroleum companies, dockyards, power and space agencies, state governments, town planners. NCSDCT spun off 1985 to Juhu as NCST; S. Ramani its head.

p. 451 [Ch. 3/7 material]
- Narasimhan director of NCSDCT 1975-1985. R&D staff c. 30, a third PhDs; total c. 50.
- Work: operating systems and language processors in high-level languages; graphics; text processing in Indian languages and scripts; data transmission and networking; databases.
- **Education:** NCSDCT's one-year modular evening course in computer science and application, at VJTI in central Mumbai, remote data link over telephone lines to the PDP-10 at TIFR; academic rigour 'at the same level as in a full-time post-graduate course'; preference for people already employed in software; employers persuaded to pay; entrance test; 60-80 students at a time; self-study, consultants, assignments, imported textbooks. Quote: 'Formal educational channels are wholly inadequate to cope with a demand of this magnitude.' (p. 451) [date of the course not given on this page: check p. 452]

p. 452 [Ch. 3/7 material]
- NCSDCT evening course 'very soon became one of the most sought after training programmes in Mumbai'; NCST later made post-graduate and advanced training a core activity.
- FST-TCS conference series from 1981.
- Policy Committee external experts: M.V. Wilkes (Cambridge) and W.A. Wulf (Carnegie-Mellon). NCSDCT self-sufficient in operating budget except UNDP input.
- Remainder of essay (pp. 452-454, 'Ideas') is on Narasimhan's cognitive-science work; not needed for Ch. 1. Pages 453-454 not read.

## S0007 Menon, 'Chasing the Machine', Caravan, 1 April 2018 (read via WebFetch 2026-10-12)

**Caution:** quotes below were extracted by the fetch tool's summariser; re-check each against the page in Phase 5 before using in text.

- Feb 1954 lunch hosted by Nehru; Mahalanobis to Soviet ambassador Menshikov: 'Surely the U.S.S.R. can have no objection in giving and teaching us things that are known to the Americans?'

- HEC-2M: bought in London for GBP 18,500, placed on loan to ISI by the Government of India. 'In early 1956, it arrived in two crates and was housed in an air-conditioned room on the institute's campus.' 'Measuring six feet in height, ten in length, and seven in breadth, it consisted of three vertical metallic cabinets.' [Dispute with S0001/S0010: arrival July 1955 / mid-1955.]

- **Morton Nadler**: 37-year-old *American* engineer who had worked at 'the foremost computer institute in Czechoslovakia - the Institute of Mathematical Machines'; suspected of espionage by both US and Czechoslovak sides; FBI (Hoover) asked to investigate; flew Prague to Calcutta on Air India, Feb 1959; two-year ISI contract. [Earlier chronology called him Hungarian: corrected 2026-10-12.] Scene candidate.
- Planning Commission Delhi computer centre, autumn 1965, 'enabled by a Ford Foundation grant worth Rs 23 lakh'. India had 15 digital computers by end-1964.

## S0012 Narasimhan, 'On the system and engineering design of the general purpose electronic digital computer at T.I.F.R.', Proc. Indian Acad. Sci. A 52(2) (1960): 47-57. Received 14 March 1960. (Read pp. 47-48 as images, 2026-10-12; rest technical.)

**Contemporary primary source: prefer over the 1999 memoir where they differ.**
- 'Since 1955, the Computer Section of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research has been engaged in the development and construction of a large-scale electronic digital computer for doing scientific computations.' (p. 47)
- Pilot 'digital calculator' designed and 'completed in September 1956 and was kept in operation for about a year' (p. 47). [1999 essay: operational by October 1956 (text) / commissioned Nov. 1956 (table). Use Sept 1956 from the 1960 paper; note the variance.]
- Full-scale design began early 1957; 'final assembly completed in February 1959'. 'Unfortunately, owing to the lack of air-conditioning facilities, the work had to be suspended till almost the end of 1959.' Testing began mid-November 1959 'with an auxiliary air-conditioning system'; 'commissioned for routine work in the third week of February 1960' (p. 47). Scene: a finished machine idle for nine months waiting for air-conditioning.
- Parallel, binary, asynchronous; stored programme of single-address instructions; ferrite-core memory of 1,024 locations [later expanded to 2,048: S0216; 1999 table gives 2,048]; input punched paper tape (5-hole teletype tape); output printed or punched (p. 47).
- Emphasis on reliability and ease of maintenance; packaged plug-in units with separate test facilities (pp. 47-48).
- 2,700 tubes (almost all double triodes), 1,700 germanium crystal diodes, c. 12,500 resistors; 'no circuit is transistorised'; power about 18 kW (p. 48) [1999 table: 20 kW].
- 40-bit words (sign + 39 bits), binary fractions.

## S0217 Menon, 'Fancy Calculating Machine', Modern Asian Studies 52(2) 2018 (abstract and endnotes only, via WebFetch 2026-10-12)
- Argument: central planning and the explosion of official statistics justified the pursuit of computers; ISI effectively authored the Second Plan (1956-61).
- URAL: designer Bashir Rameyev (Penza works); formally transferred to Government of India and lent to ISI, government keeping 'complete freedom to utilize the equipment at their discretion' (fn 87, PCMMMA Record 119). ISI house magazine *Samvadadhvam* 2(4), July-Sept 1958 on URAL (also S0001 ref 22).
- Soviet letters went to Mahalanobis directly, irritating diplomat Y.D. Gundevia (fn 58).
- Users of ISI computers: about a dozen institutions incl. Andhra University, IISc, IIT Kharagpur, TIFR, Atomic Energy Commission (fn 92).
- ISI-JU begun 1962, completed 1966, 'unsuccessful' (fn 122). [Other dates: 1964 operational (Wikipedia on Mitra), 1965-66 commissioned (S0010), 1966 (S0001).]
- TIFR CDC machine installed May 1964 for $1.5m, US AID loan (fn 124). [S0013: commissioned mid-June 1964.]
- Planning Commission centre: IBM 1620, 1965, $462,000 Ford Foundation grant (= Rs 23 lakh per S0007), open shop, 'no profit no loss'; one shift a day until 1968, then two shifts (c. 13 hours); obsolete by 1971 (fn 129).
- People: S.K. Mitra (Computer Laboratory); 'Mani Mukherjee', Electronics Division [same as Mohi/Monimohan Mukherjee? check]; Pitambar Pant (ISI cell in Delhi, Mahalanobis's correspondent); D.Y. Panov and engineers Melnicov and Zimarev (Soviet side).
- Morton Nadler: EDP consultant at ISI 1959; lectures spring 1959 -> *Topics in Engineering Logic* (1962); joined Bull, Paris, 1960; later Inria; Virginia Tech c. 1984-91; died 13 Nov 2013 aged 92; memoir *No Regrets* (S0218).
