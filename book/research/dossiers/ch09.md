## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The crisis.** In July 1991 India's foreign-exchange reserves 'would suffice to finance imports for a mere fortnight' (S0088, p. 2). The new finance minister, Manmohan Singh, devalued the rupee on 1 and 3 July (S0088, p. 4); it fell from about Rs 17.5 to Rs 26 to the dollar (S0001, p. 43). He opened the door to foreign investment. In his budget of 24 July he extended the export-income tax concession under section 80HHC to software, to 'capture the overseas software market' (S0088, p. 20).

**What the founders say mattered.** Narayana Murthy names three changes (S0034, pp. 8-9; S0001, p. 44):
- the end of computer import licensing;
- convertibility, which ended Reserve Bank permission for every business trip;
- abolition of the Controller of Capital Issues, which let firms like Infosys sell shares at market prices (Infosys listed in 1993; S0089).

**What did not change, or changed slowly.**
- The Software Technology Parks and their satellite links predated July 1991. The scheme's society was registered on 5 June 1991 (S0091†), though the links worked only from about 1992 (S0254).
- Time-based billing remained the norm: about three-quarters of Infosys revenue in fiscal 1996 (S0089).
- Onsite work fell only slowly, from about 90% of revenue in 1988 to 66% in 1995 (S0254).

**Growth.** Exports rose from $128 million in 1990-91 to $1.76 billion in 1997-98, about 45% a year (S0001, pp. 44, 48).

**Recruitment and pay.** A 1991 Infosys advertisement offered engineers 'in any discipline' a trainee package of 'Rs.45,000 plus' a year. It stated that 'A computer software background is not essential' (S0252). Indian engineers earned 'about 20%' of a Western equivalent (S0001, p. 45).

**Process and the profession.** Process certification (ISO 9001, SEI-CMM) let firms turn large intakes into productive teams quickly (S0001, p. 46). That is the industrial form of the profession the book describes.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 A fortnight of imports

The book's forex thesis finds its sharpest document in the budget speech of 24 July 1991. Manmohan Singh began: 'The new Government, which assumed office barely a month ago, inherited an economy in deep crisis. The balance of payments situation is precarious' (S0088, p. 1). Despite IMF borrowing in July 1990 and January 1991, reserves had collapsed: 'We have been at the edge of a precipice since December 1990 and more so since April 1991' (S0088, p. 1). Then: 'The current level of foreign exchange reserves, in the range of Rs.2500 crores, would suffice to finance imports for a mere fortnight' (S0088, p. 2).

The causes, in his account, were the fiscal deficit, external debt with service at 'about 21 per cent of current account receipts', and 'the Gulf crisis' (S0088, p. 2). Rajaraman adds that India 'was about to default' and was 'bailed out by the International Monetary Fund' on conditions that included devaluation and opening to foreign investors (S0001, p. 43).

The response was a turn in trade and investment policy. 'The exchange rate adjustments on 1st and 3rd July 1991 and the enlargement and liberalisation of the replenishment licence system constitute the two major initial steps' (S0088, p. 4). The rupee fell from about Rs 17.5 to Rs 26 to the dollar (S0001, p. 43). On investment: 'we should welcome, rather than fear, foreign investment. Our entrepreneurs are second to none. Our industry has come of age' (S0088, p. 4). Foreign equity of up to 51% would be approved promptly in priority industries (S0088, p. 4).

### 3.2 Software in the budget

Software got one paragraph. 'Our software industry has made considerable progress in recent years. However, there is still a vast unexploited potential for growth. It is time we make all-out efforts to capture the overseas software market. With this objective, I propose to extend the tax concession under section 80HHC of the Income-tax Act to export of software' (S0088, p. 20, para 78).

Two paragraphs later the minister extended the logic to professionals generally. Their 'skills, knowledge and experience ... have an increasing capacity to earn foreign exchange for the country' (S0088, p. 20). Software entered national economic policy as a source of foreign exchange. It was not an industrial capability in its own right. The framing is continuous with the export obligations of 1974 and 1986 (Chs. 4, 8).

Rajaraman summarises the concessions that followed (S0001, p. 44):
- 'The import duty on computers used for software export was abolished';
- 'Export earnings of software companies were made tax free for 10 years';
- multinationals could set up 100%-owned subsidiaries.

The precise dates and legal instruments of these (the STP and EOU income-tax holidays, the 1992 customs changes) are not yet sourced to primary texts.

### 3.3 What the founders say changed

Narayana Murthy's 2020 account names three reforms (S0034, pp. 8-9; repeated in S0001, p. 44).

1. **The end of computer import licensing.** 'it would take us fifty visits to Delhi and about three years of elapsed time before we got a license to import a computer of about 300,000 US dollars. The government said that was ridiculous. So, they abolished licensing' (S0034, p. 8).
2. **Convertibility.** 'Before that, if I wanted to travel abroad even for a day, I had to apply to the Reserve Bank of India ... wait for about 15 days, get the permission and then travel' (S0034, p. 8). He tells of a fellow entrepreneur who swapped a day in Paris for a day in Frankfurt and 'promptly got a show cause notice from the Reserve Bank' (S0034, p. 9). Rajaraman spells out what convertibility meant for exporters: firms could open offices abroad, pay marketing costs, 'send software engineers to clients' sites at short notice', and hire foreign consultants (S0001, p. 44). For a business that sold onsite labour, that third point mattered most.
3. **Pricing freedom for share issues.** The Controller of Capital Issues 'had no idea what the capital markets were' and priced issues on past performance. So IPOs were usually at par and 'most entrepreneurs were uncomfortable about going public' (S0034, pp. 8-9). After its abolition, 'Many software companies floated IPOs' (S0001, p. 44). Infosys 'completed its initial public offering on the Bangalore Stock Exchange in 1993' (S0089), raising 'approximately $4.4 million in gross aggregate proceeds' (S0089). Murthy recalls that the firm wanted to list in 1991 but postponed after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination. 1992 brought the Harshad Mehta stock scam, and December 1992 the demolition of the Babri Masjid (S0034, p. 17). 'So, we went public on 24th of February 1993. It was a very tough time. We barely managed it. Nobody had any idea what a software company was' (S0034, p. 18). Mastek had listed in December 1992. The shares listed in June 1993 and 'opened pretty well at about 50 percent premium' (S0034, p. 18).

Murthy's conclusion: 'there is absolutely no doubt that for an industry to take off and demonstrate rapid growth you need the blessings of the government' (S0034, p. 8). That is a founder's view, and he was an interested party in the IPO regime. The commonly repeated details of the Infosys IPO price (Rs 95 a share, undersubscribed, rescued by Morgan Stanley) come only from secondary sources and must not be used until confirmed. Murthy's 'We barely managed it' (S0034, p. 18) is consistent with an undersubscribed issue but does not confirm the details.

Rajaraman adds the 1994 National Telecommunication Policy, which let private companies into telecoms. He also notes that 'the government also permitted private software companies to have dedicated satellite links with their overseas customers' (S0001, p. 44); by the late 1990s 'over 200' had done so (S0001, p. 47). The devaluation itself 'enabled Indian software companies to be competitive in selling their services' (S0001, p. 44).

### 3.4 What came before July 1991

Myth M24 (that the industry is 'a product of the 1991 liberalisation') needs the chronology:
- The duty-free import regime for exporters dates from 1986 (S0001, p. 32).
- The first Software Technology Parks opened in 1990: Pune in August, Bangalore in October, Bhubaneswar in December (S0241, p. 97).
- STPI was registered as a society on 5 June 1991 (S0091†), seven weeks before the budget.
- The parks' shared satellite links became operational around 1992 (S0254, p. 5).

So the infrastructure for offshore work predated the reforms, while the financial reforms (convertibility, capital markets, devaluation) came after. Both mattered. The budget's own contribution to software was a tax concession.

### 3.5 Growth, and its measurement

The export series rose steeply. Subhash Bhatnagar's figures, reproduced by Rajaraman (S0001, p. 48):

| Year | Exports (US$m) | Growth |
|---|---|---|
| 1990-91 | 128 | 28% |
| 1991-92 | 164 | 28% |
| 1992-93 | 225 | 37% |
| 1993-94 | 330 | 47% |
| 1994-95 | 450 | 36% |
| 1995-96 | 734 | 63% |
| 1996-97 | 1,100 | 49% |
| 1997-98 | 1,759 | 60% |

Rajaraman calls it 'an average growth of 45% each year' (S0001, p. 44). Heeks's series, from DoE and Dataquest, runs about 5-10% different in several years: $173.9m in 1991/92, $480.9m in 1994/95, $1,650m in 1997/98 (S0085, p. 2). The book must choose one series for the data appendix and note the other (dispute (t)).

The targets set before 1991 were again missed. The World Bank recorded targets of '$400 million and US$800 million by 1992 and 1995' (S0241, p. 96). The $800m mark was passed only in 1996-97.

Employment grew with exports. NASSCOM counted about 56,000 software professionals in fiscal 1990 and about 200,000 in fiscal 1998 (S0089). Its 1996 figure was 160,000 (S0254, p. 5; S0001, p. 43). These are lobby figures, cited in a company filing and an academic paper.

### 3.6 Onsite, offshore and the visa change

The industry's location changed less than its rhetoric. The NASSCOM data used by Khanna and Morales show onsite work at '90% in 1988 to 66% in 1995 and 56% in 2000' of software revenue (S0254, p. 5). Heeks's survey put 1988 onsite development effort at 'just under 75%', and found that 'by 1997/98, it was still true that more than half of all software services export earnings came from onsite work' (S0085, p. 6).

The two 1988 figures measure different things, revenue and effort (dispute (bb)). They agree that most work was still done abroad well into the 1990s.

Two forces pushed work home:
- **Infrastructure.** The STP satellite links from about 1992 (S0254, p. 5).
- **US visa rules.** 'In 1993, the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work, as they were to be paid the prevailing market wage' (S0254, p. 5). Heeks says the same: workers 'were channelled into taking temporary work visas (rather than student or training or even tourist visas as in the past)' (S0085, p. 9).

The H-1B was created in 1990 with a cap of 65,000. In the early years the cap 'was never reached', but by the mid-1990s it 'started binding' (S0254, p. 6). By December 1998 Infosys had 231 people in the US on H-1B visas and 111 on L-1 (S0089).

Rajaraman describes the result, a 'Global Delivery Model' in which 20-30% of project time was spent with the customer and '70 to 80%' of coding and testing was done in India, in client-dedicated secure areas linked by satellite (S0001, pp. 44-45). He credits Murthy with the term. Infosys's 1999 filing claims that 'The Indian offshore development model was initiated in the mid-1980's' (S0089; a company claim). Infosys's filing describes the mechanics as practised by 1999 (S0089, p. 45):
- A 'small team of two to five IT professionals' visits the client to set the scope.
- The project managers 'return to India to supervise a much larger team of 10 to 50 IT professionals'.
- A small team stays onsite to track changes.
- The client's systems are 'linked via satellite' to as many as four Indian centres.
- Maintenance work starts with 10 to 20 people onsite, most of whom then return to India.

'By pursuing this model, the Company completes approximately 80% of its project work at its offshore software development facilities in India' (S0089, p. 45). The same page records the firm as 'Certified under ISO 9001, TickIT and at Level 4 of the Capability Maturity Model' (S0089, p. 45).

That 80% is the leading firm's figure for its own work in 1999. The model described an aspiration and the leading firms' practice. The industry average was still more than half onsite (S0085, p. 6; S0254, p. 5).

### 3.7 Pricing in the 1990s

Time-based billing persisted after liberalisation. In fiscal 1996, 76% of Infosys's revenue came from time-and-materials contracts and 24% from fixed-price; in fiscal 1997 the split was 63% and 37% (S0089). In its 1999 filing Infosys said it was 'seeking to increase the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-time frame rather than a time-and-materials basis' (S0089, p. 44).

Its reasoning bears directly on the book's final question. 'Management believes that effectively structured fixed-price, fixed-time frame projects benefit the client by reducing the client's risk, while offering the Company the potential benefit of enhanced margins for projects that are performed efficiently' (S0089, p. 44). Under time-and-materials, a firm that finishes faster bills fewer hours. Under fixed price, it keeps the saving. Thirty years later, the same distinction decides who captures the productivity gains of AI coding tools: client or vendor (Ch. 19). The 1999 statement is the earliest documented recognition by an Indian firm that the time-based model penalised efficiency. The filing adds that 'The Company's policy is to decline or discontinue projects that do not offer the potential to meet the Company's profit margin targets' (S0089, p. 44).

The decline was slow. Fixed-price work rose from 24% of Infosys revenue in fiscal 1996 to 37% in fiscal 1997, and stood at 36.3% in the nine months to December 1998 (S0089). Time-based billing remained about two-thirds of the leading firm's revenue at the end of the decade. A trade sold by the hour or the month did not quickly become a trade sold by the result.

The filing also records a revealing episode. In 1995 'the Company chose to reduce significantly the services provided to its then-largest client rather than to accept the price reductions and increased Company resources sought by such client'. The client's share of revenue fell from 21.5% in fiscal 1996 to 6.7% in fiscal 1997 (S0089). The filing does not name the client. Murthy does, in his 2020 oral history: General Electric.

**Murthy's account of GE (S0034, pp. 18-20).** Murthy says GE chose four Indian firms after a presentation to Rajiv Gandhi's government. Infosys began working for GE in 1990. GE renegotiated its contracts every five years, and in 'May 1995' it came to do so, when GE 'formed about 25 percent of our revenue'. Murthy describes the scene: 'They sequestered the vendors in different rooms at a five-star hotel in the city. They would go from room to room and would negotiate to get better and better terms' over a Thursday and Friday (S0034, p. 19). 'All other competitors of ours were ready to sign on the dotted line'. Infosys refused, because 'if we reduced our prices to them we would have to reduce prices to our other customers too', leaving less for pay, tools and productivity (S0034, pp. 19-20). Infosys withdrew and transitioned the work to competitors; 'my colleagues were unhappy with me' (S0034, p. 20).

His 25% differs from the filing's 21.5% for fiscal 1996, probably a different period. Murthy also claims that 'until 2007, 2008, probably 2009, Infosys always got 20-25 percent higher price compared to our competitors in India' (S0034, p. 16). That is a founder's claim, unverified.

The episode shows the buyer's power in a trade sold by time. Rates were set in a hotel room, and the client tried to play vendors against each other. A firm selling time could refuse a client's terms only once it had other buyers.

### 3.8 Recruiting the engineer: the 1991 advertisement

The best primary evidence of how the profession was assembled in these years is an advertisement. On 26 November 1991 Infosys took half a page in the Bombay *Times of India* under the headline 'Only 64 brilliant young engineers are destined to conquer the world of software' (S0252).

What it asked for:
- 'young male/female engineers in any discipline (or science graduates plus MCAs)', drawn from 'among the top 10 rank holders in their discipline' with 'an aggregate score of 65%' (S0252);
- 'A computer software background is not essential' (S0252).

What it offered:
- 'You will join us as a Software Engineer Trainee and will be initially based at Bangalore. The gross annual pay package is Rs.45,000 plus' (S0252);
- after twelve months' probation, 'The best can expect to earn a gross annual compensation of Rs.57,000' (S0252);
- 'The job is transferable, so you can look forward to jet setting not only in India, but all over the world' (S0252).

The firm described itself as 'a team of 400' that had grown '100%' in three years, with offices in Bangalore, Delhi, Bombay, Atlanta, Boston and Paris and clients including General Electric and Reebok (S0252). The test was at the Leela Kempinski hotel, Bombay, on Saturday 30 November. The tone was youthful and male: 'Your girlfriend is sure going to miss you next Monday' (S0252). A 2017 website retelling says Murthy recalled 1,500 applicants and a 'mini stampede' (S0253); that is unsourced and must be confirmed.

**What the advertisement shows.** The firm bought general engineering ability and trained it into software. That supports the book's argument that the industry was an 'engineer factory', converting engineers of all kinds into programmers. It also gives a 1991 data point for the pay series: Rs 45,000 a year for a trainee, against HCL's Rs 24,000 a year (Rs 2,000 a month) in 1976 (S0081; Ch. 5). Conversion to constant rupees and to dollars at the annual average rate is a Phase 3 task.

For comparison, a 1989 figure cited by Khanna and Morales from Subramanian (S0025, not seen): 'an Indian computer professional earned about $5486, whereas Microsoft was offering $40,000 plus relocation benefits and a green card' (S0254, p. 5 n. 5). Rajaraman's estimate for the 1990s is that Indian software engineers earned 'about 20%' of a Western equivalent (S0001, p. 45). Infosys's 1999 filing, citing Software Productivity Research, gives about 15% (S0089).

### 3.9 Process: certifying the factory

From the mid-1990s firms sought ISO 9001 and the Software Engineering Institute's Capability Maturity Model. Rajaraman says 'The Motorola (India) software team was the first team in the world to attain this maturity level in 1993' (S0001, p. 45); the date is to be verified (dispute (cc)).

His explanation of why process mattered is central to the book's argument. Certification 'require[d] emphasis on well documented processes which every software engineer in the organization is expected to follow'. And 'The process orientation allowed companies which were recruiting large numbers of employees each year to quickly train them to become productive members of a development team. Staff attrition also could be managed better due to the disciplined software development process' (S0001, p. 46).

Process made engineers interchangeable and so made headcount scalable. That is the same property that makes the work, decades later, legible to automation (Ch. 19). Athreye's argument that tight labour markets drove process capability (S0094, not read) should be tested here.

### 3.10 Y2K and the diaspora

The decade's lucky breaks, in Rajaraman's account:
- **Y2K.** 'Software developed during the 70s and 80s (mostly in COBOL) used 2 digits to represent the year field' (S0001, p. 46). Fixing it was 'labour intensive. There were not enough software engineers in the USA and Europe to fix the bug and it had to be outsourced. This was a godsend opportunity to many fledgling software services companies in India' (S0001, p. 46).
- **The Euro conversion** from 1999 (S0001, p. 47).

He cites Sharma for the claim that 23% of Infosys's 1997 revenue came from Y2K. Infosys's own filing says Y2K was 'approximately 7.5% of revenues for fiscal 1997' (S0089) (dispute (dd); probably a different period). Y2K belongs mainly to Chapter 11.

**The diaspora.** 'The "brain drain" of the 60s and the 70s became "opportunity gain" in the 90s' (S0001, p. 46). Indians in senior positions in American companies gave Indian firms 'small contracts at the beginning which grew as the companies delivered good quality software' (S0001, pp. 46-47). This links back to Chapter 2's IIT emigrants. Rajaraman offers no data, and this should be tested against S0099 and S0100 (diaspora sources in the register).

### 3.11 Multinationals and the first back offices

Liberalised foreign investment brought captive centres:
- American Express's back office in Mumbai in 1994;
- Tata-IBM in 1992;
- Oracle's Bangalore development centre in 1994;
- GE Capital International Services in 1997, for finance, accounting and claims processing, 'called Business Process outsourcing' (S0001, pp. 47-48).

Texas Instruments' Bangalore engineers designed a DSP chip, Ankur, announced in 1997 and 'entirely designed in India' (S0001, p. 48). These are the subjects of Chapters 10 and 15.

### 3.12 Interpretations

- **'1991 made the industry' (M24).** Founders such as Murthy credit 1991 (S0034). Growth rates support a break after 1991 (S0001, p. 48).
- **Continuity reading.** The infrastructure (STPs, 1986 import regime), the business model (time-based onsite work) and the leading firms all predated 1991 (Chs. 4-8). What 1991 changed was money: convertibility, capital markets, the exchange rate.
- **US-demand reading.** Khanna and Morales argue that US demand and the H-1B programme drove Indians into computing, and that those who could not get visas built the Indian industry (S0254, abstract). This is a model-based claim to present as one interpretation.

The book's thesis is supported here in its forex form (software as a foreign-exchange earner, S0088) and its billing form (time-based contracts persist, S0089). It should be qualified by the onsite share's slow fall and the role of US visa rules.
