**Chapter question.** What did Y2K, process certification and the US stock market do to the Indian services firm, and who gained and lost when the boom broke?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Process.** Between 1995 and 2002 the leading Indian firms turned a way of working into a product they could sell and a company they could list. Quality certification came first. ISO 9001 was needed for Europe and SEI's Capability Maturity Model for the US (S0001, pp. 45-46). Rajaraman's own explanation is that process let firms 'recruiting large numbers of employees each year' train them quickly into productive team members (S0001, p. 46). Process was a labour-management system as much as a quality badge.

**The offshore model.** Infosys described its model in 1999 as a pyramid: 'two to five' people at the client, '10 to 50' in India, linked by satellite (S0089, p. 45).

**Y2K.** Y2K arrived on top of growth that was already fast. At Infosys it was about 7.5% of revenue in FY1997, 23.3% in FY1998 and 21.4% in April-December 1998 (S0089, pp. 15, 27-28; resolves dispute dd). NASSCOM put it at 21.1% of 1998/99 exports (S0109; lobby figures). The company told US investors the work carried 'pricing and margins that are favorable' and would 'diminish rapidly' (S0089, p. 15). Y2K opened doors; it did not make the industry (M02).

**Nasdaq.** Infosys sold American Depositary Shares at $34 in March 1999 (S0259). Satyam Infoway followed in October 1999 and Rediff in June 2000 (S0261). Infosys's 1994 stock option plan, 'one of the first' in India (S0089, p. 28), had by 2000 made '1,773 rupee millionaires and 213 dollar millionaires' among its employees (S0262).

**The bust.** It fell unevenly. Sify's ADSs went from $113 to under $1 (S0261). Infosys kept growing, but campus hiring collapsed: Anna University placed about 320 students in early 2002 against 1,200 a year before, and some firms cut promised pay by half (S0263).

**Pricing.** By 2002 Infosys was deliberately moving from time-and-materials to fixed-price work for 'enhanced margins' (S0260). That shift is the hinge to the AI chapters.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The firm in 1995

The Infosys that faced GE in May 1995 was small. Its 1999 SEC registration gives the growth from that base: 'From fiscal 1994 through fiscal 1998, revenues increased from $9.5 million to $63.8 million, the number of the Company's IT professionals worldwide increased from approximately 480 to approximately 2,200, and the number of its India software development centers increased from two to nine' (S0089, p. 27). Revenue grew at a compound 63.6% a year and net income at 46.8% (S0089, p. 5).

Almost all of that revenue was in dollars: 90.0% in FY1998 and 89.2% in the nine months to December 1998 (S0089, p. 12). Most costs, including salaries, were in rupees. The rupee lost about 39.5% of its value against the dollar between December 1994 and December 1998 (S0089, p. 12). This is not a detail. Part of every Indian services firm's margin in these years came from the exchange rate rather than from the work. Indian salaries, paid in a falling currency, became cheaper in dollar terms each year unless raised. The drafting should state this plainly and should not credit all margin growth to productivity or process.

The GE episode belongs to Chapter 9, where it is told from Murthy's oral history (S0034, pp. 18-20). Here it is a bridge. The lesson the company drew was about dependence on one client, and the 1999 filing lists among the causes of fluctuating results 'the size, timing and profitability of significant projects' and 'the accuracy of estimates of resources and time required to complete ongoing projects, particularly projects performed under fixed-price, fixed-time frame contracts' (S0089, p. 10).

Murthy also claims a lasting price premium: 'at least until 2007, 2008, probably 2009, Infosys always got 20-25 percent higher price compared to our competitors in India' (S0034, p. 16). He attributes it to selling on 'Business Value Leverage', that is, on what the client gains rather than on technology. There are no independent price data in hand. This is a founder's self-account and must be presented as such.

### 3.2 Process as product

The 1990s Indian services firm sold certificates as well as code. Rajaraman sets out the sequence. 'All companies entering the export market' obtained ISO 9001, and 'many companies' also sought assessment against the Software Engineering Institute's Capability Maturity Model, whose highest level was Level 5 (S0001, p. 45). 'ISO 9001 certification is essential for bidding for software projects in Europe whereas the CMM maturity level is valued more in the USA' (S0001, p. 46).

His explanation of why process mattered is the most useful passage for this book. 'The process orientation allowed companies which were recruiting large numbers of employees each year to quickly train them to become productive members of a development team. Staff attrition also could be managed better due to the disciplined software development process' (S0001, p. 46). In other words, documented process made individual engineers easier to replace. A firm hiring thousands of new graduates a year, and losing many to rivals or to American visas, needed work broken into steps that any trained graduate could follow. CMM gave clients assurance and gave managers control. The chapter should make this argument with care, because it is an interpretation of Rajaraman's words rather than his conclusion. It connects to Chapter 12 (mass hiring) and to the AI chapters. Work that has been documented, standardised and broken into steps is the work most exposed to automation.

Infosys's own claim, in a sworn filing, was that it had 'been certified at Level 4, a level achieved by only 2% of the more than 1,000 software companies tested under the Capability Maturity Model' (S0089, p. 6). Two cautions apply. SEI appraisals were assessments, often led by authorised lead assessors, rather than certificates issued by SEI; 'certified' is the industry's word and should be attributed. And the 2% figure is the company's.

The count of Indian high-maturity organisations is disputed. Saxenian, writing in 2001: 'In December 1999, 137 Indian companies had obtained either ISO 9000 or SEI-CMM Level 2 certification, and ten companies were certified at Level 5 (the highest level, at which only six U.S. companies are certified.)' (S0255, p. 11). Rajaraman: 'In 1999, 6 out of 12 CMM level 5 certified companies in the world were in India' (S0001, p. 52). These cannot both be right for the same date and definition (new dispute gg). Either way, a handful of Indian organisations held a large share of the highest ratings by 1999. That was a fact the industry marketed hard. Saxenian's next words are that quality certification 'serves as an important marketing device' (S0255, p. 11).

Motorola's claim to be first is the origin story of this race. Its Global Software Group media kit says its India centre achieved 'PMM Level 5' in 1993 and was 'the first commercial organization in the world to reach SEI PMM Level 5' (S0199). Rajaraman repeats the claim (S0001, p. 45). Business Today, as recorded in the register, says first in India, in 1994. No SEI record is in hand. Dispute (cc) and myth M26 stay open, and the drafting must attribute the claim to Motorola.

Suma Athreye's 2005 article (S0094) argues that tight labour markets pushed Indian firms towards process capability. It has not been read. It should not be cited until it is.

### 3.3 The offshore development model

The Infosys F-1 is the clearest contemporary description of how the work was organised, in the company's own words and under securities law. 'The Indian offshore development model was initiated in the mid-1980's as a method of dividing IT project activities between a service provider's offshore software development facility and a client's on-site location' (S0089, p. 45). It lists five attractions for clients in the United States, Europe and Japan:
- 'access to a large pool of highly skilled, English-speaking IT professionals';
- 'relatively low labor costs of IT professionals offshore';
- quality at international standards;
- 'the capability to work on specific projects on a 24-hour basis by exploiting time zone differences';
- parallel processing of project phases.

The shape of a typical project follows. 'In a typical software development or re-engineering assignment, the Company assigns a small team of two to five IT professionals to visit a client's site and determine the scope and requirements of the project.' The project managers then 'return to India to supervise a much larger team of 10 to 50 IT professionals'. A small team stays at the client to track changes. The client's systems are 'linked via satellite'. After testing in India a team returns to install the system, and the company 'will often enter into an ongoing agreement to provide the client with comprehensive maintenance services' (S0089, p. 45).

This is the pyramid, drawn by the company. A few experienced people faced the client and many junior people did the work in India. The ratio is what made the economics work. It is also what made the model so dependent on a constant flow of new graduates, which is the subject of Chapter 12.

Rajaraman calls it 'what is known as the Global Delivery Model (GDM)'. In his account about 20-30% of the time was spent at the client's premises in face-to-face work, and 70-80%, 'primarily coding and testing', in India, in secure areas dedicated to one client, with dedicated satellite links (S0001, pp. 44-45). He adds the '24 hour work day': bugs reported at the end of the American day could be fixed during India's prime shift (S0001, p. 45). The F-1 of 1999 uses 'offshore development model', not 'Global Delivery Model'. The latter is a later Infosys brand, and the drafting should not put it into the company's mouth for the 1990s without a dated source.

The shift was large. Saxenian, citing Parthasarathy, gives the onsite share of Indian software exports as 90% in 1990 and 58% in 1998 (S0255, p. 10). NASSCOM data via Khanna and Morales give 90% (1988), 66% (1995) and 56% (2000) (S0254). The two series are compatible in trend (dispute ff). The Phase 1 chronology also records 57% onsite, 35% offshore and 8% products for 1999-2000 (S0086). Even at the end of the decade, more than half of export revenue was still earned at client sites, which is why the H-1B story (Chapter 13) runs alongside this one.

### 3.4 Y2K: what it was worth

Rajaraman gives the standard account (S0001, p. 46). Programs written in the 1970s and 1980s, 'mostly in COBOL', stored the year in two digits. Fixing them 'was labour intensive'. 'There were not enough software engineers in the USA and Europe to fix the bug and it had to be outsourced. This was a godsend opportunity to many fledgling software services companies in India.' He adds that firms 'earned the goodwill of clients by not only fixing the Y2K bug but also improving the software (when appropriate) at no extra cost', and that 'This opportunity gave many software services companies entry to the Fortune 500 companies and enabled them to get software development contracts later.' He credits Indian programmers 'working diligently in India between 1993 and 1999' with part of the reason the problem did not become a crisis. That is an author's judgement, not a finding, and should be attributed.

The figure most often repeated, that Y2K was 23% of Infosys's revenue 'in 1997', comes from Rajaraman citing a secondary source (S0001, p. 46). The primary figure is in the F-1. 'Year 2000 conversion projects represented 23.3% and 21.4% of the Company's revenues for fiscal 1998 and the nine months ended December 31, 1998, respectively' (S0089, p. 15). Elsewhere the same filing gives about 7.5% for fiscal 1997 (S0089, MD&A). Infosys's fiscal 1998 ran from April 1997 to March 1998, so Rajaraman's '1997' is fiscal 1998. **Dispute (dd) is resolved:** Y2K tripled as a share of Infosys's revenue in one year, peaked at about 23% in FY1998, and was already slightly smaller as a share in 1998-99.

The company was frank with American investors about what kind of business this was. 'The high demand for these time-sensitive projects results in pricing and margins that are favorable to the Company.' It expected demand to 'begin to diminish rapidly after fiscal 1999'. And it warned that 'the Company may be unable to retrain and redeploy IT professionals who are currently assigned to Year 2000 conversion projects involving legacy computer systems after such projects are completed' (S0089, p. 15). The last sentence is the most important for this book. It is a contemporary, sworn admission that one wave of demand had trained a cohort of engineers for work that would end. The parallel with skills built for a single kind of AI-exposed work is direct, and the AI chapters can use it without stretching.

At industry level the figures come from NASSCOM, the industry lobby, as reported by Computergram in June 1999 (S0109). Indian software exports in 1998/99 were $2.65bn, up 51.4%, of which Y2K work was $560m, or 21.1%. NASSCOM also said that 203 of the Fortune 1000 used Indian software services and that the top 25 firms earned 63% of exports. A separate Computergram piece the same month reported NASSCOM's estimate that Indian firms had earned $2.3bn from Y2K, perhaps $3bn by the end of 1999, falling to 'a few hundred million dollars next year'. The cumulative estimate is hard to reconcile with $560m in the peak year unless it spans several years and other categories (new dispute hh). The drafting should prefer the 21.1% share, which is anchored to a single year's exports, and attribute the larger figure to NASSCOM.

NASSCOM's answer to the end of Y2K was already in place. Its president Dewang Mehta spoke of a $50bn export target for 2007/08 and of the 'three Es': e-commerce, ERP and the euro (S0109). Rajaraman records that the EU announced in 1995 that the euro would be used from 1 January 1999, which required banks to modify legacy programs, 'a labour intensive job' that 'provided software conversion contracts to numerous Indian software companies' (S0001, p. 47). No figures for euro work are in hand.

When the millennium passed quietly, the Indian press asked whether the scare had been worth the money. Outlook's 'The Rs 1,800 Cr Hoax?' (c. January 2000) set the world's Y2K spending at $600bn and India's own spending at Rs 1,800 crore (S0108). Its dollar conversion is wrong and must not be used. The piece is useful only as evidence that the question was asked at the time.

**Myth M02 ('Y2K made the Indian IT industry').** Exports grew 28-63% a year before Y2K (S0001, table). Y2K was about a fifth of exports at its peak (S0109) and about a fifth of the leading firm's revenue (S0089). The firm itself expected it to end quickly. What Y2K did, on the best evidence, was open doors: it put Indian firms inside large American companies' legacy systems, and some of those relationships lasted (S0001, p. 46). Verdict stays MIXED, with firmer numbers.

### 3.5 The funnel

Infosys used its hiring statistics as evidence of its brand. In fiscal 1998, 'the Company received approximately 70,870 job applications, tested approximately 21,350 applicants, interviewed approximately 5,800 and extended job offers to approximately 1,790, of whom approximately 1,300 accepted' (S0089, p. 6). The filing ties this to the stock option plan: management believed the company had 'become one of the most sought after employers for Indian engineering graduates', with attrition 'significantly below the industry average'. It added that 'India is home to the world's second largest population of English-speaking technical talent' (S0089, p. 6).

The numbers then exploded. Business Today reported 183,966 applications in 1999 and 259,820 by 1 December 2000, and 2,991 hires in the six months from April to September 2000 (S0262). By fiscal 2002 the company 'received approximately 193,640 applications, interviewed approximately 6,730 applicants and extended job offers to approximately 1,710, of whom approximately 1,550 accepted'. It said it focused 'on the top 20% of students from engineering departments of Indian schools' (S0260, Item 6). About 1.8% of applicants joined in FY1998 and about 0.8% in FY2002. These are company figures, but they show something real: the supply of engineering graduates was rising faster than even the fastest-growing firm could absorb them.

Training was built into the model. In 2002 Infosys employed '57 faculty members, including 39 with doctorate or master's degrees', running 'three-month training sessions for new recruits', and had 'recently set up a 200-acre campus at Mysore' (S0260). Business Today described 'a three-storeyed chrome and glass training centre where fifty of the country's best software instructors put every batch of engineers recruited by the company through a gruelling three-month training program' (S0262). Taken with Rajaraman's account of process, this is the factory of the book's title taking shape: select from a vast pool, train for three months, place into a documented process.

The pyramid had a second effect, which an Infosys executive described without prompting. 'We are growing so fast that many people with two to three years experience are getting into managerial roles,' said Hema Ravichandar, the head of HR (S0262). Rapid growth meant rapid promotion for the people who joined early. That depends on the base of the pyramid growing faster than its middle, which can only go on while hiring grows.

### 3.6 Going to Nasdaq

Infosys had listed in India first. Murthy recalls an IPO 'between 24th and 27th of February' 1993 and a listing in June that 'opened pretty well at about 50 percent premium' (S0034, p. 18). The US listing six years later was a different order of event. The final prospectus of 11 March 1999 priced 1.8 million American Depositary Shares at $34.00 each, with an underwriting discount of $1.68, for $61.2m gross, on Nasdaq under the symbol INFY. The equivalent price of the shares in Mumbai on 10 March was $37.18 per ADS at Rs 42.50 to the dollar (S0259). The American price was set slightly below the Indian one.

The Phase 1 chronology records that Infosys was the 'first India-based company on a US exchange', sourced to the company's press release (S0090), which could not be downloaded. The claim needs checking against the release or an independent source before it is used. It is widely repeated, and the press release's exact wording matters ('Indian-registered' or 'India-based').

Why list in America? The F-1 points to reasons that are in the record:
- the company's long-standing use of US accounting ('one of the first public Indian companies to adopt U.S. GAAP reporting in fiscal 1995', S0089, p. 6);
- the attraction of a dollar-denominated currency for acquisitions and employee options;
- visibility with American clients.

The last two are inferences. The drafting should keep them as such unless the press release or a contemporary interview supplies them.

Satyam Infoway, the internet subsidiary of Satyam Computer Services, came next. 'Our ADSs commenced trading on the Nasdaq National Market on October 19, 1999' (S0261). Its later annual report is a record of the boom and bust in one paragraph: since the IPO, and after a 4-for-1 ADS split in January 2000, 'the trading price of our ADSs has ranged from a high of $113 per ADS to a low of $0.87' (S0261). A securities class action filed on 5 November 2001 named the company, executives and underwriters on behalf of buyers between 20 October 1999 and 6 December 2000, alleging undisclosed arrangements in the allocation of IPO shares (S0261). The suit was part of the wider litigation over technology IPO allocations of that period, which named many issuers; the drafting should say so to avoid implying that Sify alone was accused. Satyam Computer Services owned about 52.5% of Sify and controlled its use of the Satyam name (S0261). That thread runs into Chapter 16.

Rediff followed with its US offering in June 2000 (S0261). The register note on the CNN Money report, not re-read in this session, records 4.6 million ADSs at $12 and a first-day rise of about 61% (S0179). This chapter has no documents on the US listings of Wipro or Satyam Computer Services, or on Silverline. Those are gaps.

### 3.7 Options and the new money

The listing turned paper into wealth for employees, because Infosys had given them paper. 'In 1994, the Company established the ESOP, one of the first employee stock option plans in India' (S0089, p. 28). It was built around an employee welfare trust holding warrants for 750,000 shares (2,000,000 after stock dividends in 1997 and 1998). Employees received rights to buy shares at Rs 100 ($2.35 in February 1999), 'substantially below the market price', with five-year vesting (S0089, p. 28). The F-1 discloses the plan in detail because US accounting required a compensation expense that Indian accounting did not.

By late 2000 the results were striking. Business Today, naming Infosys Best Employer in India, reported that the company had 'managed to offer stock options to 7,853 of its 7,925 employees (as on September 30, 2000) creating, in the process, 1,773 rupee millionaires and 213 dollar millionaires' (S0262). The share price had risen from Rs 63.75 in early April 1996 to Rs 7,160 on 15 December 2000. Sales had grown from Rs 88.97 crore in 1995-96 to Rs 884.35 crore in 1999-2000. The new campus was to cost Rs 320 crore. 'Indeed, a couple of enterprising Bangalore-based real-estate developers make a living primarily from selling apartments to people who work for Infosys' (S0262). A rupee millionaire held at least Rs 10 lakh, a large sum for a young engineer in 2000 but not a fortune; the drafting should give readers the scale.

The magazine also caught the strain. An employee 'who not infrequently puts in 16 hours a day at work' said: 'Every time things get a little too much for me, I go out for a smoke, or a walk, or play a few holes of golf.' Muthamma Acharya, 'a twenty-something marketing executive', said: 'You come in one morning and find the breakfast line 400-people longer' (S0262). The article is a celebratory employer profile, and its quotations were chosen to support that frame; the drafting should note this.

### 3.8 The bust

The bust looked different depending on where one stood. For investors in Indian internet stocks it was a collapse: Sify's ADSs fell from $113 to under a dollar (S0261). For the largest services firm it was a slowdown in a rising line. Infosys's revenue grew at a compound 68.1% a year from fiscal 1998 to fiscal 2002, and its IT staff rose from about 2,200 to about 9,400 (S0260). Its outlook for fiscal 2003 was cautious. 'The economic slowdown in the U.S. has impacted the growth prospects of companies that are in the insurance, banking and financial services and telecom segments', and clients 'may cut their IT spending or postpone decisions regarding new IT expenditures' (S0260).

For graduates it was the worst year in a decade. In January 2002 Business Line reported that Anna University 'was able to place only around 320 students with various companies, down from 1,200 around the same time last year' (S0263). TCS had recruited 54 students against 177 the year before, Infosys 46 against 126, and Cognizant 69 against 105. Dr N. Baluswamy, who ran the university's placement centre, said: 'A few companies have delayed the date of joining for last year students by over a year.' Some IT companies, 'except big players such as TCS, Infosys and CTS', had cut the pay offered on campus 'by 50-60 per cent' (S0263). At IIT Madras, about 30% of students stayed away from campus interviews after 'shocking stories of lay-offs, delayed appointments and salary cuts', and most engineering students 'wanted to consider only engineering companies, rather than shift to IT'. Citibank, not an IT firm, made the highest offer, Rs 6.5 lakh a year (S0263).

This matters for the book's argument because it is the first time the engineer factory ran in reverse. The pipeline of colleges kept producing, while the firms at the end of it cut intake by half or more. The largest firms honoured their offers and kept their pay; smaller firms deferred and cut. The pattern recurs in 2008-09 (Chapter 14) and in the AI-era hiring slowdown (later chapters).

### 3.9 Selling hours, selling outcomes

The most important continuity for the book runs through the pricing language of the filings. In 1999 Y2K work carried 'pricing and margins that are favorable', because it was urgent (S0089, p. 15). In the same filing Infosys said it was 'seeking to increase the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-time frame rather than a time-and-materials basis' (S0089, p. 44), and listed the accuracy of fixed-price estimates as a risk (S0089, p. 10). By 2002 it reported progress: 'we have increased the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-timeframe, rather than a time-and-material basis, which reduce client risk, and if effectively structured and executed, provide opportunities for enhanced margins' (S0260).

The logic is plain. Under time-and-materials billing a firm sells hours, so any productivity gain reduces revenue unless it is used to win more work. Under fixed price the firm sells an outcome and keeps what it saves. Chapter 9 established that Indian contracts were overwhelmingly priced by time through the 1990s (Hanna, Heeks, Infosys's 76% time-and-materials in FY1996). This chapter shows the leading firm deciding, before 2002, that it wanted to sell outcomes instead. When AI tools cut the hours needed for coding and testing (the 70-80% of work done in India, in Rajaraman's account), how a contract is priced decides who captures the gain. The Phase 3 data appendix needs the fixed-price and time-and-materials shares by year from the 20-F series (S0161).
