## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

The Indian Institutes of Technology were designed in 1945-46 to train engineers 'to the standard of MIT' (S0003, p. 787, on the Sarker Committee), but each of the first five had a different foreign patron: the US, UK, USSR and UNESCO at Kharagpur, then the Soviets at Bombay, the West Germans at Madras, the Americans at Kanpur and the British at Delhi (S0003, p. 787). Kanpur, opened in 1960 with 100 students (S0031, p. 11), became the place where computing education in India began, because its first director, P.K. Kelkar, had absorbed American ideas of engineering science and the computer, and because a nine-university American consortium (KIAP) and USAID gave it twice the support of any other IIT (S0003, pp. 790-795).

An IBM 1620 arrived in July 1963 on a chartered DC-7 (S0003, p. 783). American visitors and young Indian faculty with US doctorates ran ten-day courses that trained 600 people from fifty institutions by August 1965 (S0003, pp. 799-800), made computing compulsory for every student from 1964 (S0001; S0003, p. 793), started a master's option in computer science in 1965 (S0043; S0003, p. 793) and India's first undergraduate computer-science degree in 1978 (S0003, p. 806). Students worked the machine through the night (S0003, p. 783; S0223†).

The chapter's tension: the education was built to serve India but became a road to America. The first graduating class (1965) coincided with the US immigration reform of that year (S0003, p. 803). Graduates helped build the ARPANET (S0003, p. 805); of the first 19 computer-science graduates (1983), 14 of 16 traced were in the US by 2007 (S0003, p. 807). Kanpur's graduates also seeded TCS and Infosys through Kohli and Murthy (S0003, pp. 800-801). Language (English), gender (no women in the first class) and cost (foreign exchange for machines) shaped who could enter (S0031, p. 15; S0225†; S0003, p. 794).

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 Designing the IITs: the MIT standard and five patrons

The engineering colleges the British founded trained Indians for subordinate posts (S0003, p. 786). In 1944 the British Nobel laureate A.V. Hill, advising the Government of India, noted that India had no institution like MIT and recommended one or two technical institutes 'of the highest possible standing' (S0003, p. 787, citing Hill, *Scientific Research in India*, 1945). In 1945 a committee chaired by N.R. Sarker, which included two Indians with MIT doctorates, proposed four regional institutions that would train students to the standard of MIT (S0003, p. 787; S0049). Independent India adopted the report and opened the first IIT at Kharagpur in 1951 with support from the US, UK, USSR and UNESCO (S0003, p. 787). For the next four the government arranged one national patron each: the Soviet Union for Bombay (1958), West Germany for Madras (1959), the United States for Kanpur (1960) and Britain for Delhi (1963, originally Delhi College of Engineering) (S0003, p. 787). Fay, writing from Kanpur in 1966, gives the government's reason: it could not reform entrenched university engineering departments, which taught as they had forty years earlier, and any extra money for one university would have to go to dozens of others; new institutes with foreign help allowed a younger faculty and much higher spending (S0031, p. 14).

These choices produced different institutions. Madras, under West German influence, emphasised practical manual training such as blacksmithing and woodworking (S0003, p. 797). Delhi's British faculty ran departments themselves (S0031, pp. 12-13). At Bombay the first director, Brigadier S.K. Bose, a former commandant of a military engineering college, was cautious about computers and in August 1961 declined a Soviet Ural-I as too costly (S0003, p. 796).

### 3.2 The turn to America before Kanpur

Before the Second World War, one to two thousand Indian students studied in Britain each year, against at most 208 in the US (S0003, p. 788). In 1944 the British Indian government began paying for Indian students at American universities, and independent India continued; by 1949-50 there were 1,680 Indian students in American universities and fewer than 700 in British ones (S0003, p. 788). Most government-sponsored students in the US studied engineering or agriculture (S0003, p. 789). Bassett argues that 'Indians educated in the United States would provide the strongest and most enduring technological link between the two countries' (S0003, p. 789).

### 3.3 Kelkar's idea: engineering science and the computer

India and the US had informally agreed by 1958 that the US would support IIT Kanpur, but delays meant the institute opened in 1960 without American help (S0003, p. 789). Its first director, P.K. Kelkar, was an electrical engineer with a 1936 Liverpool doctorate who had taught at the Victoria Jubilee Technical Institute in Bombay and had been chief planning officer for IIT Bombay with its Soviet sponsors (S0003, p. 789). In an unpublished memoir he described an epiphany on reading the proceedings of a 1957 conference of American engineering deans that reorganised curricula around 'engineering science' (S0003, p. 790). That volume had two central themes, engineering science and the computer; one contributor called the computer 'the embodiment of a new attitude toward the utilization of ideas' (S0003, p. 791). Bassett notes that in the US the engineering-science model triumphed partly because schools wanted military research funding, but its rhetoric suited an idealist who had come to engineering through physics (S0003, p. 790). Even before an American programme existed, Kelkar planned to buy an IBM computer with American funds and have Delhi University physicists train his faculty (S0003, p. 791).

Kelkar already had links to the people who would build Indian IT. In the late 1950s he and F.C. Kohli, an MIT-trained control engineer at Tata Electric, started a master's programme in control engineering at VJTI (S0003, p. 791). Kohli later sat on Kanpur's electrical-engineering selection committee, learned computing on Kanpur's 1620 and introduced computing to Tata Electric (S0003, pp. 800-801).

### 3.4 KIAP: money, people and the terms of aid

In January 1961 MIT sent three professors to India; they recommended support based almost entirely on partnership with Kelkar and proposed a consortium informally anchored by MIT (S0003, p. 790). In 1961 the two governments and nine universities agreed to set up the Kanpur Indo-American Program: MIT, Caltech, Carnegie Institute of Technology, Case, Princeton, UC Berkeley, Michigan, Ohio State and Purdue; Stanford declined (S0003, p. 790 n. 12; S0031, p. 11). Early in 1962 USAID contracted with the nine through Educational Services Incorporated, and faculty began to arrive (S0031, p. 11). The programme was to run ten years (S0031, p. 16) and did end in 1972 (S0003, pp. 783, 797).

Fay, a Caltech historian who taught at Kanpur in 1964-66, describes the Americans' position: contract employees of AID, living on AID housing, jeeps and commissary goods, paid in dollars by their home universities (S0031, p. 11); about thirty at the peak, twenty in 1966 (S0031, p. 12). They sat on committees but had no vote on appointments, were rarely consulted on promotions and never headed departments where an Indian could (S0031, p. 13). 'We have influence; we do not have power,' he wrote (S0031, p. 13). He also recorded a first-year student from Delhi who chose Kanpur over the IIT near home because, he said, 'you Americans run this one' (S0031, p. 13). The US contribution to Kanpur was about twice what the Soviets gave Bombay or the West Germans Madras, in equipment and visiting faculty, and three times the British contribution to Delhi (S0003, p. 795, citing Sebaly). By November 1965 more than $4 million of equipment and books had been requisitioned from the US, making Kanpur 'already the best equipped engineering and science school in India' (S0031, p. 16).

### 3.5 Faculty: Indians with American doctorates

Kelkar hired mostly Indians with doctorates from American institutions, often recruited while working in the US (S0003, p. 792). By 1966 almost all of the roughly 75 faculty at assistant-professor level or above were under 40, all held PhDs or equivalents, over half from the US and Canada, a dozen from Britain or Europe (S0031, p. 14). Fay compared Kanpur's mechanical engineering department, where four of twelve lacked PhDs, with Delhi's, where reportedly one of fifteen had one (S0031, p. 14). H.K. Kesavan (MS Illinois, PhD Michigan State, professor at Waterloo) arrived in January 1964 to head electrical engineering and in May became the first head of the computer centre (S0003, p. 792). V. Rajaraman (MS MIT, PhD Wisconsin) arrived in 1963 (S0003, p. 792). H.N. Mahabala and T.R. Viswanathan, with Saskatchewan doctorates, came from Waterloo (S0003, p. 792). Rajaraman and Mahabala learned computing 'on the fly, without formal training' and later spent time at Berkeley and MIT under KIAP (S0003, p. 793).

Abhay Bhushan, Kanpur's first graduate (roll number 60001), remembers that the first year's teachers were largely borrowed from the Uttar Pradesh college system and 'believed in memory and rote, even for math problems', while second-year teachers were new, many with doctorates from the US, Canada and Britain (S0225†, Part I).

### 3.6 Students: who came, and how they lived

The first 100 undergraduates arrived in July 1960 (S0031, p. 11). Bhushan recalls arriving at Kanpur Central 'with a few Naya Paisas in my pocket' and being taken by truck, with 'dreamy-eyed boys who arrived from all parts of the country', to the Government Central Textile Institute hostel in Gwaltoli; classes and labs were at Harcourt Butler Technological Institute; the institute moved to its own campus, beginning in its workshop building, in 1963 (S0225†, Parts I and III). 'There were no girls, not even one among the hundred who started' (S0225†, Part I); in student plays, boys took the women's parts (S0225†, Part III). Fay's 1966 description of the curriculum speaks of the branch 'which the boy or the boy's father has chosen' (S0031, p. 14). Sixty-six of the first hundred graduated in May 1965 (S0031, p. 11).

Admission was by a common entrance examination (S0031, p. 11). English, in which the institute taught, was a filter: Fay reports that only a fraction of students were thoroughly comfortable in it, that the English pass mark in the entrance exam was set deliberately low so as not to exclude promising engineers, and that the first-year course had become largely an English-language course (S0031, p. 15). Bhushan confirms that 'English was our first humanities subject' (S0225†, Part II). The sources read say nothing about students' caste or class; this is a gap (§10).

### 3.7 The computer arrives

The IBM 1620, a small transistorised machine introduced in 1959, cost roughly $320,000 before a 60 per cent educational discount (S0003, p. 791 and n. 15). Its delivery was scheduled for the earliest date a building could house it (S0003, p. 791). In July 1963 a chartered DC-7 landed at a military airbase in Kanpur with the machine, and 'a group of young Indian men muscled the machine into place' (S0003, p. 783). Harry Huskey of Berkeley, who had consulted on ENIAC and worked with Turing, and two Princeton colleagues, Forman Acton and Irving Rabinowitz, had it running for a demonstration to the Board of Governors in late August (S0003, pp. 783, 792). Rajaraman's history dates the installation to August 1963 and calls it the first computer with a FORTRAN compiler in an Indian educational institution (S0001, p. 16). Fay later wrote that the 1620 began 'as an idea in an American's head' and was 'a very great success' (S0031, p. 16).

The three Americans began an informal course for twenty-five specially selected first-year students, who stayed long into the night programming (S0003, p. 783). Ravi Sethi, then a first-year student, took an evening course from Acton after the 1963 midterms; 'Six of us were selected to operate the IBM 1620' (S0223†). He remembers being authorised to switch it on, 'an event in itself', punching programs one line per card, reading the console lights to tell whether a program was looping, and a summer job for Huskey working on a compiler (S0223†). By April 1964 the centre was open to students around the clock (S0003, p. 793), and by February 1964 the machine ran 24 hours a day (S0224†; S0001, p. 17). From spring 1964 every student was trained in computing (S0003, p. 793); Rajaraman calls it a compulsory course in programming and numerical methods for all engineering students (S0001, p. 17). Undergraduates worked at the centre as programmers, among them Ravi Sethi, Jayadev Misra and Raj Kanodia (S0003, p. 793 n. 18).

### 3.8 Missionaries: the ten-day courses

The Americans saw themselves as evangelists for computing: they lectured at TIFR, IIT Madras, ISI, Roorkee, Bombay University and the Physical Research Laboratory and gave a session for fifteen members of the Planning Commission (S0003, p. 799). Their main legacy was a ten-day course for staff of institutions that might use computers: programming (in FORGO, a FORTRAN variant), numerical methods and computer logic, with hands-on labs; about fifty people took each course (S0003, p. 799; S0224†). By August 1965, 600 people from fifty institutions, including other IITs, engineering colleges, national laboratories, government agencies and private firms, had taken it (S0003, p. 800). Indian faculty kept it going after the Americans left in 1965, until 1975 (S0001, p. 17). Bassett, drawing on Mahabala's 1996 reminiscence, says the faculty taught 'with an almost religious sense of mission', driving ten miles to the railway station to collect participants while their wives cooked meals (S0003, p. 800). Kelkar told a Berkeley audience in 1968 that 'there is hardly a computer installation where there is nobody who had been to Kanpur at least once' (S0003, p. 799; S0050).

### 3.9 Teaching computer science as a discipline

By 1965 the electrical engineering faculty had planned a master's degree with a concentration in computer science (S0003, p. 793); IIT Kanpur's obituary of Rajaraman describes the 1965 M.Tech with a computer-science option as 'the first time the subject was offered as an academic discipline in India' (S0043†, quoted via search summary). The 1969 catalogue lists undergraduate and graduate courses including software engineering, information theory, hardware design and image processing (S0003, p. 793). Rajaraman wrote an introductory programming textbook in 1969, *Principles of Computer Programming*, and later FORTRAN and COBOL texts (S0003, p. 793). Bassett notes that Rajaraman and Mahabala focused on teaching and on spreading computing rather than on narrow research (S0003, p. 793).

### 3.10 Obsolete machines and foreign exchange

The 1620 was saturated within a year (S0003, p. 794). In 1966 Kanpur obtained an IBM 7044, one of the most powerful computers in India (S0003, p. 794). Its list price was $3 million; IBM gave the 60 per cent educational discount 'and allowed it to pay in rupees - a major concession to a country for which foreign exchange was precious' (S0003, p. 794 n. 21; Rajaraman says it was bought with Government of India funds, S0001, p. 17). The 7044 was already obsolescent; IBM was phasing it out by 1969 and gave no software help (S0003, p. 794). Students had been learning FORTRAN II, 'almost a dead crow on the world market' in the student newspaper's words, and in 1970 Kanpur undergraduates wrote 'the first compiler written in India' to get newer FORTRAN features on old hardware (S0003, p. 794). When KIAP ended in 1972 amid the worst years of Indo-US relations, no American money came for a new computer, and Kanpur kept the 7044 until 1979, when it was replaced by a DEC-10 (S0003, p. 797 and n. 32).

The contrast with Bombay sharpens the point. IIT Bombay's Soviet Minsk II was delivered in 1965 through UNESCO but stood in storage for two years while funding for Soviet technicians was negotiated; it ran from January 1968, used the Soviet ACE language rather than FORTRAN, and was a one-off in India (S0003, p. 796). In 1971 Mahabala persuaded the West Germans to spend their computer grant for IIT Madras on an IBM machine rather than a German one with German experts; the IBM 370/155 inaugurated in November 1973 was the most powerful computer in India (S0003, p. 798; S0041, p. 25).

### 3.11 The wider computing scene

Bassett frames the wider context: national enthusiasm favoured atomic power, steel and dams; the labour-saving computer was 'a problematic technology in a country that had a surplus of labor, prized self-sufficiency, and put little stock in international trade' (S0003, p. 798). In the early 1960s IBM installed computers at laboratories and large firms, mainly for record-keeping, and unions called them 'job eaters' (S0003, p. 799). A 1974 survey found 217 computers in India, 65 per cent IBM, 13 per cent ICL and 11 per cent ECIL (S0003, p. 800). Bassett concludes that an American-oriented engineering education and an American computer firm 'did reinforce each other' (S0003, p. 800).

### 3.12 The road out: migration

The students, Bassett argues, partly frustrated the plan for an Indian institute that would solve Indian problems, by making Kanpur 'the Indian MIT' and a step to American graduate school (S0003, p. 802). Foreign students in the US rose from 26,759 in 1948 to 100,262 in 1966; almost 7,000 Indians studied there in 1965-66, and at MIT in 1966 only Canada sent more students than India's 82 (S0003, p. 802). Before 1965 a discriminatory quota limited permanent Indian immigration; in 1965, the year of Kanpur's first graduating class, President Johnson signed reform that made residence much easier (S0003, p. 803). Bhushan went to MIT on a research assistantship (S0225†, Part III). Roughly a third of Kanpur's first (1965) batch lived in the US in 2005 (S0003, p. 803 n. 50). A study of IIT Bombay found slightly over 40 per cent of its electrical-engineering graduates of 1973-77 abroad, mostly in the US, and that 90 per cent of IIT Bombay's 1970s brain drain went to the US (S0003, pp. 803, 807 n. 55; S0046). Whatever an IIT's foreign patron, the vast majority who went abroad went to America (S0003, p. 807). A 1969 British memo noted IIT Delhi's large drain to the US and doubted Britain could change it (S0003, p. 808 n. 55).

Kanpur graduates moved quickly into the centre of American computing. Request for Comments 176 of the ARPANET's Network Working Group (14 June 1971) lists A. Bhushan and R. Kanodia of MIT alongside Robert Metcalfe and Jon Postel; Bhushan chaired the group that defined the File Transfer Protocol (S0003, p. 805). In 2008, 38 faculty in America's ten top computer-science departments (1993 rankings) had Indian undergraduate degrees: 11 from IIT Madras and 10 from IIT Kanpur (S0003, pp. 805-806).

### 3.13 The first computer-science degree, 1978

Until 1978 computer science at Kanpur lived inside electrical engineering; in 1978 the institute created a B.Tech in computer science, the first such degree at any IIT (S0003, p. 806; S0001). It was the most sought-after seat in India: the last of 20 places was filled by JEE rank 44, so nearly half of the top forty candidates in the country chose it; in 1988 the 14 seats were filled by rank 38 (S0003, p. 806). Over 100,000 candidates sat the IIT entrance exam for about 1,500 seats in 1978 (S0001). Of the 19 graduates of 1983, 17 were traced in 2007: one had died, 14 lived in the US, one in the Czech Republic and one in India; nine had doctorates, five held US computer-science faculty posts, six lived in the Bay Area and at least three had founded companies; one, Rajeev Motwani of Stanford, was an early mentor to Larry Page and Sergey Brin (S0003, p. 807). Some Kanpur faculty tried, without success, to stem the flow by refusing to write recommendation letters (S0003, p. 807).

### 3.14 The road to industry

Kanpur had no IT industry of its own (S0003, p. 800). Its influence ran through people. Kohli, who led TCS for decades, had learned computing on Kanpur's 1620; Mahabala consulted at TCS in summers; Kohli often hired Kanpur's computing master's graduates (S0003, pp. 800-801). N.R. Narayana Murthy came to Kanpur from Mysore for an M.Tech in electrical engineering and got 'hooked' on computing, then worked with the MIT-trained Jashwant Krishnayya at IIM Ahmedabad on a Ford-funded HP computer, and later for Narendra Patni, before founding Infosys with six Patni colleagues in 1981 (S0003, p. 801). Bassett's judgement is that Kanpur's graduates had the skills while men like Patni and Kohli had the American business connections (S0003, p. 802).

### 3.15 Education and profession in this chapter

*Education* is the core: who was taught (a hundred young men a year at first, admitted by a national exam, in English), by whom (Indian faculty with North American doctorates and visiting Americans), what (engineering science, compulsory programming, then a computer-science master's option and in 1978 a degree) and at what quality (the best-equipped institute in India by 1965, but with obsolete machines after 1966) (S0003; S0031; S0001). *Profession*: there were few computing jobs in India in the 1960s; the documented destinations of Kanpur's computing graduates were American graduate schools and laboratories, TCS, IIM Ahmedabad and Patni (S0003, pp. 800-807). How the work paid is not documented in sources read.

## 6. Disputes

1. **1620 arrival vs installation.** July 1963 arrival (S0003, p. 783), demonstration late August (S0003, p. 792); 'installed in August 1963' (S0001). **Resolved:** both right.
2. **Size of Huskey's first course.** 25 first-year students (S0003, p. 783) vs 'about 50 participants in each course' (S0224†) vs an unverified 'three batches of 20' (search snippet). These describe different courses: the informal student course (25) and the ten-day outside course (about 50). **Resolved** with that distinction; do not use the batches-of-20 claim.
3. **When TCS was set up and when Kohli took over.** Bassett: Tata 'established a computing enterprise in 1967' and Kohli moved in 1969 (S0003, p. 800). TCS: 1 April 1968 (S0039). Kohli director-in-charge 1974 (S0038). **Unresolved**; Ch. 4 must settle.
4. **First CS degree teaching.** 1965 M.Tech option (S0043, S0003) is 'first time as an academic discipline'; 1978 B.Tech is the first undergraduate CS degree at an IIT (S0003). Not a conflict, but the book must keep the two firsts distinct. Myth check: no other Indian institution offered CS before 1965 in sources read (search further).
5. **Was KIAP American control or Indian choice?** Fay: Americans had 'influence', not 'power' (S0031, p. 13); a student thought 'you Americans run this one' (S0031, p. 13); Bassett: Indian elites chose and shaped the alignment (S0003, p. 786). Present both.
6. **Myth M14 (IITs modelled on MIT).** The Sarker blueprint set MIT as the standard (S0003, p. 787), but four IITs had non-US patrons with different curricula (S0003, pp. 787, 796-797). Revised provisional verdict: MIXED (the aspiration was MIT; the practice varied by patron; Kanpur and later Bombay adopted American models).

## 7. Documented scenes

1. **The DC-7 at the airbase, July 1963.** A chartered plane lands at a military airbase in Kanpur with an IBM 1620; young Indian men muscle it into place; Huskey is waiting (S0003, p. 783; photo in Dahl 1967 via S0003 fig. 1).
2. **Arrival at Kanpur Central, July 1960.** Bhushan steps off the train with a few naya paise and is driven by truck with boys from across India to a borrowed textile-institute hostel (S0225†, Part I).
3. **Kelkar reads a conference report.** Before any American aid, the director reads the 1957 American deans' proceedings and decides Kanpur will be built around engineering science and the computer (S0003, pp. 790-791; source: Kelkar's unpublished memoir).
4. **Switching it on.** Ravi Sethi, one of six students chosen to operate the 1620, is authorised to turn the machine on; reading console lights to spot a looping program (S0223†).
5. **The night programmers, 1963-64.** Twenty-five selected first-years stay long into the night programming; by April 1964 the centre is open round the clock (S0003, pp. 783, 793).
6. **Picking up students at the station.** Kanpur faculty drive ten miles to the railway station to collect participants for the ten-day course while their wives cook (S0003, p. 800, from Mahabala's reminiscence).
7. **Paying in rupees, 1965-66.** IBM sells Kanpur a $3 million 7044 at a 60 per cent discount and lets it pay in rupees (S0003, p. 794 n. 21).
8. **The Minsk in storage, 1965-68.** At IIT Bombay a Soviet computer sits crated for two years while the USSR and UNESCO argue over who pays its technicians (S0003, p. 796).
9. **Blackout, September 1965.** During the war Kanpur is blacked out; Americans in the Punjab are ordered to Delhi (S0031, p. 13).
10. **A compiler for a dying machine, 1970.** Undergraduates write India's first compiler to bring newer FORTRAN to the obsolete 7044 (S0003, p. 794).
11. **RFC 176, 14 June 1971.** Two Kanpur graduates' names on an ARPANET protocol document beside Metcalfe and Postel (S0003, p. 805).
12. **Rank 44, 1978.** The last of 20 places in India's first undergraduate computer-science course goes to the 44th-ranked candidate in the country (S0003, p. 806).
