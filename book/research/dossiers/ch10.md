**Chapter question.** Why did the software trade gather in Bangalore, and what did it do to the city and the people in it?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Public-sector origins.** Bangalore's technical base was built by the state after 1947. Defence and electronics firms (HAL, BEL, LRDE) and research institutes (IISc, later ISRO) were placed there, partly for strategic distance from the borders (S0034, pp. 10-11; S0249). The city grew fastest in the 1970s, 76% in a decade, on public-sector employment. That was before software (S0258†).

**Why software came.** Bombay was the software centre until the mid-1980s (S0085, p. 12). Firms then looked south. Murthy says Infosys left Pune because Maharashtra's tenancy law made renting impossible for a start-up, and chose Bangalore for its engineers (S0034, pp. 10-11). Heeks's interviews give four reasons:
- labour;
- quality of life, including climate and 'pub culture';
- infrastructure;
- proximity to previous employers once the cluster formed (S0085, p. 13).

Texas Instruments' satellite earth station of 1986, which required 'removing or breaking 25 different government rules' (S0255, p. 6), started the 'snowball effect' (S0085, p. 13). By 1998 Bangalore was home to 152 of 558 software firms' headquarters (S0085, p. 12).

**Wages and productivity.** In 1994 an Indian programmer's wage was 'less than one-tenth' of an American's (S0255, p. 9). By 1998 revenue per software worker was $15,000-20,000, against $100,000 in Israel or Ireland (S0255, p. 12).

**Hyderabad.** Under Chandrababu Naidu it competed with CyberTowers (1998) and Microsoft's development centre (S0119).

**What it did to the city.** Saxenian warned in 2001 that IT was becoming 'a small, modern enclave' insulated by private power and data links (S0255, p. 20). A study of Bangalore's slums found nearly a million poor in slums, and growth 'capital intensive rather than labour intensive' (S0257, pp. 8, 12). Of about 50 slum youths with computer training, the women found work as receptionists and cashiers; most remained under-employed (S0257, pp. 25-26).

**Gap.** The professionals' own voices are still missing.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The state's city

Bangalore's technical base came before software, and it came from the state. Narayana Murthy's account: 'right from 1947 when we got independence, the government of India decided to locate its defense oriented high-tech companies in Bangalore, which was away from the border with Pakistan and with China'. He names LRDE, Bharat Electronics and Hindustan Aeronautics, and space research 'in the late '70s' (S0034, pp. 10-11). His strategic explanation (the city's distance from both coasts meant an attacking plane 'would be shot down') is his recollection of the rationale, not a documented policy. It should be presented as his.

A social-science account of the city's slums gives the same picture: post-independence Bangalore was 'a garden city of retirees' bunglows, and ... a significant public sector presence', with 'premier scientific research institutions' set up by the centre. 'The city's development in the first four decades was closely aligned with the development of the state sector' (S0257, p. 1).

An IISc research page records that the city's population grew 76% in 1971-81, a rise 'ascribed to numerous public sector industries'. In 1991-2001, the software decade, growth was 38% (S0258†). Software did not create the city's growth. It inherited a city that the public sector had already made large and skilled. The Census urban-agglomeration series should be used to confirm the figures in Phase 3.

The private technology community also predated the multinationals. A journalist who knew the city in the 1970s recalls Vinay Deshpande at Processor Systems India, in a basement on Richmond Road, 'putting together hi-tech firmware, bound for the US' (S0249). Vijay Chandru, a former IISc professor: 'Bangalore was a tech city well before the arrival of the MNCs' (S0249). Wipro's computer business was designed in IISc's Digital Systems Lab in 1979-81 (S0069; Ch. 5).

### 3.2 From Bombay to Bangalore

The software trade began in Bombay. Heeks, who interviewed in the industry from 1988, writes: 'The software industry started in Mumbai, in the business district around Nariman Point in the late 1970s. When prices and commute times began to rise there, part of the industry decamped up to the Santa Cruz Electronics Export Processing Zone (SEEPZ)', and Mumbai 'was still the centre of the software industry until at least the mid-1980s' (S0085, p. 12).

Then 'Software firms were fast running out of labour, partly due to the overseas brain drain, plus high prices in the city and poor quality of life' (S0085, p. 12). Firms and investors began comparing locations.

Murthy gives the most specific reason for one firm's move. Infosys was founded in Pune in 1981, but 'Maharashtra was not the best place to have a startup because the tenancy laws in Maharashtra' protected long-term tenants so strongly that 'most landlords in Maharashtra refused to give an apartment on rent ... Unless you were a big company ... But no startup could do that' (S0034, p. 10). Karnataka's law was 'very fair between the landlord and the tenant', so the choice narrowed to Chennai or Bangalore. Bangalore won 'because of the availability of engineering talent': the defence public sector, and the fact that 'Karnataka had a disproportionately higher percentage of engineering talent and engineering students in the country' (S0034, pp. 10-11). Saxenian later reported, citing Dossani, that 60% of India's computer science graduates came from Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu (S0255, p. 14 n. 27). The private engineering colleges of Chapter 7 are part of that supply.

### 3.3 Why Bangalore: the four reasons, and the climate question

Heeks's fieldwork gives the most systematic answer. Using Haug's model of software location, he identifies (S0085, p. 13):
- **Labour availability:** 'abundant labour that could be drawn from its long-standing research laboratories, educational institutes and public sector electronics firms'.
- **Quality of life:** 'a good climate and social life'. IT workers joined 'the city's "pub culture"'. He contrasts Mumbai's 'long and troubled history of both communal and labour relations strife'.
- **Infrastructure:** 'You could get across town in a matter of minutes in a car', and there was 'a plentiful supply of both power and water'.
- **Proximity to previous employer and residence:** firms were founded by people leaving existing firms nearby, 'self-reinforcement of location'.

He adds that 'the perception of these factors as much as their reality has coloured locational decisions' (S0085, p. 13).

So the popular claim that Bangalore was 'chosen for its climate' (myth M17) is partly true and mostly misleading. Climate appears in Heeks's list as part of a perceived quality of life. C.S. Murali of IISc also lists the 'salubrious climate' among several draws (S0249). But Murthy's account of Infosys's choice does not mention it (S0034). Every account gives labour first. M17 stays MOSTLY FALSE as the main reason.

The infrastructure advantage was temporary. By 1998 the STPI director in Hyderabad was predicting that 'Other cities will be left behind when traffic-congestion and urban chaos overtakes them' (S0119). Saxenian reported in 2005 that engineering salaries in Bangalore were 'now among the highest' in India, with 'intensifying congestion' (S0256, p. 6).

### 3.4 The snowball: TI and the multinationals

Heeks treats Texas Instruments as 'a key event in Bangalore's history'. It set up operations in 1986 as 'the first proper offshore software facility, using satellite-based telecommunication links', and this 'was constantly reported in the home and international media'. 'TI was known to have cut through many red tape barriers, particularly on imports, and this further encouraged firms to go to Bangalore' (S0085, p. 13).

Saxenian, drawing on a 1996 interview with N. Seshagiri, adds a measure of the red tape: 'When Texas Instruments set up the first earth station in Bangalore in 1986, for example, the process involved removing or breaking 25 different government rules' (S0255, p. 6). The dish's arrival by bullock cart at Sona Towers is in Chapter 8 (S0249; S0250).

The multinationals that followed explained themselves in terms of skill and risk. A US employer in Bangalore, interviewed in 1996: 'we came here because of the skills. We expanded because of the skills. We were able to come to India because the risk of being 10,000 miles away, the risk of the satellite link and the telephones and the flights were offset by the costs' (S0255, p. 11). The head of another offshore development centre said that five years earlier he would have advised 'do anything, bodyshopping, subcontracting, modular work, but don't give full dependability here [in India] because nobody's ever done it. It's not proven'. By the late 1990s: 'Technologically, there's zero difference' (S0255, p. 11). Both are anonymous executives quoted second-hand from Parthasarathy's interviews.

Oracle opened a development centre in Bangalore in 1994 (S0001, p. 47). TI's Bangalore engineers designed a DSP chip, Ankur, announced in 1997 (S0001, p. 48). By 1998 Bangalore was headquarters to 152 of 558 Indian software firms, ahead of Mumbai's 122 (S0085, p. 12). The Mumbai figure understates its weight, since several large firms remained there.

The parks multiplied. Saxenian records that the DoE 'announced the first three in 1990 in Bangalore, Pune and Bhubaneshwar, and another four the following year'. By 1998 there were '25 STPs under various stages of planning and development' in addition to the DoE's own (S0255, p. 7). In Bangalore the new form was a private-public estate: 'The Information Technology Park, Ltd. in Bangalore, for example, is a partnership between the Karnataka government, Tata Industries and a consortium of Singapore firms' (S0255, p. 7).

STP firms had duty-free imports and five-year tax exemptions. They also carried an export obligation: they had to 'earn a net amount equal to 150% of the hardware imported within four years' and 'a net amount equal to 150% of their wage bill on an annual basis'. From January 1995 they could sell 25% of output in India, raised to 50% in 1999 (S0255, p. 7 n. 10). The wage-bill obligation is a direct statement of the forex bargain: a park firm had to earn back its payroll one and a half times in foreign exchange.

In Karnataka the number of software export companies rose from 13 to 1,154 between 1991 and 2003, and software exports from Rs 5.6 crore to Rs 12,350 crore (S0257, p. 9, citing the Karnataka Development Report 2007).

### 3.5 The rival: Hyderabad

Hyderabad's challenge was a political project. *Business Today*'s December 1998 cover story describes Chief Minister Chandrababu Naidu, 48, opening CyberTowers in November 1998 with the line: 'Until now, the world has heard of the twin cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad. From today, the world will know of the triplet cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad-Cyberabad' (S0119). The magazine notes that 'the late 1980s saw Bangalore overtaking Hyderabad' as South India's most dynamic city (S0119).

Naidu's coup was Microsoft. 'When Naidu got Microsoft's CEO, Bill Gates, to locate its R&D centre at CyberTowers, he made sure no other company could ignore the city' (S0119). Oracle, BaaN, Tata IBM, Satyam and Wipro were present by then.

The article recorded doubts as well:
- water shortages;
- growth that was 'haphazard';
- the urban planner V.K. Bawa's verdict: 'The model of Hyderabad's development is regressive; it does not conform to modern concepts of urban growth' (S0119);
- 'If he loses, the imponderables are many' (S0119), on the dependence of 'Vision 2020' on one politician.

Saxenian (2001) notes Naidu's promotion of 'e-governance', and that state governments 'have pioneered some of the most far-reaching policy innovations in the IT sector' (S0255, p. 13). Microsoft's own 1998-99 releases (S0195) were blocked and should be read in Phase 5.

### 3.6 Wages, productivity and the cost of the model

Saxenian's 2001 paper gives the economics behind the cluster:
- 'In 1994, wages for software programmers and systems analysts in India were less than one-tenth of those for their US counterparts, and lower even than other developing countries like Mexico' (S0255, p. 9).
- India employed about 180,000 software workers in 1998, with 'annual revenue per employee in India was $15,000-20,000. This compared to $100,000 per employee in other software producing countries such as Israel and Ireland' (S0255, p. 12).
- NASSCOM estimated that software wages rose '21% per year in the late 1990s, albeit from a low base' (S0255, p. 13).

Saxenian also describes body shopping in terms that differ from Chapter 6's evidence: 'In these contracts, the amount of software code is specified in advance and revenue is earned per line of code' (S0255, p. 10). The World Bank and Heeks describe hourly, monthly or time-and-materials billing (S0241, p. 31; S0085, p. 7). Saxenian gives no source for her sentence. The book should note the dissent and weigh it below the direct evidence (dispute (ee)).

Mastek's chairman, Ashank Desai, said in 1999: 'it is becoming difficult for them to compete only on one differential—the cost advantage' (S0255, p. 13). The large firms, Saxenian writes, sought turnkey work: 'Rather than competing on the basis of hourly productivity they aim to accumulate intellectual property' (S0255, p. 12). That is another contemporary acknowledgement that the trade was priced by time.

### 3.7 The enclave

By 2001 Saxenian saw the cluster's insulation from the rest of India as its main risk. 'Already most IT development occurs in STPs that are insulated from the day-to-day challenges of doing business in India by dedicated communications links, private power sources, and liberal rules for investment and taxation.' 'Professionals in IT enclaves could become better connected, both economically and socially, to distant regions than to the rest of the Indian economy' (S0255, p. 20).

She cited warnings of a 'Dutch Disease', with IT dollars strengthening the rupee against other sectors, while IT remained 'under 1% of GDP' (S0255, p. 20). Infrastructure explains the insulation. In 1997 India had '18.6 telephone mainlines per 1000 people ... compared to 55.7 in China, and the wait for a new connection was 12.17 months' (S0255, p. 16).

### 3.7a The road not taken: building for India

Saxenian's 2001 paper ends with an argument the book should carry forward. The export model left Bangalore's design capability pointed at foreign clients. India 'already has the design capabilities' for products of its own, visible 'in the growth of VLSI design in the ODCs, as well as in the activities of some small indigenous firms like Bangalore-based Silicon Automation Systems and Encore' (S0255, p. 24). 'However India lacks the environment needed to support experimentation with the application of these skills to new markets' (S0255, p. 24).

Her example was the Simputer, 'a very low-cost mobile personal computer (priced at under Rs 9,000, or approximately US $200) that was developed by a Bangalore-based team of engineers' from IISc's Department of Computer Science and Automation and Encore Software (S0255, p. 23). It ran on Linux, was 'designed to be open and modular', and included a smart-card reader for financial transactions. Encore was Vinay Deshpande's firm, the engineer whom Roye recalled soldering firmware in a Richmond Road basement in the 1970s (S0249; S0001, ref. 73). Saxenian calls the IISc-Encore collaboration 'rare in India' (S0255, pp. 23-24). Another Bangalore firm, Innomedia, developed low-cost interactive television over cable and partnered with Reliance for volume production (S0255, p. 25). What happened to the Simputer is a Chapter 17 question (the product problem).

She also quoted Ashok Jhunjhunwala's argument (December 1999) that a pager selling for Rs 2,200 could be made locally for Rs 100 and be 'affordable to 20% of the Indian population' (S0255, p. 23 and n. 43).

Her policy conclusion: the export-oriented approach 'risks accelerating the growth of IT as a small, modern enclave in a poor and backward economy' (S0255, p. 20). It needed 'leveling the playing field so that IT products and services sold domestically enjoy the same tax benefits as those currently enjoyed by software export units' (S0255, p. 23). This is the forex thesis seen from Bangalore. The tax and infrastructure regime built to earn dollars made exporting easier than building for India.

The concentration of the industry reinforced the pattern. 'the ten largest firms account for more than fifty percent of software exports', among 826 exporting companies (S0255, p. 24 and n. 44, citing NASSCOM 1999). In Bangalore, as nationally, a few large exporters dominated a long tail of small firms (S0085, p. 11; S0241, p. 31).

### 3.8 The city's other economy

What did the boom do for Bangalore's poor? Supriya RoyChowdhury's study of six inner-city slums near Lalbagh (300 households) gives a sober answer:
- The city 'has now become the icon of India's information technology revolution'. Real estate prices 'soared', and public-sector land was 'put up for sale' as units closed and workforces were 'downsized and contractualized' (S0257, p. 1).
- Growth came 'through the expansion of capital intensive rather than labour intensive industries, as well as skill-based industries, which offered limited scope of employment, and only to technically skilled personnel' (S0257, p. 8).
- The poor found work in construction and the export garment industry, 'at low wages', and in new service jobs: 'drivers of cars and vans run by BPOs and Call Centres ... security and maintenance personnel in malls and super markets'. That work 'remains in the casual/contract mode with no security of service, insurance or pension' (S0257, p. 8).
- 'In Bangalore, nearly one million poor live in slums, and about one third of slum dwellers fall below the poverty line, with a monthly income of less than Rs 2500 (US$55)' (S0257, p. 12).

The study records how the IT economy reached one slum. In K.S. Garden, 'About 50 youths in the slum had managed to get some kind of computer training. Of these, some 25-30 women were working as receptionist in small offices, or showrooms, at cash counters in small super markets' (S0257, p. 25). But 'even those with some computer training frequently remained unemployed or only partially employed'. Where someone had gained a foothold, 'a connection through family or network had worked in most cases; there had not been any systemic or policy based effort to incorporate the urban underclass into the city's growing economy' (S0257, pp. 25-26).

These are the chapter's closest approach to ordinary residents' voices, reported collectively by the researcher. The paper is undated (PDF created 2011) and its data are mostly from the 2000s.

### 3.9 Interpretations

- **Agglomeration reading.** Public-sector skill plus early movers (TI, Infosys, Wipro) produced a self-reinforcing cluster (S0085, p. 13; S0034).
- **'Silicon Valley of Asia' reading.** Saxenian called the comparison 'not only misleading but also distracts attention from the deeper challenges and opportunities that IT affords the Indian economy' (S0255, abstract). Comparisons between Bangalore, 'where future IT growth depends either on continued supplies of low cost of skill or on shifting into higher value-added activities', and Silicon Valley 'remain premature at best' (S0255, p. 19).
- **Enclave and inequality reading.** The industry created an insulated professional economy with few links to the city's poor (S0255, p. 20; S0257). Nair (S0101) and Heitzman (S0102), not read, are the urban-history accounts to test this against.

### 3.10 Profession and lives

The chapter's outline promises 'profession and lives'. The sources in hand describe the profession from outside:
- wage ratios, revenue per head (S0255);
- the transferable job and 'jet setting' promised in recruitment (S0252; Ch. 9);
- engineers' hope of transfer abroad: 'Many thus aspire to work for foreign companies not only for the relatively high wages but also for the opportunity be transferred overseas' (S0255, p. 12).

Indian H-1B holders reached 47% of all H-1B visas in 1999, 55,047 people, and 195,083 over 1989-99 (S0255, p. 12; Ch. 13).

The supply side was large. Saxenian reports that Indian institutions trained 'more than 67,000 computer science professionals annually' and that 'Another 200,000 individuals enroll annually in the private software training institutes that have mushroomed in India in the 1990s' (S0255, p. 10, citing NASSCOM 1999; Ch. 7). Many of them came to Bangalore and Hyderabad, which together with Chennai supplied 60% of the country's computer science graduates (S0255, p. 14 n. 27).

No engineer living in Bangalore speaks in their own words in the sources found so far. Upadhya's ethnography (S0095) and the 2006 NIAS study are the obvious sources and must be acquired.

## 9. Sidebar: 'Why did Ireland get the factories and India the programmers?'

Evidence in hand:
- revenue per software employee: about $100,000 in Ireland and Israel against $15,000-20,000 in India, 1998 (S0255, p. 12, citing Arora et al. 2000);
