## 1. The law

**1990 Act (S0105, IF12912 p. 1).** 'The H-1B program traces its roots to the Immigration Act of 1990 (P.L. 101-649)'. Specialty occupation means 'theoretical and practical application of a body of highly specialized knowledge' plus a bachelor's degree or equivalent. Status lasts up to three years, renewable for three more. 'Current law generally limits the number of individuals who are annually provided H-1B status to 65,000'. The cap 'is the same now as when it was established in 1990'. Congress raised it temporarily in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

**Labour condition application (IF12912 p. 1).** The employer attests to paying 'the greater of the actual wages paid to similar employees or the prevailing wages', no adverse effect on working conditions, no strike, and notice given. 'The LCA is a statement of intent rather than a documentation of actions taken.' This matters: the safeguard is an attestation, checked mostly after complaints.

**Cap history (RL30498, 2007).**
- 'The INA sets a 65,000 numerical limit on H-1B visas that was reached for the first time prior to the end of FY1997, with visa numbers running out by September 1997.' The FY1998 ceiling was reached in May 1998.
- 1998 legislation (the American Competitiveness and Workforce Improvement Act, ACWIA) raised the cap to 115,000 for FY1999-2000. 'that annual ceiling of 115,000 visas was reached months before FY1999 and FY2000 ended.'
- The 106th Congress (AC21, 2000) raised it 'to 195,000 for three years' (FY2001-2003) and expanded training programmes.
- 'When the H-1B annual numerical limits reverted to 65,000 in FY2005, that limit was reached on the first day.'
- P.L. 108-447 (2004) exempted 20,000 holders of US master's degrees.
- The FY2008 cap was reached on 2-3 April 2007, with about 119,193 petitions received in two days.
- The INS 'admitted in autumn 1999 that thousands of H-1B visas beyond the 115,000 ceiling were approved in FY1999'.
- 'H-1B dependent' firms (generally 15% or more of workforce on H-1B) must attest that they tried to recruit US workers.
- Note: one sentence in the CRS text says the FY1999 ceiling 'was reached in June 2002'. This is a typo in the source; context shows June 1999. Do not quote it.

**Fees (S0268, para II.B.1, 2013 values).** Base fee $325; $500 fraud fee; $1,500 ACWIA training fee; and $2,000 for firms with more than 50 US employees of whom more than 50% are on H-1B or L-1.

**Lottery (IF12912 p. 2).** 'Since the mid-2000s, USCIS has used a lottery'. Electronic registration came in FY2021.

**Before 1990 and 1993.** Khanna-Morales: 'In 1993, the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work' (S0254, p. 5; Ch. 9). The B-1 business visitor route was the earlier body-shopping channel. The 2013 settlement shows it was still in use 20 years later (section 5).

## 2. How Indian the H-1B became (M11)

Indian share of H-1Bs over time:
- 1999: 47% of H-1B visas, 55,047 (Lowell 2000 via S0255, p. 12; Ch. 10).
- October 1999 to February 2000: 48.5% of approvals were India-born (INS via S0104 register note; original not read).
- FY2005: 'India was the leading country of origin for newly arriving H-1B workers, comprising almost half (49.0%) of all of the new arrivals in FY2005', with China at 9.16% (RL30498, 2007). Also: 'Data previously released by DHS Immigration Statistics further estimate that nearly three-fourths of all of the systems analysts and programmers are from India.' Undated, so use cautiously.
- 2014: about 70% (USCIS 2014 via S0254, pp. 1, 11). China 8% in 2012 (S0254, p. 1 n. 2).
- FY2023: 72% India-born, 12% China-born; 71% male; median age 33; 65% computer-related occupations; median pay $118,000; 69% of 386,000 approved petitions were for continuing employment (IF12912).

Verdict stays MIXED; refine: by nationality, overwhelmingly Indian since the late 1990s; by employer, the top sponsors c. 2012-13 were offshore services firms, Indian- and US-owned.

## 3. What the H-1B did at home (Khanna and Morales)

- Abstract and p. 3: 'the H-1B program induced Indians to switch to computer science (CS) occupations, increasing the CS workforce in India by 4.9% in 2010.' This is a model-based counterfactual, not an observed count.
- p. 1: those who could not join the US workforce 'due to the H-1B cap, remained in India, enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector'. Returnees after visa expiry brought 'acquired human capital and technology'.
- p. 11: workers unable to get an H-1B, 'or return to India after their visa expires are a ready supply of labor for firms in India'.

The argument: the American prospect pulled Indian students into engineering and CS. The cap kept most of them at home. Those at home staffed the Indian industry. So the H-1B and the engineer factory (Ch. 12) are one system.

Also p. 11: in 2002 Indian software engineers had a 'median age of 26.5 years, and 58% of them had less than three years of experience' (NASSCOM 2003). A NASSCOM-Hewitt survey: '88% of firms visited engineering colleges to recruit, and 47% recruited only there'. Engineering PhDs fell from about 675 in 1987 to 375 in 1995 (Arora and Athreye 2002). Useful for Ch. 12 as well.

## 4. Silicon Valley Indians, TiE and brain circulation (M12)

**Saxenian 1999 (S0097).**
- Method (pp. 7-8): Dun & Bradstreet database of 11,443 technology firms founded in Silicon Valley 1980-98. 'Immigrant-run businesses were identified as all of the companies with chief executive officers (CEOs) with Chinese and Indian surnames.' 'we are using immigrant-run businesses as a proxy for immigrant-founded businesses'. More than 100 interviews January 1997 to January 1998, plus 42 in Bangalore, Bombay and Delhi in December 1997.
- p. 23: 'Of the 11,443 high-technology firms started during this period, 2,001 (17 percent) were run by Chinese and 774 (7 percent) by Indians.' Indian-run firms had $3,588m sales and 16,598 jobs in 1998 (Table 2.6).
- p. 24 (Table 2.7): the Indian share of start-ups rose from 3% (1980-84) to 4%, 7% and 9% (1995-98).
- p. 13: in 1990 there were 28,520 Indians in the Silicon Valley workforce, 98% immigrants; 87% of Indians in high-tech had arrived after 1970 and 60% after 1980 (1990 census PUMS).
- p. 3 n. 3: 79% of 1990-91 Indian foreign doctoral recipients in science and engineering were still in the US in 1995 (NSF).
- Glass ceiling (p. 43): 'Why do you think there are so many Indian entrepreneurs in Silicon Valley? Because they know that sooner or later they will be held back.' (Prabhat Andleigh, quoted from the *Los Angeles Times*, 15 July 1996; secondary quotation.)
- SIPA was founded by Prakash Chandra, 'a typical Silicon Valley engineer' (AMD 1984, Intel, MIPS, Cadence), who returned to India in 1992 to work for Wellfleet/Bay Networks (p. 43 n. 15). SIPA had about 1,000 members.
- **TiE (pp. 44-46).**
  - Started in 1992 by 'an older generation of Indian immigrants'. Founding members included Suhas Patil (Cirrus Logic), Prabhu Goel (Gateway Design Automation) and Kanwal Rekhi (Excelan).
  - 'This core group came together in response to a visit from India's Secretary of Electronics to Silicon Valley in 1992, but when the minister's flight was delayed they began to informally share complaints about the difficulties of running a business.'
  - Patil (via *San Jose Mercury News*, 1994): 'as individuals we are brilliant, but collectively we amounted to nothing.'
  - First annual conference 1994: 'over 500 people'; 1997: more than 800 attendees.
  - Forty charter members, by invitation, with $1,000 annual dues.
  - The name is 'Indus (rather than Indian)', to include other South Asians.
  - This corroborates S0116: an official's visit in 1992 was the trigger. Note: 'Secretary of Electronics' and 'minister' are both used in the same passage.
- **Radha Basu (pp. 62-65).**
  - Left for USC graduate study in the early 1970s and joined HP.
  - Set up HP's Bangalore software centre in the mid-1980s, which had 400 staff when she returned to Silicon Valley in 1989.
  - The first office was 'a telex machine on her dining room table'.
  - For five years she had to copy software onto tapes and ship them so customs could record exports (p. 64 n. 19, interview 1 October 1997).
  - On the 'single window of clearance': 'where is the window?'
- p. 63: 'few Indian engineers choose to live and work permanently in India ... Indian engineers - if they return at all - typically do so on a temporary basis.'
- p. 65: 'Software companies in Bangalore report turnovers of 20-30 percent per year, primarily because so many workers jump at the first opportunity to emigrate.' 'the "brain drain" of skilled workers to the United States continued unabated throughout the 1990s.' p. 66: Indian programmers' wages were 'ten times lower' and 'the fully loaded cost of an engineer is 35-40 percent what it is in the United States'.

**Saxenian 2005 (S0256).**
- p. 2: by 2000, 53% of Silicon Valley's scientists and engineers were foreign-born. About 20,000 were Indian (n. 2: Indians 13% and Chinese 14%).
- p. 6: 'few U.S.-educated emigrants returned to either India or China during the 1980s and 1990s.' The 2001 technology recession 'triggered an upsurge in cross-regional entrepreneurship'.
- n. 1: NSF data show 'over 95% of foreign-born engineering and science doctorate holders from India and China planned to stay in the United States after graduation.'
- p. 17: Draper International's India fund (1995, $55m) found only one India-based company successful by 2000. Richards: 'You could be sitting in Bangalore and hear about 10 great deals in Santa Clara'.

**Brain circulation vs drain.** Saxenian's own data for the 1990s show drain more than circulation for India (pp. 63-65 of S0097; p. 6 of S0256). Circulation is a post-2001 claim. Fuller and Narasimhan add that offshoring itself reduced migration: 'flows of people may diminish as the work travels to the site of labor' (S0118, p. 190). By 2005 most young Chennai engineers went abroad for months, not years.

**M12.** Saxenian 1999: 7% of Silicon Valley tech firms founded 1980-98 had Indian-surnamed CEOs; 9% for 1995-98 (S0097, pp. 23-24). Wadhwa et al. 2007 (15.5%, any founder) not read. Keep MIXED; record the method (CEO surname, D&B universe).

## 5. The B-1 channel: the 2013 Infosys settlement

S0268 (Settlement Agreement, Exhibit 99.2 to Infosys 6-K, 30 October 2013). Infosys agreed to pay $34,000,000. Parties: the US Attorney for the Eastern District of Texas, the State Department, DHS, and Infosys.

**Agreed facts (para II.D).** 'Infosys failed to maintain accurate I-9 records for many of its foreign nationals in the United States in 2010 and 2011', including 'a widespread failure to update and re-verify the employment authorization status of a substantial percentage of its foreign national employees.'

**US allegations (para II.E), denied by Infosys (II.G):**
- Infosys 'knowingly and unlawfully used B-1 visa holders to perform skilled labor in order to fill positions in the United States for employment that would otherwise be performed by United States citizens or require legitimate H-1B visa holders, for the purposes of increasing profits, minimizing costs of securing visas, increasing flexibility of employee movement, obtaining an unfair advantage over competitors, and avoiding tax liabilities.'
- Invitation letters stated 'meetings' or 'discussions' when the purpose included 'coding and programming'.
- A 'Do's and Don'ts' memo: 'Do not mention activities like implementation, design & testing, consulting, etc., which sound like work.'; 'Also do not use words like, [sic] work, activity, etc., in the invitation letter.'; 'Please do not mention anything about contract rates.'
- Workers were told to give Labor Condition Application destinations when assigned elsewhere (e.g. told Seattle, actually Bentonville, Arkansas, 2009).
- On 29 September 2009, emails directed staff to convert a 'time and materials contract', which disclosed names and rates, into a 'fixed price contract', allegedly to conceal B-1 holders doing skilled work.

**Infosys's position (II.G-I).** B-1 use was 'for legitimate business purposes'. The letters were accurate. The memo 'was not intended to deceive' and was ended in 2011. Contract changes were 'made to comply with internal Company guidelines'. Infosys 'denies any allegations of civil or criminal wrongdoing and/or liability.'

**Drafting rule.** Present II.D as agreed. Present II.E only as the government's allegations, with Infosys's denial, every time.

**Thesis link.** The alleged T&M-to-fixed-price conversion shows the two pricing forms doing different jobs: T&M exposes who works and at what rate; fixed price hides it. This ties to Ch. 9 and Ch. 11 on pricing.

Whistleblower: press reports name an Infosys employee in Alabama, Jay (Jack) Palmer, whose complaint started the case. Not in the agreement. Needs a court record (Palmer v. Infosys, N.D. Ala., 2011-12) before use.

## 6. The other side: displacement (Perrero)

S0269 (Senate Judiciary Committee, 25 February 2016). Leo Perrero worked at Disney in Orlando 'in some capacity for well over ten years' on the point-of-sale system. His 'last day in the technology field' was 31 January 2015.
- At a meeting in the 2014 holiday season, an executive told about two dozen IT staff: 'All of you in this room will be losing your jobs in the next 90 days. Your jobs have been given over to a foreign workforce.' Severance depended on training replacements. Only '4 people would be directly rehired by Disney of the hundreds that were lost.'
- Knowledge transfer: 'Everything was recorded with audio and video. Then the very next day the recordings were put down on paper and read right back to us.'
- 'Sadly, I choose the money over America.'
- He names neither the contractor nor the replacements' nationality beyond 'foreign'. Press reports name the contractors; not verified. Do not name them from this source.
- Outside the chapter's end date (2015 events, 2016 testimony), but the displacement happened in 2014-15. Fits.

**Thesis link.** The three-phase 90-day 'knowledge transfer' (record, document, shadow, take over) is the codification of tacit work into documents that a cheaper worker can follow. The same process later feeds AI tools. Use carefully: one witness, with an evident advocacy purpose (testimony to a reform hearing).

## 7. Families (from S0117)

- p. 146: in Chennai, parents of migrants were 'lonely old folk whose sons and daughters send them dollars'. Others visited children and became 'bored, exploited childminders marooned in American suburbia under "house arrest", to quote one elderly grandfather'.
- Migration 'damages the proper reciprocal relationship between parents and children in a way that migration within India does not'.
- Ravi (pseud.) came home for his elderly father (pp. 128-129). Balaram (pseud.) went to Australia in 1995 and returned in 2003 (p. 129).
