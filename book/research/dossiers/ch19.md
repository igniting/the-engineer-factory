## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The companies' own words.** Infosys's annual reports track the change in one risk factor. In fiscal 2024, 'clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements' because of 'the code generation capabilities of AI technologies' (S0291). By fiscal 2026 the cause is 'evolving AI technologies', and a new passage says clients are 'selectively insourcing capabilities as AI improves productivity and reduces dependency on external labor‑based models' (S0297). Wipro's FY2026 report says AI 'may reduce demand for certain services we provide' (S0298). Cognizant's says the same of 'some services' (S0299).

**Headcount.** The numbers did not collapse. Infosys made 87,286 offers in fiscal 2026, up from 26,975 in fiscal 2024 (S0291, S0297). Nasscom reports 135,000 net additions in FY26, against revenue growth of 6.1% (S0306, an industry body's figures). But the top five firms shrank by 6,981 people in FY26, led by TCS, which lost 23,460 (S0139).

**TCS.** In July 2025 TCS said it would cut about 12,200 jobs. Its chief executive said this was 'not because of AI giving some 20% productivity gains' but skill mismatch (S0301). Unions said employees were pushed to resign (S0302, S0303). Why the jobs went is disputed.

**Price.** Executives and analysts now speak of 'AI deflation': clients take productivity gains as lower prices (S0142). HCLTech's chief executive put a $100 million deal at 'maybe $80 million' today (S0142).

**Students.** A student at IIITDM Jabalpur said fewer than a quarter of his 400 classmates had offers (S0305).

**The state.** Washington imposed a $100,000 H-1B payment in September 2025 and extended it in September 2026. Two courts have blocked it (S0136, S0300, S0137). India funded Sarvam's sovereign models (S0187, S0307), and AICTE moved to put AI into all engineering curricula (S0145).

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 One sentence, three years: what the companies told investors

The most reliable record of how the Indian services companies understood AI is not their speeches. It is their filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, where a misleading statement carries legal risk. Infosys's annual report on Form 20-F contains a risk factor about wages and productivity that changed in small but telling ways between 2024 and 2026.

In the report for fiscal 2024, filed in June 2024, Infosys wrote: 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements' (S0291). In the report for fiscal 2025, filed on 1 July 2025, the sentence became: 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have started demanding higher commitments around productivity improvements' (S0296, Item 3.D, HTML p. 15). In the report for fiscal 2026, filed on 15 June 2026, the cause was widened: 'Due to the attention created by evolving AI technologies , clients have started demanding higher commitments around productivity improvements' (S0297, Item 3.D, HTML p. 15). The stray space before the comma is in the filing; the book may close it with a note.

In both later reports, the sentence before it reads: 'In addition, due to competitive market conditions and pricing pressures, we are committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts with our clients' (S0296; S0297). The sentence after it warns that failing to deliver those improvements 'may impact our profitability'. This is the mechanism the press later called AI deflation, set out by the company itself two years earlier. Clients hear that machines can write code. They ask for contracts that assume fewer hours. The vendor promises the savings and carries the risk if they do not arrive.

The fiscal 2026 report adds a passage in its operating review that was not in the earlier ones. It describes clients' choices: 'large technology platforms continue to expand their service layers, while clients themselves are selectively insourcing capabilities as AI improves productivity and reduces dependency on external labor‑based models' (S0297, Item 5, HTML p. 61). 'External labor-based models' is a fair description of what Infosys has sold since 1981. The book should quote this sentence. It is the company naming, in its own regulatory filing, the threat this chapter is about.

Wipro's reports show the same movement. The fiscal 2026 20-F adds a risk factor: 'AI‑driven automation, efficiency gains and increased client self‑service may reduce demand for certain services we provide and may adversely affect pricing, margins, service levels or the mix of services delivered' (S0298, FY2026, p. 11). In its industry outlook, Wipro predicts that 'Hiring will shift from volume to skills, emphasizing AI fluency, domain expertise, and productivity-based workforce models'. It lists 'outcome‑based pricing' among the industry's likely strategies, and names 'pricing pressure driven by competitive intensity and AI-driven productivity expectations' (S0298, FY2026, p. 33). Its operating review warns of 'elevated customer expectations on productivity due to AI advancements' that 'can potentially put pressure on our current revenue base' (S0298, FY2026, p. 47).

Cognizant's 10-K for 2025, filed on 12 February 2026, uses similar words: 'As AI-based technologies or other forms of automation evolve, demand for some services that we currently perform for our clients may be reduced and our ability to obtain favorable pricing or other terms for some of our services may be diminished' (S0299, p. 29). A second risk factor admits the company may fail to recoup its AI spending 'if our clients do not accept new pricing models that reflect the value of our AI-enabled solutions' (S0299, p. 16).

All three companies say AI will help them as well as hurt them, and the book should report both. But the risk factors are useful because they are written for lawyers, not for customers. They show that by 2026 three of the largest employers of Indian software engineers had told American regulators that the machines threatened the volume of work they sold.

### 3.2 The tools inside the companies

The filings also record which tools the engineers were given. Infosys's fiscal 2025 report says: 'We also utilize leading technology solutions like GitHub Copilot, where we are among the top GSIs in adoption' (S0296, Item 4, HTML p. 47). 'GSI' means global systems integrator; the ranking is the company's own claim. The fiscal 2026 report lists more tools: 'We utilize leading AI solutions like Github Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code and Devin to name a few' (S0297, Item 4, HTML p. 48). It says Infosys has 'infused AI throughout our software development lifecycle' and sells its own platforms for 'Agentic software engineering' (S0297). Claude Code is made by Anthropic, the company whose model is assisting with this book; see the conflict note above.

Wipro gives a count. In fiscal 2025 'over 20,000 associates' were 'leveraging AI developer tools, boosting our overall productivity' (S0298, FY2025, p. 33). A year later the same sentence said 'over 60,000' (S0298, FY2026, p. 36). That is roughly a quarter of a workforce of 'more than 240,000' (S0298, FY2026, p. 73). The productivity claim is Wipro's.

Cognizant says it has given staff 'access to leading AI tools, including Microsoft Copilot, GitHub CoPilot, Google Gemini Code Assist and Windsurf'. It adds that 'Associates were given access to leading vibe coding tools to sharpen their AI skills, producing more than 30,000 prototypes' (S0299, p. 10). 'Vibe coding' (describing software in plain language and letting a model write it) thus entered an American annual report as a training method for Indian programmers. Cognizant says it 'upskilled more than 330,000 associates on GenAI' between July 2023 and the end of 2025 (S0299, p. 10).

Training claims run through all the reports. Infosys said in fiscal 2025 that 'Over 2,75,000 of our people are trained on AI at different levels of proficiency' (S0296, HTML p. 52; the Indian digit grouping is in the original). In fiscal 2026 it said '84% of our workforce is now AI‑aware' (S0297, HTML p. 118). TCS told Moneycontrol it had trained 5.5 lakh employees in basic AI and 1 lakh in advanced skills (as reported by Outlook Business, S0135). Nasscom says 20 lakh of the industry's 59.5 lakh employees have been 'upskilled in AI' (S0306). None of these figures defines what training means, and the book should give them as claims.

### 3.3 Headcount: no collapse, but a stall

The fear in 2025-26 was that AI would empty the offices. The headcount numbers do not show that, at least not yet. They show a stall after the boom-and-bust of Chapter 18.

Infosys had 317,240 employees at 31 March 2024 (S0291), 323,578 a year later (S0296) and 328,594 at 31 March 2026 (S0297). Its offers of employment recovered from the trough of 26,975 in fiscal 2024 to 83,207 in fiscal 2025 and 87,286 in fiscal 2026. Applications rose to 4,457,748 and then 5,884,480 (S0296, S0297, Item 6.D). More people were applying for each job than ever: about 67 applications per offer in fiscal 2026, against 53 in fiscal 2025. That ratio is a calculation for the book's data appendix, not a company figure.

Wipro said it had 'more than 250,000, 232,000 and 230,000 employees' at 31 March 2023, 2024 and 2025 (S0298, FY2025, p. 68), and 'more than 240,000' at 31 March 2026 (S0298, FY2026, p. 73). Cognizant had about 351,600 at the end of 2025, of whom 256,900 were in India (S0299, p. 10). Its voluntary attrition in tech services fell to 13.9% in 2025 from 15.9% in 2024 (S0299, p. 11). That is far below the 31.7% of 2022 (S0293): people were not leaving because there was nowhere better to go.

TCS was the exception. *Business Standard*'s Avik Das, compiling the companies' results, reported that the five largest firms' total headcount fell by 6,981 in fiscal 2026, after rising by 12,718 the year before. TCS's net headcount dropped by 23,460 and Tech Mahindra's by 1,108, while Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech added people (S0139). These are the companies' reported figures as compiled by the newspaper; TCS's own fact sheet was not read.

Nasscom's industry totals show the same stall. In February 2025 it estimated 126,000 net additions in FY2025, taking the workforce to 5.8 million (S0140). In February 2026 it reported 1.35 lakh (135,000) net additions in FY26, taking direct employees to 59.5 lakh (S0306). Its president Rajesh Nambiar spoke, in PTI's paraphrase, of 'a non-linearity' between job growth of 2.3% and revenue growth of 6.1% (S0306). For thirty years the industry's revenue and headcount had grown together, because revenue was headcount multiplied by a billing rate. A gap between them is the change this chapter is about. Nasscom's figures are an industry body's estimates and were revised: FY25 revenue was raised from $282.6 billion to $297 billion a year later, partly because of a restatement of GCC revenue (S0140, S0306).

### 3.4 TCS, July 2025: the cut and the dispute about why

On Sunday 27 July 2025 Tata Consultancy Services said it would reduce its workforce by 2% in the financial year, mainly at middle and senior levels. Reuters put the cut at roughly 12,200 jobs out of more than 613,000 (S0301). TCS called it part of a plan to 'deploy artificial intelligence at scale, and realign its workforce model' (Reuters' paraphrase of the company, S0301). TCS's own statement was not obtained, because the company's site is blocked to this research.

The chief executive, K. Krithivasan, gave an interview to Moneycontrol the next day. Reuters quotes him: 'This is not because of AI giving some 20% productivity gains. We are not doing that. This is driven by where there is a skill mismatch, or, where we think that we have not been able to deploy someone' (S0301; the full quotation runs over 50 words, so the book must shorten it). Outlook Business reports him saying 'Some people, especially at senior levels, find it difficult to transition to tech-heavy roles' and 'This is not a question of demand. It is about us being future-ready' (S0135). He described a move from 'waterfall' project management to agile methods that needed fewer layers of managers (S0135).

That is a party's explanation, and it was disputed at once. Reuters Breakingviews (Shritama Bose and Ujjaini Dutta) noted that TCS's headcount had risen 36% since 2020. Revenue per employee had risen 20% in that period, but costs per employee had risen by a quarter (S0301). It cited Piyush Pandey of Centrum Broking, who estimated the cut would trim employee costs by up to 4%. It reported Kamal Karanth of the staffing firm Xpheno saying the industry's pyramid was giving way to 'a diamond shape' with a big middle (S0301, paraphrase). On that view the cut was about cost after over-hiring, which Chapter 18 documents.

The union view was different again. The IT & ITES Democratic Employees Association called it 'cost-cutting masquerading as future-readiness' (S0303). The book should set out three explanations: the company's (skills and redeployment), the analysts' (cost after the pandemic hiring) and the unions' (cheaper labour). It should not decide between them, because no document read here can. Myth M20 stays UNRESOLVED.

### 3.5 The bench and the meeting room

What the cut felt like is known from reporting in Bengaluru, though almost entirely through anonymous employees. Anisha Reddy of *South First* reported on 2 August 2025 that over 100 TCS employees had approached unions (S0302). The secretary of the Karnataka State IT/ITeS Employees Union (KITU), Lenil Babu, told her: 'So far, we have filed complaints with the Additional Labour Commissioner of Karnataka on behalf of 15 employees who were already let go. But another 100 have approached us after being put on the bench' (S0302; over 50 words in full, so shorten).

The 'bench' is where unassigned engineers wait for a project. *South First* reported that a new TCS policy, in force from 12 June 2025, limited time on the bench to 35 days a year and required at least 225 billable days (S0302). It quoted an email one employee received: 'Your prolonged un-allocation without any productive assignment is a matter of concern to the company and the company views this matter very seriously' (S0302; the reporter says she saw it). The Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES) said the policy 'frames lack of allocation as a personal failure of the employee' (S0302).

Korah Abraham of *The News Minute* reported on 7 August 2025 how resignation meetings worked, again from anonymous accounts. A mid-level employee said: 'They offered three months' pay as an incentive to resign. But if someone refuses, they're told they'll be terminated without compensation, and won't receive a relieving letter' (S0303). Two employees said they were asked to switch off their phones before meeting HR. A woman who resigned said she would have fought the case but for money: 'I come from a financially poor background' (S0303). One employee claimed more than 300 people had been made to resign from one Bengaluru campus in a week. That is a single person's estimate and must be labelled as such.

KITU filed an industrial dispute on 29 July 2025. It argued TCS was avoiding the government approval the Industrial Disputes Act requires for retrenchment at firms with over 100 employees (S0303, as KITU's claim). A conciliation meeting was held on 6 August 2025 before Additional Labour Commissioner G. Manjunath, with TCS represented by Boban Varghese Thomas, General Manager (HR). It ended without agreement and was adjourned to 8 September (S0303). The outcome was not found.

TCS told *South First* the restructuring was being planned 'with due care' (S0302). One further story, a Reddit post about a TCS manager dismissed after a failed outcome-based project, was carried by Financial Express and *People Matters* (S0304). Both said they had not verified it. It is excluded as evidence. It is noted only because it is widely repeated, and because its theme of outcome-based contracts shifting risk onto workers matches the filings in 3.1.

### 3.6 'AI deflation': the price of an hour falls

By 2026 the industry had a name for what Infosys had described in 2024. Payal Ganguly's *Forbes India* report of 11 May 2026 calls it 'AI-led deflation, characterised by a productivity-based pricing structure demanded by customers and the passing on of productivity benefits to enterprises' (S0142, the magazine's words).

It quotes HCLTech's chief executive C. Vijayakumar from the company's April 2026 earnings call: 'A $100 million deal would be much less today - maybe $80 million, just on a rough ballpark' (S0142). He added that winning the same revenue took more selling effort, and spoke of a 3-5% deflationary effect on the year's guidance (S0142, paraphrase). TCS's Krithivasan, on the Q4 FY26 call, said 'Our expectation is AI will be net accretive', with AI revenue expected to 'overcompensate' for traditional revenue that would 'slowly taper down' (S0142). Infosys's Salil Parekh spoke of multi-year deals priced on productivity (S0142, paraphrase).

The analysts were blunter. *Forbes India* cites a Motilal Oswal sector report of 4 May 2026. It estimated that 12-15% of sector revenue faced 'direct AI-driven displacement risk', and that 'The cost of delivering an outcome has fallen sharply; these savings are currently being extracted almost entirely by enterprise buyers' (S0142). A Kotak Securities report expected growth to stay 'range-bound at 3 percent' (S0142). The broker reports themselves were not read.

The headline numbers fit the story without proving it. *Forbes India* reports that TCS's US-dollar revenue fell 0.5% in FY26 and Wipro's 0.32% (S0142). The same companies report growing AI revenue: TCS's annualised AI revenue above $2.3 billion, HCLTech's 'Advanced AI' revenue of $620 million and Infosys's AI revenue of $275 million in one quarter (S0142, company figures). Nasscom put industry AI revenue at $10-12 billion in FY26, its first such estimate (S0306). Gaurav Vasu of UnearthInsight told *Business Standard* that AI revenue was under 5-7% of the top five firms' revenue (S0139). Definitions differ between firms, so these numbers cannot be added up or compared.

This connects to the book's argument about the business model. Chapter 6 showed how Indian firms learned to price people by the hour. Chapter 11 showed how the fixed-price Y2K contracts first rewarded productivity tools. The 2026 evidence suggests the hourly model is under pressure from both ends. Clients will not pay for hours a machine can save, and the vendors must promise the savings in advance.

### 3.7 Freshers and graduates

The question for the engineering colleges was whether the entry-level job survived. The evidence is mixed.

On one side, Infosys's offers rose in fiscal 2025 and 2026 (S0296, S0297), and its management told *Business Standard* in April 2026 that it would add 20,000 freshers (S0139). An offer is not a hire, and the two numbers measure different things; see dispute (eee). On the other side, TCS gave a fresher figure of 25,000 for FY27, against a usual intake of about 40,000 from engineering colleges (S0139). Wipro's chief human resources officer Saurabh Govil declined to give a number: 'Hiring will be as per demand' (S0139). HCLTech's Ramachandran Sundararajan said: 'Given the current environment, it is difficult to provide a number' (S0139). Krithivasan said, in the paper's paraphrase, that freshers take 'up to nine months to come on the floor, while a lateral can deliver immediately' (S0139). This is an old argument (Chapter 7 shows NIIT and Aptech building on it), now sharpened because AI tools do some of the junior work.

*Business Standard* also attributes the bleak outlook to geopolitics: 'The impact of the conflict in West Asia is most pronounced in the hiring outlook for this fiscal' (S0139). The book must keep this in view. Fresher hiring fell in 2023-24, before agentic coding tools were in wide use, and onboarding delays were documented from 2022 (S0133, S0134). M30 cannot be settled by timing alone.

The students' experience is documented by Yashraj Sharma of *Rest of World*, 9 December 2025 (S0305). Rishabh Mishra joined the Indian Institute of Information Technology, Design and Manufacturing in Jabalpur in 2022, intending to 'study computer science, write code, and one day make it to Silicon Valley' (the reporter's words). Among his 400 classmates, fewer than 25% had job offers, according to the article; the source of the figure is not stated. 'It is really bad out there,' Mishra said. Some classmates were considering further study, but he said 'after one year, if you return to the job market, your degree is even more irrelevant' (S0305). Another student, Nishant Kaushik, who studied computer science at a college in eastern India, had decided to look for sales or marketing roles (S0305, paraphrase). The same article reports an EY finding that Indian IT services firms had cut entry-level roles by 20-25% because of automation and AI. The EY report was not read.

These two students, named in published reporting, meet the chapter's rank-and-file requirement. The book should not imply that Mishra's experience is typical. One college's placement rate in one year is a single data point.

### 3.8 Washington's $100,000 question

On 19 September 2025 President Trump signed Proclamation 10973, 'Restriction on Entry of Certain Nonimmigrant Workers' (S0136, 90 FR 46027). Its opening sentence says the H-1B programme 'has been deliberately exploited to replace, rather than supplement, American workers with lower-paid, lower- skilled labor' (the hyphenation break is in the Federal Register text). It singles out 'Information technology (IT) firms' and 'IT outsourcing companies', citing:
- the IT share of H-1B approvals, which rose from 32% in FY2003 to over 65%;
- a '36 percent discount for H-1B "entry- level" positions';
- New York Federal Reserve figures for unemployment among recent computer-science and computer-engineering graduates, of 6.1% and 7.5%.

It also cites 'A 2017 study' finding wages 2.6-5.1% higher and employment 6.1-10.8% higher absent foreign workers in computer science (S0136, p. 46028). That appears to be the Bound, Khanna and Morales work discussed in Chapter 13 (S0254); confirm before saying so in the text.

The operative clause restricted entry of H-1B workers 'except for those aliens whose petitions are accompanied or supplemented by a payment of $100,000', from 12:01 a.m. on 21 September 2025, for 12 months (S0136, Sec. 1(a)). It applied only to entries after that date (Sec. 3(a)). It ordered rulemaking on prevailing wages (Sec. 4).

On 18 September 2026 a second proclamation, 11069, extended the restriction to 21 September 2027 (S0300, 91 FR 60497; POST-CUTOFF). Its preamble gives the administration's account of the results:
- the payment had been made for 'over 700 petitions';
- a weighted H-1B lottery favouring higher wages had been in force for the FY2027 season;
- 'the largest IT staffing and outsourcing firms' had cut 'their combined H-1B registrations from 24,946 to 2,055, a 92 percent decrease' (S0300, p. 60498).

The firms are not named. These are a party's figures and must be labelled as the administration's claims. If they are right, the route that carried Indian engineers to American client sites since the 1990s (Chapters 6 and 13) had nearly closed for the outsourcing firms within a year.

The courts then intervened. According to Clark Hill's summary, Judge Leo T. Sorokin of the District of Massachusetts ruled on 8 June 2026, in a suit by a coalition of states, that the fee was unlawful: a tax not authorised by Congress, a breach of the separation of powers and a breach of the Administrative Procedure Act (S0137). According to DiRaimondo & Schroeder, the First Circuit refused to stay that ruling. On 30 September 2026, in *Global Nurse Force v. Trump*, Judge Haywood S. Gilliam Jr. of the Northern District of California vacated the agency policies implementing both proclamations, for want of notice and comment. That firm says 'The Court did not strike down the President's orders themselves' (S0137). As of 1 October 2026 the fee was blocked, and DHS had separately proposed a fee of about $103,000 through formal rulemaking (S0137). The two firms describe the Massachusetts ruling differently (dispute aaa), and the orders were not read. The book should state only what both agree on: the fee was blocked by two courts and the government was appealing. The status must be re-checked at final verification (Phase 5).

### 3.9 The state builds models

India's government answered the AI question as it had answered payments (Chapter 17) and identity (Chapter 16): by building. The IndiaAI Mission was launched in March 2024 with INR 10,372 crore, about $1.1 billion, for compute, data, models and research (S0187; the cabinet release, S0186, was not read). On 26 April 2025, according to *tele.net*, Sarvam AI was selected to build 'an open source 120 billion parameter' model, after 506 proposals; three more start-ups (Soket AI, Gnani AI and Gan AI) were selected later (S0308). *Business Today* reported the selection on 28 April 2025 and quoted the minister, Ashwini Vaishnaw: 'We are confident that Sarvam's models will be competitive with global models' (S0307).

Sarvam was founded in August 2023 by Vivek Raghavan and Pratyush Kumar (S0187). At the India AI Impact Summit in February 2026 it unveiled two models, Sarvam-30B and Sarvam-105B (S0187). *Inc42*'s Debarghya Sil wrote that they were competitive in their size class and strong on Indian languages, but trailed the frontier models of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta in 'raw reasoning depth' (S0187; the magazine's assessment, based partly on the company's own evaluations). Raghavan called the launches a 'significant first step', as reported (S0187). Raghavan's earlier role at UIDAI would link this chapter to Chapter 16, but it is not documented in any source read here; verify before using.

### 3.10 Education: the regulator responds

The All India Council for Technical Education declared 2025 the 'Year of Artificial Intelligence' on 23 December 2024, promising initiatives across 'over 14,000 colleges and 40 million students' (S0145, Careers360). The initiatives included an 'AI for All' campaign, AI labs and faculty workshops. On 6 June 2025 its chairman T.G. Sitharam told PTI that 'A committee of experts has been constituted to suggest necessary revisions in the model curriculums', so that AI could be included in all branches of engineering, IT and management (S0145). He noted AICTE had introduced a B.Tech in AI and Data Science in 2017. No circular making AI compulsory across branches was found, and the revised model curriculum was not located. The chapter should say only that the regulator announced the change, not that it happened. This connects to Chapter 12's finding that AICTE approves curricula but cannot make colleges teach them well.

### 3.11 Developers everywhere

GitHub's *Octoverse 2025*, as reported by Joe Fay in *The Register* on 29 October 2025, projected that India's developer community would grow to 57.5 million or more by 2030, passing the United States' projected 54.7 million. The US currently has 28 million (S0146). GitHub said 80% of new users began using Copilot in their first week. These are a company's figures about its own platform. A GitHub account is not a job, and the book should not treat the projection as a count of engineers. It is still a striking frame for the epilogue: more Indians writing code than ever, at the moment code became cheap.

## 6. Disputes

- **(aaa) What the Massachusetts court vacated (8 June 2026).** Clark Hill says 'the Presidential Proclamation'. DiRaimondo & Schroeder say 'the implementing policies', and add that the California court did not strike down the orders themselves. The court orders have not been read. Until they are, say only that the fee was blocked (S0137).
- **(bbb) Why TCS cut about 12,200 jobs.**
  - CEO: skill mismatch and failed redeployment, 'not because of AI' (S0301, S0135).
  - Breakingviews and analysts: cost after pandemic over-hiring (S0301).
  - Unions: cheaper labour, 'cost-cutting masquerading as future-readiness' (S0303).

  M20 stays UNRESOLVED.
- **(ccc) How the TCS exits happened.** TCS: a planned restructuring with severance and support (S0135, S0302). Unions and anonymous employees: forced resignations, with one claim of 300 in a week at one campus (S0302, S0303). Neither the conciliation outcome nor any Labour Department finding was found.
- **(ddd) Sarvam's model size.** The April 2025 selection was for a '120 billion parameter' model (S0308); the February 2026 release was Sarvam-105B (S0187). The PIB text is needed to know what was contracted.
- **(eee) Infosys fresher hiring.** The 20-F reports 87,286 offers in FY2026 (S0297). *Business Standard* reports Infosys will 'add 20,000 freshers' in FY27 (S0139). The measures differ (offers of all kinds vs fresher hires; different years), so these are not contradictory. The text must not set them side by side as if they were.
- **(fff) Indian developers on GitHub in 2025.** The register's '>5 million joined in 2025' is not in *The Register*'s text (S0146). Mark it unverified until the Octoverse report is read.
