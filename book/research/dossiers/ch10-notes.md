## S0034 Murthy CHM part 2, pp. 10-11 (read in full)

### Why Infosys left Pune (p. 10)

- 'when we founded the company in 1981, it was in Pune. We pretty soon realized that Maharashtra was not the best place to have a startup because the tenancy laws in Maharashtra ... if I was a tenant in an apartment for seven years, then the landlord could not make me vacate the place.'
- 'most landlords in Maharashtra refused to give an apartment on rent ... Unless you were a big company, unless you gave some big bank guarantee ... But no startup could do that.'
- 'Karnataka was one such state. The tenancy laws in Karnataka have always been very fair between the landlord and the tenant. So, that is why we wanted to be either in Chennai, Tamil Nadu or in Bangalore, Karnataka.'

### Why Bangalore over Chennai (pp. 10-11)

- 'because of the availability of engineering talent'.
- 'right from 1947 ... the government of India decided to locate its defense oriented high-tech companies in Bangalore, which was away from the border with Pakistan and with China'.
- Bangalore is roughly 250 miles from the Arabian Sea and 200 from the Bay of Bengal, so an attacking plane 'would be shot down'. A geographic rationale as Murthy recalls it; an assertion, not documented policy.
- Institutions named: 'LRDE ... BEL ... HAL'; ISRO 'in the late 70s' (Kapoor's prompt).
- p. 11: 'Karnataka had a disproportionately higher percentage of engineering talent and engineering students in the country.' Consistent with private engineering colleges from the 1980s (Ch. 7) and Saxenian's 60% figure (S0255, p. 14).
- **No mention of climate.** Supports M17's verdict.
- Date of the move: c. 1983 per the chronology (unverified line). Murthy gives no year here.

## S0085 Heeks (1998), pp. 12-13 (read in full earlier)

- Table 3, company headquarters (558 firms, Dataquest 1998): Bangalore 152, Mumbai 122, Chennai 93, Delhi 86, Hyderabad 34, Calcutta 27, Pune 22.
- 'The software industry started in Mumbai, in the business district around Nariman Point in the late 1970s'. It moved to SEEPZ, and Mumbai stayed the centre 'until at least the mid-1980s'.
- Mumbai's problems: 'Software firms were fast running out of labour, partly due to the overseas brain drain, plus high prices in the city and poor quality of life'.
- Bangalore's factors (Haug 1991 framework):
  - 'Labour Availability': labs, institutes and public-sector electronics firms. He names 'Bharat Electricals Ltd' (sic, probably Bharat Electronics), IIM and IISc.
  - 'Quality of Life': 'a good climate and social life ... pub culture'; the BAIT club joke ('a minimum of six mugs of beer').
  - 'By contrast Mumbai has had a long and troubled history of both communal and labour relations strife'.
  - 'Infrastructure': 'You could get across town in a matter of minutes in a car'; 'plentiful supply of both power and water'.
- 'the perception of these factors as much as their reality has coloured locational decisions'.
- TI 'set up operations in 1986 ... constantly reported in the home and international media'. Then the 'snowball effect': 'Proximity to Previous Employer and Residence'.
- **Climate appears here as one factor among four.** M17 'chosen for its climate' stays MOSTLY FALSE as the *main* reason, but climate is part of the 'quality of life' perception.

## S0255 Saxenian, 'Bangalore: The Silicon Valley of Asia?' (Stanford WP 91, February 2001). Full text read; PDF page = printed.

### Policy and TI

- p. 5: Sen (1994) quoted: 'until 1991-92, there was virtually no policy support at all for the software sector. Even the term "benign neglect" would be too positive a phrase'.
- **p. 6: TI.** 'When Texas Instruments set up the first earth station in Bangalore in 1986, for example, the process involved removing or breaking 25 different government rules' (citing N. Seshagiri interview, 24 June 1996, via Parthasarathy 2000).
  - 'Without reliable telecommunications links, Indian firms had no alternative to providing contract programming on-site'.

### STPs (p. 7)

- Tax exemption for five years; 64 kbps data lines; duty-free imports; 100% foreign ownership 'in exchange for a sizable export obligation'.
- n. 10: firms must earn net 150% of hardware imported within four years, and net 150% of their wage bill annually. From January 1995, 25% domestic sales allowed; 50% from 1999.
- 'the DoE announced the first three in 1990 in Bangalore, Pune and Bhubaneshwar, and another four the following year'. STPI registered June 1991. STP directors as 'friend, philosopher and guide'.
- 1998: 25 STPs. The ITPL Bangalore is a Karnataka-Tata-Singapore consortium partnership.

### Wages and skills (p. 9)

- 'India's greatest asset is a large, educated, English-speaking workforce that is willing to work at relatively low wages.'
- 'In 1994, wages for software programmers and systems analysts in India were less than one-tenth of those for their US counterparts, and lower even than other developing countries like Mexico (Table 2.)'
- Unix advantage: 'India's Unix programmers had a skill that was extremely scarce elsewhere in the world' (pp. 9-10). Corroborates S0001, p. 40 (Ch. 5).

### Body shopping (p. 10)

- '"input-less" export' (Heeks).
- **'In these contracts, the amount of software code is specified in advance and revenue is earned per line of code.'**
  - **Dispute (ee) for the thesis:** this conflicts with S0241, p. 31 ('hourly or monthly rate') and S0085, p. 7 (time and materials). Saxenian cites no source for the line. Weigh it: the World Bank study and Heeks's survey are direct, while Saxenian's sentence is unsourced. Record it as a dissenting description.
- 'Indian engineers who work overseas are paid their salaries in rupees and provided with minimal allowances for housing and expenses. Some refer to this business as "resume selling"'.
- 'more than 67,000 computer science professionals annually' from institutions; '200,000 individuals enroll annually in the private software training institutes' (NASSCOM 1999). Ch. 7, 12.
- Sen (1994): 1987-93 'a significant portion of export growth was accounted for by the falling value of the rupee'.
- Onsite '90% of Indian software exports in 1990' to '58% by 1998' (Parthasarathy 2000a, p. 28). Compare S0254: 90% (1988), 66% (1995), 56% (2000).
- n. 19: by 1995, 435 STP companies, 'more than 16% of exports'.

### Offshore centres (p. 11)

Anonymous quotes from Parthasarathy's 1996 interviews:

- A US employer in Bangalore: '. . . we came here because of the skills. We expanded because of the skills. We were able to come to India because the risk of being 10,000 miles away, the risk of the satellite link and the telephones and the flights were offset by the costs.'
- An ODC chief: 'if they had asked me then, I would have said no. I would have said, "do anything, bodyshopping, subcontracting, modular work, but don't give full dependability here [in India] because nobody's ever done it. It's not proven."'
- The same chief: 'Technologically, there's zero difference.'
- December 1999: 137 Indian firms with ISO 9000 or SEI-CMM Level 2; ten at Level 5.

### Productivity and wages (pp. 12-13)

- p. 12: Wipro and Infosys win 'turnkey' work; 'Rather than competing on the basis of hourly productivity they aim to accumulate intellectual property'.
- p. 12: '180,000 workers in 1998, the annual revenue per employee in India was $15,000-20,000. This compared to $100,000 per employee in other software producing countries such as Israel and Ireland (Arora et al. 2000).' **For the Ireland sidebar.**
- p. 12: Indian H-1Bs 'nearly half of all visas issued in 1999 (47 percent.) This amounted to 55,047 Indian workers in 1999 alone, and a total of 195,083 between 1989 and 1999' (Lowell 2000).
- p. 12 n. 24: five firms close to 50% of exports: TCS, Infosys, Pentafour, Tata Infotech, Wipro.
- p. 13: 'wages in the software industry rose 21% per year in the late 1990s, albeit from a low base (NASSCOM 1998)'.
- p. 13: Ashank Desai (Mastek), 1999: 'it is becoming difficult for them to compete only on one differential—the cost advantage'.

### States and infrastructure (pp. 14-16)

- p. 14 n. 27: 'Sixty percent of India's computer science graduates come from these three states' (AP, Karnataka, TN; Dossani 2000).
- p. 16: in 1997 India had '18.6 telephone mainlines per 1000 people ... compared to 55.7 in China, and the wait for a new connection was 12.17 months'.

### Enclave (p. 20)

- 'Dutch Disease' risk; IT under 1% of GDP.
- 'Software employment and investments are overwhelmingly concentrated in urban areas in the southern states'.
- 'Professionals in IT enclaves could become better connected, both economically and socially, to distant regions than to the rest of the Indian economy.'
- STPs 'insulated from the day-to-day challenges of doing business in India by dedicated communications links, private power sources'.

## S0257 RoyChowdhury, 'Livelihood and Income: Informality and Poverty in Bangalore's Slums' (Oxford CSASP WIP 10, c. 2011). Full text; PDF = printed.

- p. 1 (Introduction): post-1947 Bangalore as 'a garden city of retirees' bunglows [sic], and for a significant public sector presence'.
  - Since the late 1980s, reform governments wound down public-sector units. Public-sector land 'put up for sale' and workforces 'downsized and contractualized'. 'real estate prices soared'.
- p. 8:
  - Bangalore houses '500 international IT companies'; '40% of whole of India's IT'; '33% of India's $32 billion IT exports' (date unclear, c. 2007).
  - Growth 'through the expansion of capital intensive rather than labour intensive industries, as well as skill-based industries, which offered limited scope of employment, and only to technically skilled personnel'.
  - Low-end service jobs: 'drivers of cars and vans run by BPOs and Call Centres ... security and maintenance personnel in malls'. Work 'remains in the casual/contract mode with no security of service, insurance or pension'.
- p. 9: 'the number of software export companies in Karnataka grew from 13 to 1154 during 1991-2003 and software exports grew from Rs.5.6 crores to Rs.12,350 crores' (Karnataka Development Report 2007). Karnataka grew more than 8% in the late 1990s.
- p. 12: 'In Bangalore, nearly one million poor live in slums, and about one third of slum dwellers fall below the poverty line, with a monthly income of less than Rs 2500 (US$55).'
- pp. 25-26 (K.S. Garden slum, Lalbagh area; focus groups):
  - 'About 50 youths in the slum had managed to get some kind of computer training. Of these, some 25-30 women were working as receptionist in small offices, or showrooms, at cash counters in small super markets.'
  - 'even those with some computer training frequently remained unemployed or only partially employed'.
  - 'there had not been any systemic or policy based effort to incorporate the urban underclass into the city's growing economy'.
  - The collective voice of the city's poor; anonymised.
