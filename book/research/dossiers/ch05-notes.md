## S0001 Rajaraman (2012), pp. 29-43. Full text read 2026-10-12.

Page numbers are taken from the printed page number at the foot of each page.

### Section 5 opening, p. 29

The period 1978-1990 runs from the exit of IBM to Unix minicomputers and 'the beginning of software export through "body shopping" (i.e., sending software engineers to client sites abroad to develop software)'. It also covers offshore work by satellite, first by TI and then from STPs.

- Computers: 'less than 1000 in 1978' and 80,000 in 1990.
- Exports: 'insignificant' in 1978, USD 128 million in 1990.

### FERA and IBM, p. 29

- FERA, 1973: 'to conserve foreign exchange'. Foreign companies 'except those considered essential were to dilute equity to 40% and take an Indian partner'.
- IBM's position: it 'refused as it was not its global policy'.
- IBM's counter-offer:
  - a 100% export-oriented manufacturing unit, with export earnings used to import computers for sale in India;
  - '26% of its profit would be invested in an R and D Centre'.
- 'IBM was very confident that it would be considered "essential"'. It had 'over 150 IBM installations in India' (Railways, Planning Commission, textile and petrochemical firms, labs), and its data centres and service were 'excellent'.
- 'After prolonged negotiations the government was unwilling to relax FERA for IBM. Consequently IBM decided to withdraw from India in 1978.'
- Rajaraman points to Subramanian (S0025), pp. 161-176, for the negotiations. **Acquire.**
- IBM's Indian business: refurbishing and renting used 1401s, maintenance, card manufacture, data centres.

### What happened to IBM's business, pp. 29-30

- Refurbishing stopped.
- Rented computers 'were sold to the customers at their book value which was almost zero'.
- Card manufacture and data centres went to International Data Machines (IDM), formed by 'a group of ex-IBM employees'.
- Maintenance went to CMC, 'set up by the DoE in 1976 to maintain imported computers foreseeing such a contingency'.
  - Spares were sold to CMC 'at nominal price'.
  - 'Most employees of IBM joined CMC. There was thus no disruption of operations of computers' (ref [4] = Sharma).
  - **Conflict:** S0236 says around 150 employees, many with 'nowhere to go'. S0237 says one-third were laid off in 1976.
- ICL complied: it diluted to form ICIM with a local partner and made the ICL 2900 series from 1977 (p. 30).

### Politics, p. 30

- 1977: Janata coalition under Morarji Desai.
- 1978: Menon moved to the Department of Science and Technology; Biswajit Nag became DoE secretary and Electronics Commission chairman.
- 1980: the coalition fell; Indira Gandhi returned (Rajaraman writes 'in 1981').
  - That is an error in Rajaraman: she returned in January 1980. Check before use.
- 1981: Nag resigned; P.P. Gupta replaced him.
- 1984: Rajiv Gandhi became prime minister.

### Sondhi committee and the 1978 minicomputer policy, pp. 30-31

- The Mantosh Sondhi committee (secretary B. Nag) found 'long delays in importing computers due to the cumbersome procedures of the DoE' and suggested private manufacture.
- Emphasis on 'systems engineering companies' not necessarily making CPUs. Small-scale entrepreneurs were preferred for dedicated machines (data entry, accounting, typesetting).
- **'The policy also stated that it would continue to be restrictive in allowing use of computers in areas where labour displacement was likely to be involved.'** The labour thread continues from Ch. 3.
- Foreign collaboration was generally not permitted; up to 40% foreign equity in special cases.
- Licence required. Production value capped at Rs 20 million, with a limit on numbers.
- 'Only the public sector company ECIL would be allowed to manufacture 32 bit computers.'

### Asian Games 1982, p. 31

- Rajiv Gandhi organised the games. DCM computers served as terminals, linked to an HP server.
- The software was built in six months by NIC engineers.
- This brought N. Seshagiri (NIC Director General) close to Rajiv Gandhi.

### 1984 policy, pp. 31-32 (Ch. 8 material; noted here for the end-point)

- Anyone could make 32-bit machines; capacity limits were removed.
- Imported assembled boards were allowed: 'It changed manufacturing to assembly and system integration... Thus local manufacturing of boards slowly disappeared.'
- Software import was allowed. Software was recognised as an 'industry'.
- Customs duty on computers for export was reduced from 'over 100%'.
- **'Sending engineers abroad to develop and maintain software for clients at their sites and profits earned thereby was recognized as "software export"'** (p. 32).
  - This is the legal recognition of onsite work. Key for Ch. 6.
  - Note: the RBI and income-tax treatment needs a primary source.

### 1986 incentives, p. 32 (Ch. 8)

- Duty-free import of computers for export, with an obligation of 250% of cost within 4 years. Where no foreign exchange was spent (for example, an NRI bringing a machine), the obligation was 150%.
- Compare the TCS obligation in 1974 of 2x in 5 years (S0038).

### Telecom, p. 33

- 'In 1982 there was a waiting time of over 2 years to get a land line telephone connection.'
- MTNL and VSNL (1986); C-DOT 1984 (Pitroda).

### IIT Kanpur B.Tech CS, 1978, p. 34

- First IIT undergraduate CS programme, 'under my leadership'.
- Over 100,000 sat the entrance exam for 1,500 seats in 1978.
- The senate 'grudgingly allowed 20 students' because CS was not 'mainstream'.
- 'the rank of the last student admitted to the IITK CSc course was 40!' Other IITs followed.

### MCA, 1980 onwards, pp. 34-35

- The Panel on Computer Manpower Development (chaired by Rajaraman; he sat on the Electronics Commission 1979-82) recommended more CS undergraduate places and a new MCA for B.Sc and B.Com graduates. The MCA combined CS, maths and management, with a semester in an organisation.
- 'Initially the Government of India decided to fully fund ten institutions'.
- 'This programme which was uniquely Indian'.
- Ch. 7 and Ch. 12 material; note here as the human-capital response of 1980.

### 1981 export committee, p. 35

The 1981 Electronics Commission committee (Rajaraman) on allowing computer imports for software export 'recommended liberalization of import of computers for genuine exporters'.

### Rangarajan committee, p. 36

- RBI committee: all banks to have EDP cells and back-office computing; Unix as standard OS.
- Bank demand spurred private Unix minicomputers.
- Unions observed 1984 as an 'anti-computerization year'. S0237 says the BMS declared an 'Anti Computer Year', with 1 March as Anti-Computer Day.
- Changed by the 1986 railway reservation system.

### Consequences of the 1978 policy, pp. 39-40

- 'did not completely free the industry from the "licence-permit raj"'.
- Many licensees were traders who opted out.
- Firms that did manufacture: DCM Data Products (Delhi), ORG Systems (Baroda), Hindustan Computers Ltd (Delhi), PSI Data Systems (Bangalore), Wipro Systems (Bangalore), Patni Computers (Pune), IDM (Mumbai), Zenith Computers (Mumbai).
- 'They designed their systems from scratch using imported microprocessors... designed and fabricated printed circuit boards, mounted and soldered all the components and tested the systems.'
- **'As software could not be imported they had to develop operating systems, device drivers, compilers and application programs. UNIX was the operating system of choice as it was easily available'** (p. 40; ref [73] Vinay Deshpande, personal communication).
- Berkeley Unix was 'modified and adopted'. Rangarajan's Unix standard made the bank market 'large and could not be ignored'.
- **'Thus during 1978-1984 a large number of systems programmers in India became experts in UNIX development and usage. They also adopted C widely... This expertise came in handy when India entered the software services export market in the late 80s.'** (p. 40)
- Other effects:
  - 'considerable local design';
  - companies 'modified the UNIX OS and wrote compilers from scratch';
  - prices fell;
  - 'ECIL found it difficult to compete with these companies and lost many of their designers to the private sector companies' (p. 40).

### 1984 consequences, pp. 40-41

- Computer numbers grew 100% and costs fell 50% within two years.
- 'The old-guard derisively called such manufacturing "screw driver" manufacturing' (S0025, pp. 66, 73).
- Sterling's Shiva PC sold at Rs 29,000 in 1987.
- An anecdote of a 'consultant' who sourced Taiwanese, Singapore and Hong Kong parts for local 'manufacturers' who 'put their labels on them'.
- **'The emphasis on "self-reliance" and building computers using locally made components which was the cornerstone of the Bhabha Committee report was truly and completely buried.'** (p. 41)
- ECIL then collaborated with Norsk Data (1985) and CDC (1988, the Medha 810/830).

### Exports, p. 41

From 'insignificant' in 1978 to USD 131 million in 1990.

- Note: p. 29 says 128 million. The internal inconsistency is in the source itself.

### Training and lobbies, p. 42

- NIIT 1981 (franchising from 1987); Aptech.
- MAIT 1982; NASSCOM 1988 (38 members, rising to 464 by 1999).
- DoE secretaries were technocrats 1970-88; an IAS officer from 1989.

### Assessment, p. 42

- 'The only major failing was not investing enough resources to develop a local integrated circuit manufacturing industry.'
- Indian engineers could design LSI circuits for TI and others 'but had no foundry to fabricate them in India'.
- This is the key line for the Korea/Taiwan sidebar.

## S0056 Sharma, 'Full Story: George Fernandes and the Infamous IBM Exit', The Wire, 1 February 2019. Full text read (articleBody in page metadata).

- Fernandes is 'widely remembered for asking multinationals – IBM and Coca Cola – to fold up their operations'. But 'the stage was already set'. 'All that Fernandes did was to close the window opportunity available for the two companies to renegotiate'.
- Root: 'the 1973 amendment to the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) that capped foreign equity at 40%'. Firms above 40% 'had to obtain fresh approval from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to continue their operations'.
- PAC enquiry:
  - Following CAG observations on IBM's dealings with the Railways, the PAC under Hirendra Nath Mukherjee 'launched a full-scale enquiry into the functioning of IBM in November 1975'.
  - It found IBM had 'defrauded the country of enormous revenues by resorting to various unfair practices like transfer pricing under the garb of inter-company billing system, misuse of import entitlements'. That is about 25 words, usable.
  - 'IBM was found to dump used and obsolete mainframe computers in India at inflated prices.'
- IBM's offer:
  - split Indian operations into card manufacturing and data centres 'with 60% Indian equity as required under FERA', plus a fully owned export manufacturing unit;
  - 'But it was not willing to dilute its equity in its marketing and maintenance business – which was its core business.'
  - 'DoE was hopeful of a negotiated solution. Meanwhile, the government changed in March 1977.'
- Fernandes:
  - first communications minister, then industry minister, replacing Brij Lal Verma;
  - 'As a trade Union leader in Bombay, Fernandes had led anti-computer agitation of workers in the 1960s.' Links to Ch. 3.
  - Campaign line: India did not need 'foreign technology for making brassieres and tooth paste'.
- IBM approached the top; Morarji Desai sent it to Fernandes. Fernandes to Sharma, 2006 interview:
  - 'IBM was very cocky. They went to the extent of telling me that they have refused to accept what the French President, General Charles de Gaulle, had told them' (about 30 words);
  - '"if you think the General succumbed to you, I am telling you that I am not succumbing to you. You get out." Yes, I said "You get out".'
  - He also held that IBM computers 'were taking away jobs of Indian workers'.
  - This is a 29-years-later recollection by a politician claiming credit. The de Gaulle claim is unverified and should not be stated as fact.
- 'The file on IBM and Coca Cola came to him as FERA cases, and he took the decision based on recommendations of the FERA committee.'
- IBM came in 1951 'following an invitation from prime minister Jawaharlal Nehru' (unverified); 'As is' policy of refurbished discards 'kept the technology in Indian market one or two generations behind the latest'.
- 'When the DoE was set up in 1971' is an error; S0001 says 1970.

## S0055 Sharma, 'Rise, fall and rise of IBM in India', Business Today / Mail Today, 17 June 2011. Via WebFetch †.

- Entry 1951; 'market share of 80 per cent'.
- Trouble began in 1967, when the Electronics Committee under Vikram Sarabhai wanted IBM to stop 'bringing in old machines' and 'refurbishing and leasing them out at inflated rates to government departments' †.
- 'The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) only came in handy to facilitate IBM's exit.' 'The real reason for IBM's exit was a power struggle between a multinational firm and the government.' †
- 'It shut its operations in June 1978 and began exploring business with India in 1980.' 1986 proposal for a software and training institute; back February 1992 as Tata Information Systems Ltd; IBM India 1997 †.
- IBM 'created ... a pool of highly trained computer professionals in systems engineering, programming and maintenance' †.
- 'Ironically, CMC, which was set up to maintain IBM computers in India, became its first customer' (on return) †.

## S0081 Nadar, Knowledge at Wharton interview (Ravi Aron), 20 September 2006. Full text read.

- Origins: 'My colleagues and I were engineers who worked for DCM's calculator division.'
- The government's Electronics Commission 'was interested in making entrepreneurs out of executives and engineers. They asked us to write a report — and we did — saying that we could build an eight-bit machine.'
- 'We launched HCL in August 1976. Our most difficult challenge was getting permission from the government to make computers.' Licence obtained by a joint venture with the UP government: 'our toughest challenge was not designing or making computers but getting the government's approval to produce them.'
- Revenue ceilings under licensing. Rs 100 crore reached by 1989.
- **On IBM:** 'IBM wasn't evicted. The real story was different.'
  - He says the government ruled that a product made in India must have its source code reside in the country, and that 'the source code requirement' was 'the main reason why IBM decided to leave India, as did Coca-Cola'.
  - Equity dilution 'did not affect companies too much because they had to agree to dilute rather than dilute equity per se'.
  - **New dispute (q):** no other source mentions a source-code rule. The parallel with Coca-Cola's formula suggests he may be conflating the two. Needs Grieco (S0026) or Subramanian (S0025).
- 'if IBM had stayed on, we would have grown faster. See, IBM was fundamentally creating the market for computers in India.' 'At that time no one saw the computer as a necessity. It was a convenience.'
- Capital:
  - 'The total money was Rs. 20 lakhs [approximately $180,000]. One-fourth of that amount came from the U.P. government'.
  - The first (calculator marketing) company was started with '$16,000' from founders' savings.
  - Compare S0237: Microcomp with Rs 1.75 lakh.
- Age and pay:
  - 'I was 31, and I was the oldest.'
  - **'We offered a monthly salary of Rs. 2,000 [$180], which was more than what Citibank was paying at that time.'**
  - 'Otherwise, how do you get someone to work in an attic with a bedroom, a table and a chair?'
  - 'We hired five students from the Indian Institute of Management in Calcutta.'
  - This is the first salary datum for the book's pay series (1976, private computer firm, Rs 2,000 a month). It is a founder's recollection 30 years on.
- 1980s: wrote a relational database, Genesis, 'from scratch'.

## S0069 IISc Connect, 'IISc's role in helping Wipro launch its minicomputers in 1981' (December 2019). Full text read.

- The piece says FERA came in 1974 (an error) and that the Desai government 'forcing IBM to exit operations' (institutional framing).
- Sridhar Mitta (joined Wipro 1980 as R&D manager): the world was moving from 'expensive vertically integrated systems' to 'more open and cheaper, horizontally integrated systems'. Mitta says the Desai government 'issued a directive asking Indian companies to develop minicomputers with microprocessors'.
- Wipro was 'until then in the business of producing vegetable and refined oils'.
- Consultancy agreement with IISc's CSIC, July 1979, about Rs 14 lakh.
  - IISc Registrar's letter, 5 October 1979, to Ashok Narasimhan (Wipro GM Finance & Planning): project for 'design and development of microprocessor-based minicomputer systems'; know-how to be transferred 'in another fifteen months'.
- Premji (convocation, September 2019): 'it would be quite accurate to say that the Wipro IT business was born in IISc' (company founder; label).
- N.J. Rao (head, Digital Systems Lab): importing Intel's Microprocessor Development System 'would have been really difficult for a company... So IISc procured it.'
  - This is the import-licence barrier, worked around through a university.
- Product and team:
  - Wipro Series-86 launched 1981: Intel 8086, Multibus, OS licensed from Sentinel Computer Corporation (US). Housing by a CEDT team under Kishore Babu.
  - First-year revenue Rs 2 crore (Mitta).
  - First office 45 Dickenson Road, Bangalore.
- Early staff:
  - Y. Narahari (then a BE student, later EECS chair): 'Out of the first few employees of Wipro, several were trained in this lab.'
  - Named early hires: Victor Jayakaran, Vallab Kulkarni, Srikant Seshadri, Anand Talwai, Anujan, Varma, Ravi Bail.
  - Narahari was offered a Wipro job in early 1982 but chose the ME programme.
  - These are rank-and-file names with no first-person voice, except Narahari's.

## S0237 Dataquest, '1976-1985: The Big Blue Impact' (30 December 2006). Via WebFetch †.

- 1976: T.B. Finn became GM of IBM India and 'lays off one-third of the existing workforce' within four months. 'Obviously, IBM had already made up its mind to exit India.'
- 1976: Nadar leaves DCM DP and starts Microcomp 'with six friends' and Rs 1.75 lakh, renamed HCL.
- 1977: NIC with UNDP help (Seshagiri).
- 1978: 'IBM exits from India on June 1, as it could not agree with FERA regulations.' ICL becomes ICIM. 'CMC takes up maintenance of 800 IBM installations across the country.' 'The rest of IBM's business passes on to IDM.'
  - **Conflict:** 800 here against S0001's 'over 150 IBM installations'. 800 may count all IBM-maintained equipment, including unit-record machines. Note it; do not choose.
- 1980: Premji takes Wipro into IT; Raj Saraf founds Zenith.
- 1981: Infosys (Pune, Rs 10,000) and NIIT.
- 1983: Rangarajan committee formed; Indonet announced.
- 1984: New Computer Policy, 19 November. The BMS declares an 'Anti Computer Year', with 1 March as Anti-Computer Day. Aptech founded. Wipro Infotech under Ashok Soota.
- 1985: Rangarajan report (Stage I 2,500 branches; Stage II 6,000). CMC procures '100 minis from foreign vendors and 400 from Indian sources' for RBI. 'Unix arrives in India'.
  - Conflicts with S0001, which has Unix in use from 1978-84. The DQ line probably means AT&T-licensed Unix; check.
- Trade-press retrospective, unsourced. Use for leads, not facts.
