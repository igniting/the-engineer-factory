## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

India's first computers were bought and built by scientists for the state, not for a market. At the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta, P.C. Mahalanobis wanted machines to handle the flood of survey data that national planning produced (S0217, S0010). He tried for an American UNIVAC from 1950 to 1954 and failed, partly because a 1953 US Embassy dispatch called him a Soviet sympathiser (S0007†). He then bought a small British machine, the HEC-2M, which two ISI engineers learned to install by watching it being built in London (S0001), and in 1958 obtained a Soviet URAL through UN technical assistance (S0001, S0007†). For a few years ISI was India's de facto national computer centre (S0001).

In Bombay, Homi Bhabha's Tata Institute of Fundamental Research took the other route: build one. A team of about six physicists, only one of whom had been abroad and none of whom had used a computer, built a pilot machine by September 1956 and a full-scale computer by February 1959 (S0013, S0012). It then sat idle for most of 1959 for want of air-conditioning, and was commissioned in February 1960; Nehru named it TIFRAC in 1962 (S0012, S0013). By the time it ran, its designer judged it obsolete; in 1962-63 TIFR chose to buy an American CDC 3600 with a US aid loan (S0013, S0001).

The chapter's turn: building one machine produced a trained group, not an industry. Half a dozen TIFRAC engineers went to American universities between 1959 and 1962, and three stayed (S0013). The education was on the job and tiny; the profession was government science. The 1958 Scientific Policy Resolution already imagined exporting skills in return for raw materials (S0220 via S0001), an early statement of what India would later sell. The national choice between building and buying computers, and the politics of foreign exchange and aid, set up the rest of the book.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 Computing before computers: ISI and the data of planning

The Indian Statistical Institute was founded in Calcutta in 1931 under Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis (S0001, p. 15; S0010†). In its early decades 'computers' were people: Mahalanobis designated statistical staff as computers to tabulate survey data such as jute crop estimates (S0010†). By late 1951, according to a 1953 National Sample Survey report quoted by Gopalakrishnan and co-authors, ISI had two new models of IBM tabulators (S0010†). Mahalanobis helped set up the National Sample Survey in 1950 (S0010†).

The historian Nikhil Menon argues that the decision to plan India's economy centrally, and the 'explosion of official statistics' it produced, provided the justification for the pursuit of computers; ISI's influence culminated in its effectively authoring the Second Five Year Plan (1956-61) (S0217, abstract). Rajaraman, a participant-historian, also records that the Second Plan was drafted by Mahalanobis (S0001, p. 13 [check page]). Menon stresses that computers were sought for development and planning, not for military use (S0217, prize citation; S0008 summary).

Mahalanobis first saw an electronic computer at Harvard in 1947 (S0007†). In 1950 ISI opened a laboratory under the engineer S.K. Mitra (S0007†), and by 1953 that laboratory had delivered an analogue computer built from Second World War surplus, able to solve simultaneous linear equations in up to ten variables (S0007†; S0010† calls it 'India's first electrical analogue computing machine'). At about the same time V. Rajaraman, then at the Indian Institute of Science, was working on a differential analyser (S0010†).

### 3.2 The Cold War search for a digital machine

From 1950 to 1954 Mahalanobis held informal talks in Washington about acquiring a UNIVAC; the campaign failed, partly because a 1953 secret dispatch from the US Embassy in Delhi described him as a 'notorious fellow traveler and sympathizer of the Soviet Union' (S0007†). Menon's peer-reviewed article reports the same constraint: Mahalanobis was hampered in sourcing a US computer by perceptions that he was too close to the Soviet Union (S0217, prize citation). The endnotes of that article document a run of letters from Mahalanobis to his colleague Pitambar Pant, who ran an ISI cell in Delhi, written from Geneva (12 April 1954), London (23 June), Prague (27 June) and Moscow (7 and 17 July 1954) (S0217, nn. 46-69). These letters are held in the Pitambar Pant Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library (S0217); their contents were not seen.

In February 1954, at a lunch hosted by Nehru, Mahalanobis pressed the Soviet ambassador Mikhail Menshikov for help (S0007†). Gopalakrishnan and co-authors say the USSR and the UN agreed some eight months later to fund a digital computer purchase (S0010†). Soviet correspondence went directly to Mahalanobis rather than through diplomatic channels, which irritated the Indian diplomat Y.D. Gundevia (S0217, n. 58). Menon adds that Mahalanobis had fixed on the UNIVAC since the 1940s, partly because of its fame in American popular culture, and that the machine would have cost about a million dollars (S0007†).

In summer 1954 Mahalanobis, Mitra and a delegation of Indian scientists visited the Soviet Union; in Moscow they saw the Institute of Precision Mechanics and Computer Engineering, which was building a computer with the backing of Nikita Khrushchev, and Mahalanobis persuaded Soviet authorities to give India a machine, which became the URAL (S0007†). Writing from London that summer he spoke of 'a great sense of urgency' and said real planning would 'require the use of such computers' (S0007†, quoting his letter; the fuller wording circulated in S0010† is not yet traced).

### 3.3 The HEC-2M: buying a machine without support

The first electronic digital computer in India was a British machine. According to Rajaraman, Mahalanobis ordered it from the British Tabulating Machine Company (BTM), which sold punched-card equipment in India; BTM agreed in 1954 to sell a computer to ISI 'without any technical support', so that ISI had to install and maintain it with its own staff (S0001, p. 15). In Rajaraman's later interview account, BTM 'was not willing to instal and maintain the machine in India, because only one machine was coming to the country' (S0010†).

ISI sent two engineers, Mohi (Monimohan) Mukherjee and Amresh Roy, to England in December 1954; the HEC-2M (Hollerith Electronic Computer Model 2M) was designed by A.D. Booth at Birkbeck College and assembled in BTM's London workshop, and the two men watched it being built and gathered the information they would need to run and maintain it (S0001, p. 15, citing Mukherjee's own memoir, S0015). Menon gives the price as GBP 18,500, paid by the Government of India, which lent the machine to ISI (S0007†). He describes it as six feet high, ten long and seven wide, in three metal cabinets (S0007†).

The machine had a drum memory of 1,024 words of 24 bits, was programmed in machine language in binary, and used punched-card input and output and a printer (S0001, pp. 15-16); Gopalakrishnan and co-authors say it had no printer or tape (S0010†): a discrepancy on the printer. Rajaraman writes that 'around a dozen people learnt to program the computer', which was used for some statistical calculations (S0001, p. 16), and that it 'was found to be a very difficult machine to use' (S0001, p. 16). It also processed requests from the Planning Commission and the National Sample Survey (S0051, weak; confirmed by S0007†).

Menon reports that the HEC-2M was used mainly for calculation requests from the Planning Commission and the National Sample Survey, and for other scientific institutions; Mahalanobis had judged the analogue machine too weak for planning work (S0007†). He also notes the machine took nine months to build, three to ship and two to assemble (S0007†).

The arrival date is disputed (see §6).

### 3.4 The URAL: a Soviet gift and Russian manuals

Mahalanobis then looked for a second machine; foreign exchange was the obstacle (S0001, p. 16). A URAL-1 was ordered in 1958 with UN technical assistance; it was a first-generation vacuum-tube machine with a drum memory, punched tape and magnetic tape, 40-bit words and about 12,000 operations a second, installed by Russian engineers; the manuals were in Russian and ISI engineers learned Russian to maintain it (S0001, p. 16). Gopalakrishnan and co-authors say it came with a complete set of manuals and eight Russian engineers (S0010†); Menon says it was unveiled in Calcutta in December 1958 and that the *Times of India* reported it could calculate '600 times faster than a single man' (S0007†). The machine was formally transferred to the Government of India and lent to ISI, the government retaining 'complete freedom to utilize the equipment at their discretion' (S0217, n. 87, citing PCMMMA Record 119). ISI's house magazine *Samvadadhvam* described the URAL in its July-September 1958 issue (S0001 ref. 22; S0217 n. 89).

In 1958, Rajaraman writes, ISI was the only institution in India with two working computers and a group that could program and maintain them; it was the de facto national computer centre, used by universities, the atomic energy establishment and defence laboratories (S0001, p. 16). Menon lists about a dozen institutional users, including Andhra University, IISc, IIT Kharagpur, TIFR and the Atomic Energy Commission (S0217, n. 92).

In February 1959 Morton Nadler, a 37-year-old American engineer who had worked at Czechoslovakia's Institute of Mathematical Machines and had been suspected of espionage by both American and Czechoslovak authorities, flew from Prague to Calcutta to take a two-year contract at ISI (S0007†). He worked mainly at the Computer Laboratory as a consultant; his lectures at ISI in spring 1959 became the book *Topics in Engineering Logic* (1962); he left for Bull in Paris in 1960 (S0217, n. 27). His memoir *No Regrets* (S0218) has not been obtained. Menon's account of Nadler's background explains why ISI could hire him at all: he had joined the Communist Party in 1936, lost a radio-engineering job in Chicago over his politics, moved to Prague in 1948, had his passport seized by the US embassy in 1950 over alleged betrayal of radar secrets and renounced his citizenship; disillusioned after the Soviet suppression of the 1956 Hungarian uprising, he could neither leave Czechoslovakia easily nor return to the US, and Mahalanobis's job offer gave him a way out (S0007†). The episode shows how thin India's pool of computer expertise was, and how far Mahalanobis would go to fill it.

### 3.5 TIFR: deciding to build

Bhabha founded TIFR in Bombay in 1945 (S0001, p. 15). Computing activity began there in 1954 in the Instrumentation Group; towards the end of 1954 the institute decided 'to design and build a fullscale, general purpose, electronic digital computer using contemporary technology' (S0013, p. 448). Narasimhan, who led the project, credits Bhabha's 'acceptance and active support' as essential and later names Sarabhai and M.G.K. Menon as his other benefactors (S0013, p. 447).

Narasimhan himself had a B.E. (Hons) from the College of Engineering, Guindy (1947), and spent 1948-1954 in the United States on a part-loan scholarship from the J.N. Tata Endowment (S0013, p. 448). The project team at the start numbered about six, most with an M.S. in physics specialising in electronics; except for Narasimhan none had been outside India, and none, including him, had any experience of using or operating a computer, let alone building one (S0013, p. 448; S0216† gives the same picture). The team therefore worked in two phases: a pilot machine to try out circuit and logic design and to train personnel on the job, then a full-scale computer (S0013, p. 448).

### 3.6 The pilot machine and TIFRAC

Narasimhan's paper, received by the Indian Academy of Sciences on 14 March 1960, a few weeks after commissioning, is the contemporary record (S0012, p. 47). It says the Computer Section had been working since 1955; a pilot 'digital calculator' was completed in September 1956 and kept running for about a year; design of the full-scale machine began early in 1957 and its final assembly was completed in February 1959 (S0012, p. 47). His 1999 memoir gives the pilot as operational by October 1956 and, in its table, commissioned in November 1956 (S0013, pp. 448-449).

The full-scale machine then waited. 'Owing to the lack of air-conditioning facilities, the work had to be suspended till almost the end of 1959'; testing began in mid-November 1959 with an auxiliary air-conditioning system, and the computer was commissioned for routine work in the third week of February 1960 (S0012, p. 47; S0013, p. 448 agrees on February 1960).

It was a parallel, binary, asynchronous, stored-programme machine with single-address instructions and a ferrite-core memory of 1,024 words; input was by punched paper tape (5-hole teletype tape) and output printed or punched (S0012, p. 47). It used 2,700 vacuum tubes, almost all double triodes, 1,700 germanium diodes and about 12,500 resistors; no circuit was transistorised; power consumption was about 18 kW (S0012, p. 48). Words were 40 bits (S0012, p. 48). TIFR's archive says the memory was later expanded to 2,048 words (S0216†), which matches the 1999 table (S0013, p. 449, which also gives 20 kW). Its control logic, Narasimhan wrote in 1999, 'benefited crucially through design details kindly made available by the ILLIAC team' at the University of Illinois (S0013, p. 449). P.V.S. Rao, a junior team member, is quoted describing how the team 'strung the memory matrix together', threading hair-thin enamelled wires through sub-millimetre magnetic cores held in Plexiglass frames (S0052†; original probably S0219).

The machine was named TIFRAC in 1962, when Prime Minister Nehru inaugurated TIFR's new building (S0013, p. 448; S0216†; S0001, p. 16). TIFR's archive expands the name as 'TIFR Automatic Calculator' (S0216†; also S0001, p. 16), while Narasimhan in 1999 wrote 'TIFR Automatic Computer' and called it 'the first fullscale electronic digital computer designed, built, and operated in India' (S0013, p. 447).

### 3.7 What TIFRAC did, and what it taught

TIFRAC ran from 1960 to early 1964, by the end on two shifts a day (S0013, p. 449; S0216†). Its users were TIFR scientists, the Atomic Energy Establishment and some universities, including a crystallography group in Madras; analysis of TIFR's cosmic-ray air-shower experiments would not have been feasible without it (S0013, p. 449). Programming support was an interpretive routine for floating-point arithmetic; the memory was too small for any compiler (S0013, p. 450).

Narasimhan's verdict on its technical place is blunt: the pilot was close to the state of the art in 1954 and the 1957 design 'not very much behind', but by commissioning in 1960 TIFRAC was 'an obsolete first generation machine'; second-generation computers needed production know-how that only large manufacturers had (S0013, p. 449). Its lasting outputs were people: the computer section grew from about half a dozen to some two dozen; a 'hard core of specialists' could design digital equipment; a group was trained in the preventive maintenance of complex electronics; and, in his words, many Indian computer users 'had their first introduction to programming through the use of TIFRAC' (S0013, pp. 449-450).

### 3.8 The first brain drain

Between 1959 and 1962 about half a dozen of the scientists who had designed and built TIFRAC took visiting appointments at American universities; three settled permanently in the US, one returned in 1962 after two years on a second-generation computer project at Illinois, and others returned later (S0013, p. 450). Narasimhan himself spent 1961-1964 as a visiting scientist at the Digital Computer Laboratory, University of Illinois (S0013, p. 450; S0001, p. 16 gives 1961-63). This is the earliest documented case in this book of Indian-trained computer engineers being drawn to the United States (link to Ch. 13).

### 3.9 From building to buying: the CDC 3600

The presence of TIFR staff in the US with working knowledge of computers allowed TIFR to set up an expert committee, chaired by Narasimhan, to assess commercial large systems; it compared the IBM 7094, CDC 3600, Philco 211 and Univac 1107 and recommended the CDC 3600, of which only one was then operating, at the maker's Minneapolis plant (S0013, p. 450). The CDC 3600-160A was bought with a US aid loan for $1.5 million (S0001, p. 16; S0217, n. 124), commissioned at TIFR in mid-June 1964 (S0013, p. 450; S0217 n. 124 gives May 1964), and from October 1964 ran as a National Computation Centre, maintained and operated entirely by TIFR staff (S0013, p. 450). Rajaraman says over 150 institutions used it for more than a decade (S0001, p. 16); Gopalakrishnan and co-authors put its uptime above 98 per cent from 1965 to 1970 (S0052†). Bhabha persuaded the government to arrange the funding (S0001, p. 16).

### 3.10 ISI's last build, and the national picture

When TIFRAC was inaugurated in 1960, ISI decided to build a transistorised machine with Jadavpur University; ISIJU was completed in 1966 but was unreliable and lacked software (S0001, pp. 16-17). Other dates for it range from 1964 to 1965-66 (S0010†; S0217 n. 122 says begun 1962, completed 1966, unsuccessful). The UN did not supply a card reader and line printer free of charge, and voltage fluctuations disrupted its data link (S0010†).

Menon counts 15 digital computers in India by the end of 1964 (S0007†); Rajaraman counts twelve installed in R&D organisations and two in educational institutions between 1961 and 1964 (S0001, p. 18 [check]). In autumn 1965 the Planning Commission opened its own computer centre in Delhi, an IBM 1620 bought with a Ford Foundation grant (Rs 23 lakh, S0007†; $462,000, S0217 n. 129), run 'open shop' on a no-profit-no-loss basis, one shift a day until 1968 (S0217, n. 129).

### 3.11 The policy frame

Parliament's Scientific Policy Resolution of 1958 declared that science and technology 'can make up for deficiencies in raw materials by providing substitutes, or, indeed by providing skills which can be exported in return for raw materials', and aimed to train scientific and technical personnel 'with all possible speed' (S0220, quoted in S0001, p. 14). Rajaraman notes that apart from this resolution there was no government policy on computers before the 1960s (S0001, p. 18). Bhabha headed an electronics committee from 1963 after the 1962 war (S0001; S0052†); that story belongs to Ch. 3.

### 3.12 Education and profession in this chapter

*Education.* There was no computer education in India before the machines. Programming was learned on the job by a few dozen people: 'around a dozen' at ISI on the HEC-2M (S0001, p. 16), ISI engineers learning Russian for the URAL (S0001, p. 16), Nadler's 1959 lectures at ISI (S0217, n. 27), and TIFR's group learning by building (S0013). The engineers came from physics M.Sc. programmes and electrical engineering (S0013, p. 448). The first formal programming courses came with IIT Kanpur's IBM 1620 in 1963 (Ch. 2).

*Profession.* Computing was a scientific civil service: salaried posts at state-funded institutes (ISI, TIFR, AEE), working on statistics, physics and planning. There was no market for programmers, no private employer and no export. The one market signal was the outflow of trained people to American universities (S0013, p. 450).


### 3.13 A new interpretation: the 'external constraint' argument (Banerjee 2026)

Dwaipayan Banerjee's *Computing in the Age of Decolonization* (Princeton, February 2026) is the most recent scholarly history of this period (S0221). It has not been read; what follows rests on the publisher's description (S0221) and an MIT News summary (S0222), both checked live on 2026-10-12 and both after the knowledge cutoff of this project's drafting model. According to these, Banerjee argues that India's effort from the 1950s to the 1980s to build a self-reliant computing industry was blocked by Cold War politics, trade imbalances, US corporate monopolies and the preference of India's technocratic elite for profitable technical services (S0221); that TIFRAC was never replicated or produced at scale; that Cold War restrictions limited the flow of American blueprints; that IBM's FORTRAN needed about four times TIFRAC's memory; that India's 1958 foreign-exchange crisis made larger memory stacks unaffordable; and that World Bank-led rescue loans were conditioned on opening markets to Western capital (S0222). The summary quotes him: 'This is very much an external constraint story' (S0222).

This interpretation bears directly on the book's working thesis (scarce foreign exchange as a shaping constraint) and on central question 1. It also competes with Narasimhan's own account, which stresses that second-generation machines required production know-how only large manufacturers had (S0013, p. 449) and that TIFR chose to buy rather than build (S0013, p. 450). Note also that the ILLIAC team did share design details with TIFR (S0013, p. 449), which qualifies any claim that US knowledge was simply withheld. The publisher's description refers to 'the expulsion of IBM', which conflicts with this book's provisional verdict on myth M03 (S0001, S0055: IBM chose to leave). Reading S0221 is a priority before drafting Chs 1, 3 and 5.

## 6. Disputes

1. **HEC-2M arrival.** July 1955, working August 1955, per Mukherjee's memoir as summarised by Rajaraman (S0001, p. 15); 'mid-1955' (S0010†); 'early 1956', in two crates (S0007†, Menon, archival). **Unresolved.** Handling: give 1955 as the date of installation reported by the engineer who installed it, and note that a historian working from ISI papers dates the arrival to early 1956.
2. **TIFR pilot completion.** September 1956 (S0012, written 1960) vs October 1956 (S0013 text) vs November 1956 (S0013 table). **Resolved** in favour of the contemporary 1960 paper; variance noted.
3. **TIFRAC's name.** 'TIFR Automatic Calculator' (S0216†, S0001) vs 'TIFR Automatic Computer' (S0013). **Resolved:** use 'Calculator' with a note that its designer later wrote 'Computer'.
4. **TIFRAC naming date.** 1962 at the new building's inauguration (S0013, S0216†, S0001) vs accounts placing Nehru's naming at commissioning in 1960 (popular sources). **Resolved:** 1962.
5. **TIFRAC memory and power.** 1,024 words, 18 kW at commissioning (S0012) vs 2,048 words, 20 kW (S0013 table). **Resolved:** expanded later (S0216†).
6. **HEC-2M printer.** Had a printer (S0001) vs no printer or tape (S0010†). **Unresolved**; avoid the detail.
7. **CDC 3600 installation month.** Mid-June 1964 (S0013) vs May 1964 (S0217 n. 124). Minor; say 'mid-1964'.
8. **ISIJU completion.** 1964 (Wikipedia on Mitra), 1965-66 (S0010†), 1966 (S0001, S0217). Use 'completed in 1966' (two independent sources).
9. **Interpretation: was TIFRAC a success?** Narasimhan: a success in know-how and people but obsolete on arrival (S0013). A critical view (that it was never replicated or produced at scale) appeared in commentary found in Phase 1 but not registered; register it before use. Both can be true; the chapter should present the 'build vs buy' choice as a real debate.
10. **Why India did not build a computer industry.** Banerjee (S0221, S0222): external constraints (Cold War, forex, monopolies) plus an elite preference for services. Narasimhan (S0013): production know-how and economics of second-generation machines; buying was prudent. Both to be presented; S0221 must be read.
11. **'Expulsion of IBM'** (S0221 publisher description) vs IBM's choice to leave (S0001, S0055). See myth M03 and Ch. 5.

## 7. Documented scenes

1. **The Menshikov lunch, February 1954.** At a lunch hosted by Nehru, Mahalanobis presses the Soviet ambassador for computer help, arguing India could be a useful partner (S0007†). Quote available via S0007† (to verify).
2. **Two engineers in a London workshop, December 1954 onward.** Mukherjee and Roy watch the HEC-2M being assembled at BTM, taking notes because no support will come with the machine (S0001, p. 15, from S0015).
3. **Unpacking in Calcutta, 1955 (or early 1956).** The machine arrives in two crates without manuals; the two engineers install and test it from their notes; it works by August 1955 (S0001, p. 15; S0007† for crates).
4. **The URAL unveiled, December 1958.** Eight Soviet engineers assemble it from Russian manuals; ISI engineers learn Russian; the *Times of India* reports it can calculate 600 times faster than a single man (S0001, p. 16; S0007†; S0010†).
5. **Morton Nadler's flight, February 1959.** An American engineer under suspicion in both Prague and Washington flies Air India to Calcutta to join ISI (S0007†; S0217 n. 27).
6. **The finished computer that could not run, 1959.** TIFR's machine is assembled in February 1959 but sits idle for most of the year because there is no air-conditioned room; testing begins in November with an auxiliary system (S0012, p. 47; S0013, p. 448).
7. **Hand-wiring the memory.** Young team members thread hair-thin wire through tiny ferrite rings in Plexiglass frames (S0052†, quoting P.V.S. Rao; original S0219 to obtain).
8. **Nehru names the machine, 1962.** At the inauguration of TIFR's new building, the Prime Minister names the computer TIFRAC (S0013, p. 448; S0216†, with photograph of Nehru, Bhabha and D.Y. Phadke beside it).
9. **Two shifts a day, 1963-64.** In its last year TIFRAC runs two shifts to meet demand from physicists and universities (S0013, p. 449; S0216†).
10. **Choosing the CDC 3600, 1962-63.** A TIFR committee chaired by Narasimhan, staffed by engineers then working in American universities, compares four American machines and picks one that exists only in its maker's Minneapolis plant (S0013, p. 450).
