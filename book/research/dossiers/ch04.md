**Chapter question.** How did a Tata data-processing unit become the template for an export industry that sold Indian programmers' time abroad, and what did the foreign-exchange rules have to do with it?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Founding.** TCS began in 1967-68 as a service bureau for the Tata group's own data processing, not as an exporter (S0228†; S0039; S0231, PDF p. 4). F.C. Kohli, an MIT-trained power engineer from Tata Electric, took over as general manager in 1969. He became director-in-charge in 1974 (S0038†; S0228†). He had learned computing on IIT Kanpur's IBM 1620 and recruited Kanpur's graduates (S0003, pp. 800-801).

**Early machines and domestic work.** TCS ran on leased IBM machines and a second-hand ICL 1903. By Kohli's account the 1903 was bought cheaply from a government-owned insurer after a union blocked its installation as a 'job-eating monster' (S0038†). The early domestic work was bank reconciliation, payroll and a telephone directory (S0038†; S0039). It did not pay well enough: 'TCS was up, but it wasn't running' (S0038†).

**The turn to export.** Exports came from scarcity. Imports of computers needed foreign exchange, and clearance took one to three years (S0001, p. 23). From 1972-74 the Department of Electronics let firms import computers if they earned the cost back in exports (S0001, p. 28; S0230†).

**Burroughs.** TCS tied itself to Burroughs, IBM's nearest rival. Lacking a Burroughs machine, its engineers wrote software on the ICL and converted it with a 'filter' program (S0038†; S0228†). It then imported a Burroughs mainframe on condition of exporting twice its value within five years, and did so (S0038†; S0001, p. 28).

**Onsite work.** Burroughs also asked for programmers to install software at its US customers' sites (S0230†). That onsite arrangement, later called 'body shopping', became the industry's first business model (S0230†).

**Patni.** In parallel, Narendra Patni, from Cambridge, Massachusetts, had Pune operators key American documents onto tape from 1972 (S0229†). His later firm hired N.R. Narayana Murthy in 1977 (S0229†; S0034).

**What is missing.** The evidence is heavily from founders and the company. No rank-and-file TCS or Patni employee from the 1970s has yet been found in print. The contract terms (per person, per hour or fixed price) are unknown, and that is the central gap for the book's thesis.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 A bureau for the group (1967-1969)

TCS did not begin as an exporter. Sharma, the industry's most thorough historian, writes that it 'had been established in 1967 as Tata Computer Centre to handle the data-processing work of all companies of the Tata group' (S0228†). Bassett, writing from archival work and interviews with Kohli, also says Tata 'established a computing enterprise in 1967' (S0003, p. 800).

The Tata group's own pages, and Kohli's 2009 speech, give 1968 (S0039; S0235; S0231, PDF p. 4). Kohli said: 'TCS was set up in 1968 to partake in the new technology' (S0231, PDF p. 4). The likely reconciliation is that an in-house computer centre came first and was constituted as TCS in 1968. The chapter should say '1967-68' and explain the difference in a note (dispute (k)).

Sharma names the founding team as three MIT management graduates who had interned with the Tatas: Lalit Surajmal Kanodia, Nitin Patel and Ashok Malhotra. He adds Yashpal Sahni, 'a postgraduate of the Indian Statistical Institute' (S0228†). The early staff therefore came partly from management schools and statistics, not from engineering colleges. The Tata group's own tribute says Kohli 'recruited the most promising engineers, chartered accountants, and science and maths postgraduates' (S0235†). Both points complicate the later picture of an industry built by IIT engineers (myths file).

The work was the work of a service bureau. Tata's own list is 'bank reconciliation services, payroll and accounting' (S0235†; S0039). Kohli's profile in *MIT Technology Review* adds inter-branch reconciliation for a nationalised bank and the Bombay telephone directory (S0038†). Rajaraman notes that most Tata companies, like the textile firms, already had IBM 1401s, which they rented from IBM (S0001, p. 19). A group bureau made sense where computers were scarce and expensive: in about 1970 a high-end 1401 rented for about Rs 850,000 a year (S0001, p. 18).

### 3.2 The control engineer

Kohli's route into computing ran through power engineering, not mathematics or physics. Born in Peshawar in 1924, he took a physics degree at Government College Lahore. In 1946, 'before the partition', he went to Queen's University in Canada for an electrical engineering degree (S0036, pp. 2-3). He then worked about a year at General Electric, took an MIT master's on a scholarship, and learned load dispatch at New England Power (S0036, p. 3).

He returned to Tata Electric in 1951 to set up what he called 'the first load dispatch in India' (S0036, p. 3). In his telling, digital computer control of the Bombay grid followed in 1968 (S0231, PDF p. 4). In 2020 he said 1969 (S0036, p. 3).

He was also tied into the American-designed engineering education of the period:

- With P.K. Kelkar he set up a control-engineering master's at VJTI Bombay in the late 1950s (S0003, p. 791).
- He sat on IIT Kanpur's electrical engineering faculty-selection committee (S0003, p. 800).
- He learned computing on Kanpur's IBM 1620, arriving at the ten-day course by cycle rickshaw (S0003, pp. 800-801 and n. 43).

Bassett argues that 'American-oriented' elites such as Kohli and Patni 'gave a special potency to the computing work of IIT Kanpur graduates' (S0003, p. 802). That is because they had the connections abroad that the graduates lacked.

Kohli moved to TCS as general manager in 1969, at J.R.D. Tata's request (S0036, p. 3; S0038†; S0228†). Sharma says he was then deputy general manager at Tata Electric and 'succeeded Kanodia' (S0228†). The *Technology Review* profile says that in 1974, 'when the director of TCS died', Kohli took over (S0038†). An obituary carried by PTI (via search summary) gives the three steps:

- general manager, 1969;
- director-in-charge, 1974;
- later chief executive.

The director who died in 1974 is not named in any source seen. The 1969 and 1974 changes are different events, which resolves dispute (l) in part.

### 3.3 Machines on a budget, and a union's veto

TCS's equipment shows how scarce foreign exchange shaped the business. According to the *Technology Review* account, based on Kohli's interviews, TCS's first mainframe was an ICL 1903. It was bought cheaply from a government-owned insurance company after a labour union blocked its installation, calling it a 'job-eating monster' (S0038†). Two leased IBM machines supplemented it (S0038†). Rajaraman says TCS's first computer was an IBM 1401 (S0001, p. 19). That fits if the IBMs were leased first and the ICL was the first machine TCS owned (dispute (m)).

The insurer is not named. If it was the Life Insurance Corporation, the story ties directly to Chapter 3. There, the All India Insurance Employees' Association's campaign against LIC's computer (dated from 1965 by the Committee on Automation, S0020, p. 2) became the national test case of white-collar resistance.

Even unnamed, the episode is telling. The union's veto in one public enterprise produced a cheap machine for a private firm, and that firm then turned it to work that did not displace Indian clerks: work for foreign clients. This link is an inference from two separate sources, so it must be written as one. It needs a second source naming the insurer and year before the draft treats it as fact.

Imports of new machines were slow and uncertain. Rajaraman, then on the government's side of several committees, writes that import clearances took one to three years unless UNDP or a foreign government paid, and that this 'hurt software exporters' as well as laboratories and universities (S0001, p. 23). The 1971 war brought American embargoes on electronics and computers, and the 1974 nuclear test brought an embargo on advanced computers and scientific software (S0001, p. 23). The 1973 oil shock made foreign exchange scarcer still (S0001, p. 23). Tata's own tribute to Kohli says customers valued TCS's 'quality service at a compelling cost, even more so after the 1973 OPEC oil crisis' (S0235†). That is the company's own statement of the cost advantage.

Sharma adds that IBM and ICL 'followed the practice of importing used machines'. After an audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General, the government reined them in and gave the task to the new Department of Electronics (S0228†). The parliamentary side of this, the Public Accounts Committee's 1975-76 report, is in Chapter 3 (S0022, via S0001, p. 23).

### 3.4 'Up, but not running'

The domestic market could not sustain the company. Kohli's own summary, as quoted in *Technology Review*, was blunt: 'TCS was up, but it wasn't running' (S0038†). The profile says the company had struggled and took on management-consulting work to stay afloat (S0038†).

Late in life Kohli gave a different and simpler account. In 2020 he said: 'First few years it was built only on exports. People didn't understand.' (S0036, p. 5) He also said: 'There was no question of my going for business. The people came to us' (S0036, p. 4). Both statements are contradicted by better evidence:

- the domestic bureau work listed by the company itself (S0039; S0235);
- Sharma's finding that Kohli visited the United States 'at least nine times between 1972 and 1974' to find work (S0228†).

The oral history was recorded when Kohli was 95, and he corrected the interviewer's dates several times (S0036, pp. 2, 5). It is best used for his framing, not for facts.

A second witness confirms that the domestic market was thin. Narayana Murthy started a firm called Softronics 'within a couple of years' of returning from France, aimed at the Indian market. He closed it in less than a year: 'there was no domestic market in India for software. There were hardly any computers in India' (S0034, p. 4). Murthy's conclusion, that the market 'has to be a global market, a market particularly in the United States', led him to Patni (S0034, p. 4). This is a founder's retrospective account. Its value is that it comes from outside TCS and points the same way.

### 3.5 The export rule

The policy that turned scarcity into an export business is dated differently by different sources.

- Aspray, a historian of computing, says the Department of Electronics sponsored 'a 1972 bill that created a Software Export Scheme, which relaxed the import of hardware for companies that would use the hardware to create software for export' (S0230†).
- Rajaraman, closer to the events, writes that the DoE 'allowed import of computers exclusively meant for software export. The first policy paper on this was published in 1974' (S0001, p. 28).

Dispute (n) is open until a DoE annual report or Subramanian (S0025) or Heeks (S0070) is read. The chapter can safely say 'from the early 1970s'.

The mechanism matters more than the date. The *Technology Review* account describes it for TCS's own Burroughs machine:

- the computer cost $340,000, and more than twice that once tariffs were added;
- the government approved the import on condition that TCS export twice the machine's value within five years;
- TCS met the obligation (S0038†).

So a computer was licensed against future foreign earnings. Under that rule, a firm that wanted machines had to sell abroad, and what it had to sell was its people's skill. This is the core of the book's question 1 and of the thesis about forex scarcity.

Two cautions:

- The '2x in five years' terms come from one press account based on Kohli. The wording of the actual import licence or policy is not seen.
- Aspray notes that by 1980 'most of the hardware being imported to India was being used for domestic purposes, not for creating software exports' (S0230†). The rule was leaky, and the chapter should not present it as a clean success.

### 3.6 Burroughs

Why Burroughs? Sharma says simply that Kohli 'decided to work with Burroughs – IBM's closest rival' (S0228†). Kohli in 2020 called it 'number two computer company at that time' and said: 'Burroughs, I built up relationship when I was there in United States' (S0036, pp. 4-5). Aspray says TCS became Burroughs' exclusive agent in India (S0230†).

An inference that needs a source before it is drafted: IBM dominated the Indian market (about 65% of installations in 1974; S0003, p. 800) and was in conflict with the government over equity (Ch. 5). An alliance with the second American maker therefore gave TCS a foreign partner that was not IBM.

The accounts of the first Burroughs work differ in emphasis:

- **Healthcare package (S0038†).** Burroughs wanted a healthcare software package to sell with its new computers. TCS had no Burroughs machine: 'But we didn't have a Burroughs machine in India.' So TCS wrote the software on its ICL and built a 'filter' program to convert it for Burroughs hardware. The work made Burroughs a large repeat client.
- **Systems software (S0228†).** Sharma describes the breakthrough as a Burroughs contract for 'writing OS software for a new series of mainframes'. He credits 'young engineers' with a software filter that made ICL software work on Burroughs' platform.
- **Conversion from other makers (S0001, p. 28).** Rajaraman says TCS imported a Burroughs B6700 and converted other makers' software to run on Burroughs machines.
- **Hospital accounting (S0039).** The company timeline speaks of converting a hospital-accounting system for a Detroit client in 1973, which it calls the 'first recorded instance of offshore software delivery'. That is a company claim and must be labelled as one.

These are compatible as a sequence: conversion first, then application packages, then systems software. Ramadorai's memoir and the itihaasa clip (S0037, S0233) should settle it.

The work was done in two places:

- In Bombay, code was written for machines the programmers did not have.
- In the United States, TCS staff installed software at Burroughs customers. Aspray writes that 'Burroughs asked TCS to send programmers to the United States to install systems software for Burroughs customers', and calls this the birth of 'the Indian export software business' (S0230†).

The clients that followed, per Sharma, were the Detroit Police, the City of Detroit, the State University of New York and banks (S0228†). A review of Ramadorai's memoir says the Detroit Police project of 1974 was split between Bombay and Detroit, and that 'offshore' grew from that arrangement (S0037, review†). Dispute (p) is whether the first offshore job was the 1973 hospital or the 1974 police project; possibly both.

The partnership spread beyond services. Rajaraman says a Tata-Burroughs manufacturing company was formed in 1975 and closed after about four years (S0001, p. 28). Sharma names Tata Burroughs Limited as a separate software unit 'which along with TCS remained India's top software exporter for a long time' (S0228†). These may be the same entity described from two angles; to check. Tata's timeline says the Burroughs partnership ended in 1978 (S0039), the year IBM left India (S0230†; Ch. 5).

The company also lists 1970s projects for an Iranian electric utility (1971) and a UK housing society for Burroughs (1974). It reports revenue of over $1 million in 1976, with Kohli receiving an export award from the Engineering Export Promotion Council (S0039). All are company claims. Rajaraman independently mentions work scouted in Iran, Switzerland, South Africa, Canada and the United States (S0001, p. 28).

### 3.7 Onsite and the birth of 'body shopping'

The onsite model was not a deliberate strategy so much as the form demand took. A manufacturer installing systems at its customers' sites needed programmers there, and TCS supplied them (S0230†).

Aspray writes that 'When TCS was successful in this "body shopping" as the service came derisively to be known, other Indian firms began offering programming services in competition'. He adds: 'As of 1980, the Indian software industry was predominantly a body-shopping industry', with 21 firms exporting 'only $4 million' a year (S0230†). These 1980 figures need checking against Heeks (S0070, S0085) and DoE data before use. Their scale fits the 1976 company claim of $1 million for TCS (S0039).

This is where the book's thesis about billing must be tested. The sources so far show programmers sent abroad and machines licensed against exports. None shows how TCS charged Burroughs or its US clients: per person-month, per hour, or a fixed price per job. The 'filter' story, and the hospital and housing-society packages, suggest some fixed-scope deliverables. The onsite installation work suggests time-based billing.

Until a contract, a rate or a firsthand account is found, the chapter must not assert that 'hourly billing' began here. It can say that the industry's first exports were people's time on site together with code written at home, and that the record of how they were priced is missing. (Gap 1 below; Ch. 6 continues.)

### 3.8 The Ahmedabad speech: an ideology of export

In January 1975, as president of the Computer Society of India, Kohli addressed its annual convention at Ahmedabad. In his own 2009 quotation of that speech, he said: 'Many years ago, there was an industrial revolution. We missed it due to reasons over which we had no control.' The new revolution 'requires the capability to think clearly. This we have in abundance.' And: 'we even have the opportunity to be leaders' (S0231, PDF p. 4).

The quotation is Kohli's own, 34 years later. The 1975 text is not seen, though it is probably in his collected speeches (S0234). Sharma's obituary frames 1975 as the 'eureka moment' of the industry (S0228†). Tata's tribute says Kohli 'addressed the Computer Society of India, prophesising the role that it could play in building the nation' (S0235†).

The speech matters because it states the bargain in national terms: India had missed industry, but it had minds. It also sets 'think clearly' as the qualification, not machines or capital. That framing recurs in Chapters 6, 12 and 19, and the chapter should note how close it is to later NASSCOM language (labelled as lobby self-description where used).

### 3.9 Patni: data by air

Narendra Patni's route was different and earlier in one respect: it was offshore from the start. Sharma, in a 2011 article drawn from his book, writes that Patni 'formed Data Conversion Inc (DCI), forerunner to PCS, to convert physical data into paper and magnetic tapes' in 1972 (S0229†).

The test run is a documented scene: 'Narendra and his wife Poonam actually tested the offshore model at their apartment in Cambridge, Massachusetts. They designated the living room as the "United States" and the bedroom as "India"' (S0229†). After that, 'A team of two dozen data entry operators was then hired in Pune (then known as Poona) for the job' (S0229†). A 2007 Business Standard profile (S0044, not read; known from search summary) says Poonam Patni started the Pune office with 20 people.

The work was keying documents, mostly legal papers by one search summary (to confirm), onto tape for American clients. The idea, Patni reportedly said, came from the paper tape of Indian telex and teleprinter offices (search summary of S0229; to confirm).

This was the first Indian offshore 'process' work: the ancestor of the 1990s back offices (Ch. 10), two decades early, and run by an IIT Roorkee and MIT graduate (dates from secondary summaries; confirm). The Pune operators are the chapter's most obvious rank-and-file figures, and none is named in any source.

Patni then moved to software. Sharma says Narendra formed Patni Computer Systems with his brothers Gajendra and Ashok 'in 1976' and wrote software for Data General customers (S0229†). Other summaries say 1978 (dispute (o)).

In August 1977 Patni hired Murthy, an IIT Kanpur M.Tech, to head the software division (S0229†). Murthy says he joined 'as the general manager of software' and visited the US 'many, many times'. There he saw how departmental computing markets were 'exploding, thanks to the superminicomputers' (S0034, p. 4). His team at Patni included Nandan Nilekani and Kris Gopalakrishnan, 'software engineers at that time' with 'about a year to a year and a half of experience' (S0034, p. 4). Murthy resigned in December 1980, and Infosys followed in 1981 with six Patni colleagues (S0229†; S0003, p. 801). Patni's firm was thus the training ground for the founders of Chapter 6's most important company.

### 3.10 Interpretations

Three readings of the decade are available in the sources.

- **Founder's reading (Kohli, Tata).** Vision and talent: Kohli 'could see' the potential, recruited the best, and prophesied in 1975 (S0231; S0235). This is self-description, and the chapter should treat it as such. Kohli's own 2020 line, 'Father is a misnomer' (S0036, p. 7), is a useful corrective in his own voice.
- **Policy reading (Aspray, Rajaraman).** The export industry was a by-product of import controls and foreign-exchange rules. Firms exported because that was the price of getting machines (S0230†; S0001, p. 28). Aspray adds that the rule was widely evaded.
- **Network reading (Bassett).** What mattered was the pairing of an American-style engineering education with men like Kohli and Patni who had American connections (S0003, pp. 801-802).

These readings are not exclusive. The evidence assembled here supports the policy and network readings more than the founder's, but it cannot weigh them precisely.

**Banerjee (S0221, 2026), not read.** As reported by MIT News (S0222†), Banerjee argues that Indian computing was shaped by decolonisation and by the departure of foreign firms. That bears directly on the 1978 end-point. Reading it is a priority before this chapter is drafted.

### 3.11 Education and the profession in this chapter

The chapter marks the point where a computing education became a job market. Before TCS, Kanpur's computing graduates had nowhere obvious to go in India (S0003, p. 800, 'No IT industry in Kanpur itself'). TCS and Patni gave them employers that valued exactly what they had learned. Kohli 'often hired Kanpur's computing master's graduates' (S0003, p. 801). Mahabala, one of the Kanpur faculty who had learned computing 'on the fly', spent summers consulting for TCS (S0003, p. 801).

But the recruitment pool was wider than engineers:

- MIT management graduates and an ISI statistician among the founders (S0228†);
- chartered accountants and science and mathematics postgraduates among the hires (S0235†).

The profession at this stage was defined by the task (systems programming, conversion, data processing), not by a degree. Formal software training came from IBM, ECIL and NCSDCT (Ch. 3) and, increasingly, from the firms themselves. TCS's own training programme of the 1970s is mentioned only in itihaasa clip titles (Lakshmi Narayanan, 'TCS training 1976').

Kohli's view, in 2009, was that technology would be obsolete 'in less than 24 months', hence the need for 'continuing education in new technologies' (S0231, PDF p. 4). Retraining employees became a permanent feature of the firms; Chapter 13 returns to it.

There is no evidence on gender for this decade at TCS or Patni. The Pune data-entry operators may have included women; no source says so. The chapter must not guess.
