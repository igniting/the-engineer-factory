**Chapter question.** How did Indian firms come to export programmers' time, and what does the record show about how that time was priced?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The decade's export model.** In the 1980s India's software exports were mostly people. Firms sent programmers to work at American clients' sites, on the clients' computers, for six to eighteen months. The trade called it 'body shopping'; the World Bank called it 'manpower contracts' (S0241, p. 31; S0085, p. 7).

In 1988 about 65% of export contracts were carried out wholly onsite, and about three-quarters of all export development happened overseas (S0085, p. 6). Exports grew from $4 million in 1980 to $131 million in 1990/91 (S0085, p. 2). By one estimate, net foreign-exchange earnings were only about 40% of the gross (S0085, p. 2). Two Tata firms supplied nearly 70% of body-shopping exports (S0240).

**How the time was priced.** This is the book's central test, and the evidence is now direct:
- A 1994 World Bank study: the overseas buyer 'typically buys "hours"', and programmers were 'contracted out to work for a client at an agreed hourly or monthly rate' (S0241, p. 31).
- Heeks: over 1988-98 at least 65% of contracts were programming-only work 'billed on a "time and materials" basis' (S0085, p. 7).
- A 1990 case documented in 1995: Sun paid HCL '$50 an hour' for a Bombay programmer's work, of which HCL kept about half. The programmer received about $800 a month in India plus $40 a day in expenses (S0242).

**Why this model.** Body shopping needed no imported computers, little capital and no marketing beyond a contact abroad. It suited a country where a $300,000 computer took three years and fifty trips to Delhi to license (S0085, pp. 14-15; S0034, p. 5). Infosys, founded in 1981, began this way: four of its seven founders spent most of the decade at US client sites (S0089, pp. 54-55).

**The costs.**
- Brain drain: up to 50% of IIT graduates emigrated (S0241, p. 74).
- Gender: about 10% of developers were women, and onsite work was 'the young, single man's game' (S0085, p. 7).
- Labour: tied contracts, including a $30,000 penalty for leaving TCS (S0242).

**An early warning.** The 1994 World Bank study already warned that demand for these skills 'is likely to be eroded by tools that automate many of these tasks' (S0241, p. 33).

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 What 'body shopping' meant

The term was a pejorative, used by the trade itself. Rajaraman, writing as a participant-historian, defines it as 'sending software engineers to client sites abroad to develop software' (S0001, p. 29). Aspray adds that the service 'came derisively to be known' by that name (S0230†). Heeks, writing from fieldwork in the late 1980s and 1990s, describes it as the Indian name for 'the combination of onsite and programming work' (S0085, p. 7).

The World Bank's 1994 study, the most detailed contemporary description, called it 'body-shopping' or 'manpower contracts', in which the overseas buyer 'typically buys "hours" and not a total project or product with the associated management and high-value added technical services' (S0241, p. 31).

The model predates the decade. TCS and Patni had sent staff to American sites in the 1970s (Ch. 4). Sharma: 'TCS and PCS had been doing this in the 1970s and this became a dominant way of doing software business in the 1980s' (S0240). By 1980, Aspray says, 21 Indian firms exported only about $4 million a year, and the industry 'was predominantly a body-shopping industry' (S0230†). Heeks's series gives exactly $4.0 million for 1980 (S0085, p. 2).

### 3.2 Why export people rather than code?

The sources agree on the mechanism. Indian firms 'did not have the necessary hardware and software tools as well as basic infrastructure to take up any meaningful work', so 'the best way to leverage it was go to the customer site and work there using their computers' (S0240).

Heeks puts the point as a general explanation. Because the work was done onsite, it did 'not require the purchase of hardware or software by the Indian company or the provision of any significant technology infrastructure within the country'. Firms 'have chosen a path of almost "inputless" exports which requires only a contact overseas, a little finance, and the names of some local programmers who can be hired if a contract is forthcoming'. And 'Such a path can also be followed relatively independent of government policy measures, particularly those on imports, because no imports are needed' (S0085, p. 14).

This is the clearest statement in the sources of the book's thesis link. **Import controls, driven by foreign-exchange scarcity, made computers expensive and slow to obtain at home, and made labour the easiest thing to export.**

The firms' own experience bears this out:
- Infosys needed 'about three years and fifty visits to Delhi to get a license to import a computer worth 300,000 US dollars' (S0034, p. 5).
- A founder needed Reserve Bank permission, and about 15 days' wait, to travel abroad 'even for a day' (S0034, p. 8).
- Customs duty on computers imported for export had been 'over 100%' before 1984 (S0001, p. 32).

In the CHM interview the interviewer put it to Murthy that Infosys could 'get going quickly because you could send your colleagues to go work in the U.S. at customer sites', and Murthy agreed (S0034, p. 5).

Heeks's interviews add the managers' 'pull'. Onsite programming 'produces quick revenue for low investment, which suits the lack of risk-taking favoured by many Indian business managers' (S0085, p. 8). Sharma calls it 'a high margin business' (S0240).

There was also a pull from the workers themselves: 'Many Indian staff also want to work overseas and, if denied the opportunity by one company, will simply join another which will send them abroad' (S0085, p. 8).

### 3.3 How the time was priced (the thesis test)

Until this chapter, the book had no evidence on pricing (STATE open problem 18). Three independent kinds of source now converge.

**1. The institutional description (World Bank 1994).** 'Typically, programmers are contracted out to work for a client at an agreed hourly or monthly rate for six to eighteen months. They perform data conversion or coding jobs, such as conversion from batch-processing to on-line systems or conversion from source code to UNIX-based systems' (S0241, p. 31). These contracts were 'short in term, low in risk, low in value added, and low in investment, but they represent 70-80 percent of the industry's export revenues' (S0241, p. 31). Sharma repeats the 'buy "hours"' formulation from this source (S0240, n. 62).

**2. The researcher's survey (Heeks).** 'During the period 1988-1998, at least 65% of export contracts were solely for programming work billed on a "time and materials" basis, with programming figuring strongly in the remaining one-third of contracts' (S0085, p. 7, citing his 1996 book and Dataquest 1998).

'Time and materials' means the client pays for staff time at agreed rates, plus expenses. It is not necessarily hourly; rates could be per hour, per day or per person-month. The book should use 'time-based billing' as the general term and 'hourly' only where a source says hourly.

**3. The individual case (1990, documented 1995).** Pratap Chatterjee's investigation for *Multinational Monitor* describes a Bombay programmer whom the magazine calls 'Rajesh Ahuja (not his real name)'. HCL hired him and sent him 'to its offices in Palo Alto, California, on a B1/B2 visa in 1990' (S0242). He 'worked anywhere HCL sent him, at whatever company he was subcontracted to', mostly at Sun Microsystems. 'For Ahuja's services, Sun paid HCL $50 an hour, about half of which remained with HCL' (S0242). He received 'my Indian salary of about $800 a month paid into my bank account in India' and '$40 a day for expenses' (S0242).

Simple arithmetic, to be labelled as such in the text and not attributed to the source:
- at 160 hours a month, $50 an hour is about $8,000 billed;
- the programmer's pay plus a 30-day per diem comes to about $2,000 (about $800 plus about $1,200);
- housing, car and travel came on top.

The pseudonym is the magazine's. The book must say so, and must not add details beyond the article.

**Persistence.** The model persisted well beyond the decade. In 1998, 63.7% of Infosys's revenue came from time-and-materials contracts (S0089). Chapters 11 and 12 take that up.

**Verdict for the thesis.** The claim that India's export industry priced engineers by time, not by product, is **supported** for 1988-1998 by survey evidence, and for 1990 by a documented hourly rate. For 1978-1987 it rests on the World Bank's general description ('typically'). That description covers the 1980s but was written in 1994. The thesis wording 'by the hour' should be softened to 'by the hour or the month' unless more hourly evidence appears.

### 3.4 Who went, and on what papers

The programmers were mostly young men. Heeks found 'Only some 10% of Indian software developers are female'. Women faced difficulties because the work 'often entails working unsocial hours and, more significantly, working overseas'. Onsite programming was seen, 'as one manager put it, as "the young, single man's game"'. Yet women interviewees 'felt that software development offered a more relaxed and less discriminatory atmosphere than many other Indian employment options' (S0085, p. 7). He cites a contemporary SNDT Women's University study (Jayanthi and Madhavan 1985), which should be found.

The visas are thinly documented for the 1980s. Heeks says that only after the US rules tightened in the 1990s were Indian workers 'channelled into taking temporary work visas (rather than student or training or even tourist visas as in the past)' (S0085, p. 9). The 1990 case used a 'B1/B2 visa', which the magazine describes as 'intended for foreign consultants to work in the United States for relatively short stints' (S0242). The outline's mention of L-1 visas has no source yet. The H-1B, created in 1990, belongs to Chapters 11 and 13.

The living arrangements in the 1990 case:
- 'I shared a two-bedroom apartment and a car with three other programmers';
- HCL rented an apartment complex in which 'one person would be in charge of all the paperwork, transportation and living arrangement for 160 of us';
- when he returned home, HCL 'had not bothered to renew his visa' (S0242).

The programmer's own reason for accepting the terms: 'All my life I have dreamed about coming to the United States ... I get to do this for two years and my flight is even paid for' (S0242).

### 3.5 Bonds and disputes

The firms protected their investment in training with bonds. Chatterjee reports that Kala Sivasubramanian, 'an Indian programmer who was brought to San Francisco by TCS', tried to move to another company. 'the company sued to enforce her contract, which called for her to pay more than $30,000 to the company if she quit'. TCS's lawyer said the company 'has sued 16 former employees successfully' (S0242). The case date is not given; it is probably early 1990s, at the edge of this chapter. Use it with that caveat, or move it to Chapter 11.

The World Bank explains the firms' fear: 'once the personnel are abroad, the temptations to remain abroad are strong', and 'the costs of endlessly training new personnel must be added to low profit margins' (S0241, p. 31).

In the US, the Department of Labor later found Indian firms paying '$15,000 a year plus housing and travel', against '$70,000 plus benefits' for American programmers (S0242). This is a 1990s finding. It belongs mostly to Chapter 13, but it frames what the 1980s model became.

### 3.6 The firms

**Tata.** Two Tata firms, TCS and Tata Burroughs (renamed Tata Unisys in 1986), 'accounted for nearly 70 per cent of exports through body shopping' (S0240). In the World Bank's table of top exporters (c. 1991-92), TCS earned Rs 1,754.8 million and Tata Unisys Rs 560.0 million, far ahead of the rest (S0241, p. 32).

**The minicomputer firms' route.** Sharma shows how the minicomputer distributors of Chapter 5 slid into export work. Patni, selling Data General machines: 'we started doing software work for them including operating systems. That's how our software stream started' (S0240, interview 2006). ORG Systems, Sperry's distributor: 'We developed P1024 protocol, which Sperry used in its mainframes worldwide', recalls K.R. Trilokekar, who joined ORG in 1978 (S0240, n. 61).

Sharma's synthesis: the American minicomputer makers 'began using local talent to write software for computers sold locally', found Indian engineers 'cheaper' and good, and so 'began using them for customers back home' (S0240).

**Citibank.** Citibank set up a software unit at SEEPZ in 1984-85 (Citicorp Overseas Software Ltd, inaugurated 28 October 1985). By 1989-90 it was the third-largest exporter (S0240). This was the first big foreign captive, a theme for Chapter 15.

**Small firms.** By the early 1990s, 'Over three-fourths of the companies have fewer than 25 professionals'. Some, 'renting out programmers abroad', ran on 'a one-room office, telephone and fax' (S0241, p. 31).

### 3.7 Infosys: founders as body shoppers

Infosys's founding story is usually told as a garage start with Rs 10,000 (M15; Ch. 5). For this chapter the more telling evidence is in its 1999 SEC filing:
- Nandan Nilekani 'From 1981 to 1987 ... was in the United States managing the marketing and development efforts of Infosys';
- K. Dinesh 'From 1981 to 1990 ... managed projects for Infosys in the United States';
- S.D. Shibulal 'From 1981 to 1991 ... worked for Infosys in the United States on projects in the retail and manufacturing industries';
- Kris Gopalakrishnan ran 'all projects at the U.S.-based KSA/Infosys', a joint venture with Kurt Salmon Associates, from 1987 to 1994 (S0089, pp. 54-55).

Four of the seven founders spent most of the decade at or near American client sites.

Murthy's account of the first client fits the pattern. Data Basics sold an apparel-industry package to firms such as Jockey and Reebok, running on Data General NOVA and DEC PDP-11 minicomputers. Infosys offered to port it to a 32-bit Data General MV/8000 and later an IBM 4381: 'It was a godsend for us' (S0034, p. 6). Murthy's founding conviction, stated in 2020, was that 'there's going to be a great future for a company that develops bespoke software for a large market like the U.S.' (S0034, p. 4).

The F-1 claims, as a company statement, that 'The Indian offshore development model was initiated in the mid-1980's' (S0089). Label it as the company's self-description.

### 3.8 The counter-currents: offshore and captives

The policy changes of 1984 and 1986 (Ch. 8) began to make offshore work feasible:
- the 1984 policy formally recognised profits from 'Sending engineers abroad to develop and maintain software for clients at their sites' as 'software export' (S0001, p. 32);
- the 1986 policy allowed duty-free import of computers against an obligation to export 250% of their cost within four years (S0001, p. 32).

Firms began 'remote software development', but 'there were still impediments such as high telecom costs' (S0240).

Texas Instruments' Bangalore centre with its own satellite link was 'the first proper offshore software facility' in Heeks's words (S0085, p. 13). It is dated 1986 by Heeks and 1985 by Rajaraman (dispute (w); Ch. 10).

Heeks's reasons why onsite work persisted are worth summarising for the text (S0085, pp. 7-8):
- client distrust;
- client priorities: cost first, then credentials, productivity and quality;
- projects too small to send offshore (under $100,000);
- the need for face-to-face interaction;
- a workforce that was 85% programmers;
- managers' preference for quick, low-investment revenue.

Onsite was still more than half of export earnings in 1997/98 (S0085, p. 6).

### 3.9 What it earned, and what it cost

**Growth (S0085, p. 2).**

| Year | Exports (US$m) |
|---|---|
| 1980 | 4.0 |
| 1984 | 25.3 |
| 1987 | 54.1 |
| 1989/90 | 105.4 |
| 1990/91 | 131.2 |

This series resolves dispute (t): Rajaraman's $131 million is 1990/91. The World Bank gives growth of 35% a year in exports 1987-91 (S0241, p. 28).

**Net earnings.** Heeks: 'these "headline" figures represent gross foreign exchange earnings. Net earnings (less outflow of foreign exchange from India to pay for travel and living allowances of Indian software workers who undertake their contracts overseas, marketing ...) are now estimated to be around 40% of the gross figures' (S0085, p. 2).

This qualifies the thesis. An industry justified as a foreign-exchange earner kept less than half of what it earned, because sending people abroad itself cost foreign exchange.

**Value captured.** 'Coding is the lowest paid part of the software development process, earning only 10-15% of total project revenue' (S0085, p. 15, citing Bhatnagar and Jain 1991). And 'earnings are directly proportional to the number of people working in the industry' (S0085, p. 14). Revenue grew only by adding people, which is the structural feature the book argues AI now threatens.

**Brain drain.** The World Bank: 'Migration of graduates from premier institutions like the IITs is estimated to be as high as 50 percent' (S0241, p. 74). 'Nearly 60% of IIT Bombay and Madras graduates in computer science leave the country' (S0241, p. 98). It blamed 'The orientation of the sector toward renting out programmers' (S0241, p. 98).

### 3.10 The 1994 warning

Two contemporary sources foresaw the threat this book ends with:
- The World Bank in 1994: 'Demand for these skills is likely to be eroded by tools that automate many of these tasks' (S0241, p. 33). And: 'Globally, a long-term decline in demand for routine or low-level programmers has been forecasted' (S0241, p. 33).
- Heeks in 1998 listed 'automation from new technology' among the model's vulnerabilities (S0085, p. 14). He also noted that offshore-ready, tightly specified projects 'are then more amenable to automated software tools' (S0085, p. 7).

These warnings did not come true for three decades. That is itself a point for the epilogue: earlier forecasts of automation (CASE tools, fourth-generation languages) did not erode the model. The book must be careful not to treat the 2025-26 shift as certain for the same reason.

### 3.11 Interpretations

- **Pejorative reading.** Body shopping was low-value labour export, a 'body shopping prison' (Heeks's section title, S0085, p. 6) that built few higher skills and drained talent (S0241; S0073 as quoted in S0085).
- **Stepping-stone reading.** Heeks also allows that 'Some have therefore been able to use onsite "body shopping" services as a stepping stone to better things' (S0085, p. 14). The stage model runs from labour to services to products (S0085, p. 15). Infosys's later offshore model fits this reading.
- **Policy reading.** The model was a rational response to import controls and foreign-exchange rules. It persisted after they eased, because clients and managers liked it (S0085, pp. 8, 14).

Xiang Biao's ethnography (S0057) treats body shopping as a labour system in its own right, built on the bench and flexible labour. It is not read, and its fieldwork is late 1990s; it is relevant mainly to Chapter 11.

### 3.12 Education and the profession in this chapter

Body shopping changed what a software education was for. The work sold abroad was mostly coding, testing and conversion. The client set the requirements and design (S0085, p. 7; S0241, p. 31). Heeks found that '85% of workers in Indian software exports are programmers', although 'The average software development project requires less than half the labour input to come from programmers' (S0085, p. 8). The industry hired, and the colleges supplied, a workforce shaped for the lower stages of development.

**The new routes in.** Two of them date from the start of this period:
- **The MCA.** Rajaraman's 1980 Panel on Computer Manpower Development proposed it for B.Sc and B.Com graduates, explicitly to produce people who could 'develop business/management information systems for use in organizations both in India and abroad' (S0001, p. 34). Rajaraman later judged that 'The availability of well trained MCAs was one of the contributing factors for the rapid growth of software services industry in India' (S0001, p. 35).
- **Private training.** NIIT, founded in 1981, began franchising to smaller towns in 1987 (S0001, p. 42). Chatterjee reported in 1995 that training institutes such as NIIT were 'matriculating more than 100,000 computer programmers each year' (S0242). That is a journalist's figure, probably including short courses; check it in Ch. 7.

**Exports competed with the rest of the economy for graduates.** The World Bank noted that 'software export needs could "cannibalize" other sectors by taking almost one-half of all new computing course graduates from government-initiated courses' (S0241, p. 98). It suggested 'recycling graduates in other disciplines into software engineering' (S0241, p. 73).

**Training firms and leavers.** Firms also trained their own recruits briefly before sending them abroad. Sharma: firms 'had to hire the right people, train them for a brief while, send them to American clients to execute projects and then they would come back' (S0240). The cost of that training is the reason for the bonds and exit penalties in section 3.5 (S0241, p. 31; S0242).

**Indian pay against US pay.** By the early 1990s the gap was the industry's selling point:
- a Department of Labor finding of '$15,000 a year plus housing and travel' for Indian programmers in the US, against '$70,000 plus benefits' for Americans (S0242);
- in India, 'their best and brightest counterparts in India earn $10,000' a year against $100,000 in California (S0242, 1995);
- Infosys's 1999 filing put India's average software wage at 'approximately 15% of the average U.S. rate' (S0089, citing Software Productivity Research).

No source yet gives a 1980s Indian programmer's salary. That gap matters for the pay series in Phase 3.

**The profession's self-image.** It took shape around going abroad. Heeks's interviews show that working overseas was a reason to join a firm and a reason to leave one (S0085, p. 8). The pseudonymous HCL programmer of 1990 accepted unpaid overtime because the job let him see America (S0242). The chapter should present this ambition in the workers' own terms rather than only as exploitation. Both readings are in the sources.
