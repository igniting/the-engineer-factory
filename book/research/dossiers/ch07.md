**Chapter question.** How did India produce the people the software trade needed, when public universities could not expand fast enough, and who paid?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The shortage.** In 1980 India had 149 engineering and technology colleges (S0248). A government manpower panel chaired by V. Rajaraman foresaw a shortage of computer professionals. It proposed a new degree, the Master of Computer Applications, to turn science and commerce graduates into software designers 'for use in organizations both in India and abroad' (S0001, pp. 34-35). The state could not pay for expansion on the scale needed.

**Three answers.** Over the next fifteen years three private answers filled the gap.

1. **Private engineering colleges.** In the Sixth Plan (1980-85), Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh let private trusts open 'self-financing' engineering colleges (S0247, quoting a 2010 NUEPA paper). The number of engineering colleges rose from 149 to 242 in five years (S0248).
2. **Training companies.** Firms sold short courses in programming outside the degree system. NIIT, founded in 1981, began with a four-week BASIC course and a twelve-week COBOL course (S0244). It franchised centres into smaller towns from 1987 (S0001, p. 42); Aptech followed in 1986 (S0083†).
3. **Coaching for elite entrance exams.** Coaching for the IIT entrance exam grew up around a Kota engineer who began teaching eight students at his dining table in the early 1980s (S0246†).

**The capitation dispute.** The private colleges charged heavily for admission, and states passed laws against 'capitation fees':
- Karnataka in 1983-84 (S0064);
- Maharashtra in 1987-88, citing 'large scale commercialisation of education' (S0075);
- Tamil Nadu in 1992 (S0063).

In 1992 the Supreme Court, in *Mohini Jain*, called capitation 'nothing but a price for selling education' (S0064, p. 670). In 1993 a five-judge bench in *Unni Krishnan* accepted that private colleges could charge more than government ones. It created a scheme of 'free' and 'payment' seats (S0063).

**Gaps.** Intake numbers, fees actually paid and students' own accounts are thin. The voice of the trained remains the chapter's main gap.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The shortage the state saw

By 1980 the government knew the computer industry would need more people than the universities produced. The Electronics Commission set up a Panel on Computer Manpower Development under V. Rajaraman, who was also a member of the Commission from 1979 to 1982 (S0001, p. 34).

Its proposals:
- expand undergraduate computer science 'at IITs and other engineering colleges';
- create a new programme, the Master of Computer Applications (S0001, p. 34).

The reasoning, in Rajaraman's words, was that India's B.Sc and B.Com degrees 'do not prepare a student for a profession', while 'India needed a large number of persons to develop business/management information systems for use in organizations both in India and abroad' (S0001, p. 34).

The MCA curriculum combined computer science 'with emphasis in software engineering and systems analysis', mathematics, and management science, and included 'a semester of actual system design in an organization' (S0001, pp. 34-35). The government 'decided to fully fund ten institutions' at first. Rajaraman later judged that 'The availability of well trained MCAs was one of the contributing factors for the rapid growth of software services industry in India' (S0001, p. 35).

That is the author of the programme assessing it. The panel's report is in Banerjee's 1996 volume (S0016, pp. 411-438), not yet seen. MCA intake numbers by year are a gap.

The premier institutions could not meet the need. In 1978 more than 100,000 candidates sat the IIT entrance examination for 1,500 seats (S0001, p. 34). NIIT's co-founder recalled that in 1981 'there were a handful of people coming out of the IITs, but they were not the ones writing programs' (S0244). Even the elite supply drained abroad: the World Bank estimated IIT graduate migration 'as high as 50 percent' (S0241, p. 74; Ch. 6).

### 3.2 Private colleges and the 'self-financing' turn

The decisive change was made by four state governments. As a 2010 NUEPA paper puts it (quoted in S0247): 'during the Sixth Five Year Plan (1980-85), when the central and state governments were finding it difficult to expand technical education in the country, a few state governments, especially the governments of Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh took a bold decision to permit private registered societies and trusts to establish and run technical institutions on a self-financing basis'. The NUEPA paper itself has not been identified; the quotation must be verified before use.

The official count of engineering and technology colleges shows the effect (S0248):

| Year | Colleges | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 1970-71 | 107 | |
| 1980-81 | 149 | |
| 1985-86 | 242 | +62% on 1980-81 |
| 1990-91 | 289 | +19% |
| 1994-95 | 337 | +17% |

The first half of the 1980s was the surge. The early 1990s were slower, and the big expansion came after 1996 (486 colleges in 1997-98; 1,077 in 2001-02; Ch. 12). The table counts institutions, not seats. For seats, the 1992 Programme of Action gives 'annual admission capacities of 40,000' at degree level in 'over 200' institutions (S0243, p. 77). Those figures match the mid-1980s count and may be out of date. An AICTE intake series is needed for Phase 3.

The colleges were 'self-financing', meaning students paid. How much they paid is the heart of the legal record. The court documents describe fees and their justifications in detail:
- Maharashtra prescribed 'an uniform fee of Rs. 6,500/- per annum in the case of private un-aided engineering colleges, which was raised to Rs. 8,500/ in 1991'; and in 1992 Rs 17,000 'in the case of outside students' (S0063, p. 639).
- In Andhra Pradesh the previous year's permitted fee was 'Rs. 10,000 per annum' (S0063, p. 691).

These are the official fees. The capitation payments the laws targeted were by nature unrecorded.

### 3.3 The capitation laws

The states that licensed private colleges also legislated against their excesses:
- **Karnataka.** An ordinance in force from 11 July 1983, replaced by the Karnataka Educational Institutions (Prohibition of Capitation Fee) Act, 1984 (S0064, p. 663).
- **Maharashtra.** Act of 1987, assent 13 April 1988. Its preamble records that capitation 'is on the increase in the State', and that 'this undesirable practice has been contributing to large scale commercialisation of education which is not conducive to the maintenance of educational standards'. It invokes the National Policy on Education of 1986, which 'envisages that the commercialisation of technical and professional education should be curbed' (S0075). It defined 'capitation fee' as 'any amount, by whatever name called ... in excess of the prescribed or, as the case may be, approved, rates of fees' (S0075, s. 2(a)).
- **Tamil Nadu.** Act of 1992 (S0063).

The national framework: the NPE 1986, as quoted by the Supreme Court, promised that 'commercialisation of technical and professional education will be curbed' and that 'An alternative system will be devised to involve private and voluntary effort in this section of education' (S0063). The AICTE became a statutory body under the AICTE Act of 1987 (S0243, p. 77). The Union government told the Supreme Court that 'so far as engineering colleges are concerned, permission is being granted by the A.I.C.T.E. subject to the condition that they do not collect any capitation fee' (S0063, p. 673).

By 1992 the Programme of Action was urging AICTE to set 'a realistic fee structure based on actual cost of education' and to encourage 'private initiative, support and participation in technical education system'. It also wanted a 'penal clause' in the AICTE Act 'for effectively curbing malpractices' (S0243, p. 78). The state wanted private money and feared private greed at the same time.

### 3.3a What the 1987 regulator was told to do

The AICTE Act gives the regulator's mandate in its own words. It was enacted 'with a view to the proper planning and co-ordinated development of the technical education system throughout the country, the promotion of qualitative improvements of such education in relation to planned quantitative growth and the regulation and proper maintenance of norms and standards' (S0076, long title).

'Technical education' is defined in s. 2(g) as 'programmes of education, research and training in engineering, technology, architecture, town planning, management, pharmacy and applied art and crafts', plus any further areas the Central Government notifies. A 'technical institution' is 'an institution, not being a university' that offers such programmes (S0076, s. 2(h)).

Among the Council's functions in s. 10 (S0076):
- to 'fix norms and guidelines for charging tuition and other fees' (s. 10(j));
- to 'grant approval for starting new technical institutions and for introduction of new courses or programmes' (s. 10(k));
- to 'take all necessary steps to prevent commercialisation of technical education' (s. 10(n));
- to 'provide guidelines for admission of students' (s. 10(o));
- to 'inspect or cause to inspect any technical institution' (s. 10(p)).

The Act also charged it to 'undertake survey ... collect data ... and make forecast of the needed growth and development in technical education' (s. 10(a)). This is the manpower-planning ambition of the 1980 panel, now in statute.

Two observations follow. They are inferences from the text and should be presented as such.

First, the Act's list of technical fields does not name computer applications or software, and its definition of an institution excludes universities. So the large market for short computer courses run by companies such as NIIT and Aptech sat outside the regulator's definitions unless the government notified otherwise. No source yet confirms how such courses were treated in practice; the point needs checking against an AICTE notification or a secondary account.

Second, the regulator was created in the same years as the private surge it was meant to discipline: colleges rose from 149 in 1980-81 to 242 in 1985-86, before the Act (S0248). The courts later found that the Act's fee and commercialisation powers had not stopped the 1992 Andhra admissions (S0063, pp. 636-37).

The Programme of Action of 1992 admitted an 'urgent need to strengthen the AICTE in terms of its professional and administrative functions'. It asked for a 'penal clause in the AICTE Act for effectively curbing malpractices' (S0243, pp. 77-78). That is the government's own acknowledgement that the 1987 regulator lacked teeth.

### 3.4 Mohini Jain (1992)

The case that brought the issue to the Supreme Court was about medicine, not engineering, but its reasoning covered both.

Mohini Jain, 'a resident of Meerut', was offered a place on the MBBS course at Sri Siddhartha Medical College, Tumkur, beginning in early 1991. She was told to pay Rs 60,000 for the first year and give a bank guarantee for the rest. 'The petitioner's father informed the management that it was beyond his means to pay the exorbitant annual fee of Rs.60,000 and as a consequence she was denied admission' (S0064, pp. 663-64). She also alleged a demand for 'capitation fee of rupees four and a half lakhs', which the college denied (S0064, p. 664).

Karnataka's own notification of 1989 had set three prices for the same course (S0064, p. 663):

| Category | Fee per year |
|---|---|
| 'Government seats' | Rs 2,000 |
| Other Karnataka students | up to Rs 25,000 |
| 'Indian students from outside Karnataka' | up to Rs 60,000 |

The colleges' association argued that it received no state aid, spent about Rs 5 lakh per student over five years, and had to recover the cost from management-quota students (S0064, pp. 664-65).

The two-judge bench held that 'Capitation fee is nothing but a price for selling education. The concept of "teaching shops" is contrary to the constitutional scheme and is wholly abhorrent to the Indian culture and heritage' (S0064, p. 670). It found 'a clear class bias': 'A poor student with better merit cannot get admission because he has no money whereas the rich can purchase the admission' (S0064, p. 673). It declared that the right to education 'flows directly from right to life' (S0064, p. 669).

### 3.5 Unni Krishnan (1993)

The private colleges responded at once. In *Unni Krishnan*, petitioners 'running medical/engineering colleges in the States of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu' told the Court that if *Mohini Jain* stood 'they will have to close down; no other option is left to them' (S0063).

The judgment records how Andhra Pradesh's private engineering colleges behaved in 1992:
- When the state inserted Section 3-A into its 1983 Act on 15 April 1992, 'these private engineering colleges took the stand that they are entitled to admit students to the extent of 50 per cent of the seats according to their choice, irrespective of merit'. The Court's comment: 'It is obvious that such a stand meant collection of capitation fee as much as they could' (S0063, pp. 636-37).
- 'There was an uproar among the student and teaching community.'
- The convenor allotted only half the seats, 'thereby sending an explicit signal that the colleges were free to fill up the rest on their own' (S0063, pp. 636-37).

The five-judge bench partly overruled *Mohini Jain*. Citizens could not demand 'that the State provide adequate number of medical colleges, engineering colleges and other educational institutions to satisfy all their educational needs' (S0063, p. 655). The right to free education ran to age fourteen.

Private unaided professional colleges could charge more than government institutions, within limits; 'commercialisation of education' remained impermissible (headnote). From 1993-94 the Court imposed a scheme:
- all admissions through a common state process;
- half the seats as 'free seats' filled on merit at the government fee;
- half as 'payment seats', also filled in order of merit, among those willing to pay a fixed higher fee (S0063, pp. 685-88).

The colleges' argument for 'self-financing' or 'cost-based' education was recorded. So was the opposing view that 'a capitation fee did not cease to be a capitation fee just because it was called as cost-based education' (S0063, headnote).

The settlement lasted until *TMA Pai* (2002), which is Chapter 12's starting point.

**What this means for the book.** The legal record shows that by 1992 an engineering seat in the private colleges of the south and west had a market price. Families paid it because the degree led to jobs, and from the late 1980s increasingly to software jobs. No source yet links the capitation economy directly to software demand in this period. That link should be tested, for example with the share of computer science seats in the new colleges, before the draft asserts it.

### 3.6 NIIT and the training companies

Outside the degree system, private training companies sold programming skills directly. NIIT's co-founder Rajendra Pawar described the situation in 1981 as follows: IT education 'was non-existent. But the manufacturing industry was already anticipating a problem of talent shortage' (S0244). NIIT was 'born out of that threat'.

Its first two courses, announced in 1981:
- 'an entry level BASIC. It was a 4-week programme, 5 days a week and 2 hours a day';
- a long-term programme, 'Entry level COBOL, for 12 weeks' (S0244).

It also taught FORTRAN to engineers and scientists. It ran Edward Yourdon's structured-methodology courses under an arrangement in which 'TCS was a very big participant' (S0244). And it ran a course for executives called 'How to buy a computer'. Pawar's summary: 'quite clearly the largest need was for people who could write programs' (S0244). That matches the export trade's demand for coders (Ch. 6).

The growth model was the franchise. Rajaraman dates NIIT's franchising 'to spread their courses to smaller towns' to 1987 (S0001, p. 42). Pawar: 'We found like-minded entrepreneurs in cities and motivated them to go back to their towns and set up an NIIT centre'. He recalls that the *Far Eastern Economic Review* called it 'the McDonald's of education' (S0244; the FEER piece is not seen). In 1988, according to Dataquest, *Asiaweek* called NIIT 'the biggest computer trainer this side of the Suez' (S0245†).

The franchisees were local professionals. Pratap Agarwal, who had 'worked as a software engineer in the US', took the Chandigarh franchise in 1988 and recalled growing 'from a single center in Chandigarh in 1988, to over 60 centers now'. A former TCS and Wipro HR manager took Faridabad in 1992 (S0245†).

Aptech, founded by Atul Nishar in 1986, became the second large chain. A 2014 profile recalled that 'the 1980s and 1990s were all about queues of students eager to sign up for multi-year courses' (S0083†).

These are founders and press retrospectives. No enrolment, fee or placement figures from the 1980s are in hand. A contemporary 1995 claim that institutes such as NIIT were 'matriculating more than 100,000 computer programmers each year' (S0242) is unsourced and misnames the company; it should not be used as a figure.

### 3.7 Coaching: the race for the elite seats

Alongside the expansion of private seats, competition for the few elite seats created a separate industry. In Kota, V.K. Bansal, a BHU-trained mechanical engineer who had come to work for JK Synthetics in 1971, developed muscular dystrophy. 'In the early 1980s, he started teaching eight students sitting around his dining table. In 1983, when his company shut down, Bansal started teaching full-time' (S0246†). 'In 1991, Bansal Classes was born' (S0246†).

Kota's later scale is outside this chapter: more than 150 institutes and about 200,000 students today. In 2023 there were 17,385 IIT seats for more than 1.1 million candidates (S0246†). The 1978 comparison was 100,000 candidates for 1,500 seats (S0001, p. 34).

The source is a 2023 book excerpt about Byju's. Bansal's early years need a second source, for example contemporary press or an obituary, before drafting.

### 3.8 Who paid, and who was trained

The chapter's distributional question is who could buy their way into the new profession. The legal record answers part of it.
- The Karnataka scale charged an outsider up to thirty times the government-seat fee for the same course (S0064, p. 663).
- The Court in *Mohini Jain* saw 'a clear class bias' (S0064, p. 673).
- The *Unni Krishnan* scheme tried to keep merit as the ranking principle while letting the payment-seat half subsidise the free half (S0063).

Training companies sold shorter, cheaper courses without entrance exams, open to B.Sc and B.Com graduates and others without engineering seats. Prices are unknown.

For gender, the only figure is Heeks's estimate that about 10% of Indian software developers were women in the 1990s (S0085, p. 7; Ch. 6). A contemporary SNDT Women's University study (Jayanthi and Madhavan 1985) is cited there and should be found. Caste and region are not addressed in any source in hand.

### 3.9 Interpretations

- **Market reading.** Private colleges and training firms met a demand the state could not. NUEPA's 'bold decision' (S0247) and the PoA's call for private participation (S0243) are close to this view.
- **Commercialisation reading.** The courts and state legislatures saw 'teaching shops' and 'large scale commercialisation' (S0064; S0075).
- **Manpower-planning reading.** The MCA and expanded CS places were deliberate state design for an export industry (S0001, pp. 34-35).

The three operated together, and the book should show all three. It should also note that the export industry's demand for coders (Ch. 6) shaped what all three supplied.

### 3.10 Education and the profession

This is the chapter in which software became a career that could be bought. Before 1980, entry ran through a few elite institutions, IBM and the state (Chs. 2-3). By 1995 it ran through private engineering colleges, the MCA, and franchised training centres in district towns. All of these were oriented to the language skills (COBOL, BASIC, later C) that the trade bought by the hour or the month (S0244; Ch. 6).

The new colleges also needed teachers. In 1985 the Department of Electronics set up a committee under S. Sampath, which 'suggested training teachers in Computer Science for the colleges which were being newly established' (S0001, p. 36). The faculty shortage is a theme Chapter 12 returns to.

The 1994 World Bank study suggested 'recycling graduates in other disciplines into software engineering' (S0241, p. 73), which is what the training companies were already doing. It also warned that exports might 'cannibalize' other sectors by taking 'almost one-half of all new computing course graduates from government-initiated courses' (S0241, p. 98).
