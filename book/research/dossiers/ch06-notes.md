## S0241 Hanna, *Exploiting Information Technology for Development: A Case Study of India* (World Bank Discussion Paper 246, 1994). Full text read (OCR).

Printed page numbers follow the '-nn-' markers. These are judged to sit at the foot of each page. Sharma (S0240, n. 62) cites the body-shopping passage as p. 32, which confirms the reading.

### Body shopping, p. 32

The passage is the closest thing yet to a documentary statement of the pricing model.

- Export growth 'has come mainly from the provision of contract programmers to carry out low-level work ("body-shopping" or "manpower contracts" in which the overseas buyer typically buys "hours" and not a total project or product with the associated management and high-value added technical services)'.
- **'Typically, programmers are contracted out to work for a client at an agreed hourly or monthly rate for six to eighteen months.'** (23 words; exact.)
- The work: 'data conversion or coding jobs, such as conversion from batch-processing to on-line systems or conversion from source code to UNIX-based systems'.
- These contracts 'represent 70-80 percent of the industry's export revenues'. They are 'short in term, low in risk, low in value added, and low in investment'.
- Drawbacks:
  - 'once the personnel are abroad, the temptations to remain abroad are strong'.
  - Training costs are added 'to low profit margins, though exact figures on these costs are not available'.

### p. 34 (continuation)

- Returnees have gained 'little or no experience of larger scale project management'.
- 'There is a low perception of Indian software skills in OECD markets'.
- **'Demand for these skills is likely to be eroded by tools that automate many of these tasks.'** China and Eastern Europe are 'undercutting Indian prices'.
  - A 1994 forecast of automation risk. Key for Ch. 19 and the epilogue.
- 'Globally, a long-term decline in demand for routine or low-level programmers has been forecasted.'
- A survey of 150 US and European firms (InfoTech 1992, for DoE) ranks Indian services as having 'the lowest labor cost after China' (pp. 34-35).

### Other passages

- Growth: the industry grew at 21% a year 1987-1991, exports at 35% (p. 29).
- Firm structure, p. 32:
  - 'Over three-fourths of the companies have fewer than 25 professionals'.
  - Some firms, 'renting out programmers abroad', 'operate with a one-room office, telephone and fax'.
  - About 95% of firms had software revenue under $100,000.
  - Firms above $1 million in sales: 15 in 1990, 44 in 1992; 27 exported over $1 million in 1992.
- Top exporters, Table 7, p. 33 (Rs million; NASSCOM): TCS 1,754.8; Tata Unisys 560.0; Digital Equipment (India) 317.8; Citicorp Overseas Software 209.4; Wipro Systems 172.0; Siemens Information Systems 160.0; PSI Data Systems 156.5; Patni Computer Systems 156.2; ... Texas Instruments 128.4; Infosys 120.6. The year is probably 1991-92; check the table heading.
- Telecoms: better data links would 'open major opportunities for off-shore software services activities in India, thereby reducing dependence on body-shopping (on-site delivery of services) and the consequent brain-drain' (p. 28).
- Brain drain:
  - 'Migration of graduates from premier institutions like the IITs is estimated to be as high as 50 percent' (p. 75).
  - Annex 2: 'Nearly 60% of IIT Bombay and Madras graduates in computer science leave the country for continuing studies or jobs overseas' (p. 99).
  - Annex 2: 'The orientation of the sector toward renting out programmers for the export market has led to the ubiquitous problem of "brain drain"' (p. 99).
- Annex 2 also reports HP's view that China and Central Europe are 'now cheaper sources' (p. 99).

### Assessment

A contemporary World Bank staff study drawing on NASSCOM data and the 1991-92 DECTA and InfoTech surveys. It is not a primary contract or rate card. But it is the earliest institutional description found of body-shopping contracts as priced in 'hours' or months.

  - **Use this series for the data appendix** (Phase 3). It resolves Rajaraman's $128m/$131m inconsistency (dispute (t)): 131.2 is 1990/91.
- 'these "headline" figures represent gross foreign exchange earnings. Net earnings (less outflow of foreign exchange from India to pay for travel and living allowances of Indian software workers who undertake their contracts overseas, marketing ...) are now estimated to be around 40% of the gross figures' (p. 2).
  - A thesis point: the forex gain from body shopping was much smaller than the headline.
- Onsite share: 'Back in 1988, an average of 65% of export contracts were carried out wholly at the client site, while 35% contained some offshore elements (Heeks 1991). This translated into just under 75% of Indian software export development taking place overseas and only 25% in India. This was even true of work in India's export processing zones' (p. 6).
- **Billing basis:** 'During the period 1988-1998, at least 65% of export contracts were solely for programming work billed on a "time and materials" basis, with programming figuring strongly in the remaining one-third of contracts (Heeks 1996, Dataquest 1998).' (p. 7)
  - **The core documentary support for the 'priced by the hour' thesis.** Time and materials means billing for staff time at agreed rates. It is not necessarily hourly; rates may be daily or monthly.
- Schware 1987, quoted: 'Coding is a relatively simple process … Common business programming representing more than 80% of the world's programming requires comparatively low-level skills' (p. 7).
- 'In the Indian context, the combination of onsite and programming work has come to be known as "body shopping".' (p. 7)
- Gender:
  - 'Only some 10% of Indian software developers are female.'
  - Onsite programming 'is seen, as one manager put it, as "the young, single man's game"'.
  - Women interviewees 'felt that software development offered a more relaxed and less discriminatory atmosphere' (p. 7; cites Jayanthi and Madhavan 1985, *A Comparative Study of Men and Women in Software*, SNDT Women's University: a contemporary study to find).
- Why onsite persisted (pp. 7-8):
  - client trust and risk;
  - client priorities for onsite work: cost first, then credentials, productivity, quality (InfoTech 1992);
  - project size: offshore not worth it under $100,000 (Nicholas 1994);
  - interaction;
  - skills profile: '85% of workers in Indian software exports are programmers';
  - managers' preference: 'quick revenue for low investment';
  - staff wanting to go abroad: 'if denied the opportunity by one company, will simply join another which will send them abroad'.
- Visas (p. 9): under Clinton, 'Indian workers were channelled into taking temporary work visas (rather than student or training or even tourist visas as in the past)'. The cap was lowered and equal-pay rules enforced more thoroughly.
  - **The 'as in the past' clause is evidence that 1980s onsite work used student, training and tourist visas.** Needs a second source and dates.
- Footnote (p. 10): the time-zone 'fairy tale' of US problems solved overnight in India.
- Mumbai: the industry 'started in Mumbai, in the business district around Nariman Point in the late 1970s', then moved to SEEPZ. Mumbai remained the centre 'until at least the mid-1980s' (p. 12).
- TI: 'Once TI arrived and set up operations in 1986, it was the first proper offshore software facility, using satellite-based telecommunication links' (p. 13).
  - Date conflict: Rajaraman says 1985 (DoE-DoT negotiation). Ch. 10 issue.
- Conclusions (pp. 14-15):
  - Body shopping was a path of almost 'inputless' exports, 'which requires only a contact overseas, a little finance, and the names of some local programmers who can be hired if a contract is forthcoming'.
  - It needs no imports, so it worked 'relatively independent of government policy measures, particularly those on imports'.
  - **This directly links import controls to the choice of labour export.**
  - 'earnings are directly proportional to the number of people working in the industry'.
  - 'Coding is the lowest paid part of the software development process, earning only 10-15% of total project revenue (Bhatnagar & Jain 1991).'
  - Correa's three strategies: export of labour, services, products. India's growth came from 'dogged following of the low-risk, low-barrier first path'.
- Vulnerable to 'automation from new technology' (p. 14). A 1998 forecast; relevant to Ch. 19.

## S0242 Chatterjee, 'Invasion of the "Body Shoppers"', *Multinational Monitor* 16, no. 5 (May 1995). Full text read.

This is contemporary investigative journalism from a critical, labour-oriented magazine. It describes 1990 conditions, which fall at the end of the chapter's period.

### The 1990 case

- **'Take Rajesh Ahuja (not his real name), a computer programmer from Bombay, India, who was hired by HCL and sent to its offices in Palo Alto, California, on a B1/B2 visa in 1990.'**
  - The pseudonym is the magazine's. The book must say so. This does not breach the no-composites rule, since it is a documented individual under a pseudonym given by the source.
- He 'worked anywhere HCL sent him, at whatever company he was subcontracted to, and was required to work overtime without extra pay'.
- Ahuja: 'All my life I have dreamed about coming to the United States and being able to go to New York and Disneyland, and listen to bands like the Rolling Stones. I get to do this for two years and my flight is even paid for.' (40 words.)
- Ahuja: 'I shared a two-bedroom apartment and a car with three other programmers, and got $40 a day for expenses. This is enough for me and plus I get my Indian salary of about $800 a month paid into my bank account in India, which is all savings' (49 words; at the limit, so trim).
- **'For Ahuja's services, Sun paid HCL $50 an hour, about half of which remained with HCL.'**
  - The first documented hourly billing rate in the book.
  - Arithmetic (labelled as such): about $8,000 a month billed at 160 hours, against his pay of about $800 a month plus about $1,200 a month in per diem.
- When he went home, HCL 'had not bothered to renew his visa'.
- Ahuja: 'one person would be in charge of all the paperwork, transportation and living arrangement for 160 of us who lived in an apartment complex they rented for us'.

### TCS and the lawsuits

- Kala Sivasubramanian, brought to San Francisco by TCS. When she tried to leave, TCS 'sued to enforce her contract, which called for her to pay more than $30,000 to the company if she quit'.
- TCS's lawyer (D.S. Fastri [sic]) says TCS 'has sued 16 former employees successfully'.
- Date of the Sivasubramanian case is not stated (early 1990s).

### Wages and enforcement

- The US Department of Labor found Indian firms paying immigrant programmers '$15,000 a year plus housing and travel', against '$70,000 plus benefits' for US counterparts.
- Softpac estimates 50,000 temporary computer workers entered the US in 1990-93.
- Syntel was fined $117,000 for underpaying 40 programmers (1995).
- Californians for Population Stabilization sued HCL in 1993 for underpaying programmers.
- Visa restrictions in 1994 cut the onsite share to 63%, and programmers admitted fell from 2,000 to 1,092 (numbers unsourced).

### Firm figures

- TCS: 4,500 employees, $67.3 million in software exports in 1994. 'About 350 of these employees work as contractors for U.S. clients'.
- Tata Unisys: $25 million.
- 'The first "body shoppers" arrived in Silicon Valley in 1987' (unsourced; conflicts with TCS onsite work in the 1970s, so probably Silicon Valley specifically).

### Others quoted

- India-US pay gap: 'good software engineers can earn $100,000 a year in California, their best and brightest counterparts in India earn $10,000' (1995).
- Murli Menon (C&C magazine, 1994): 'In 1984, the government still believed in self-reliance, indigenisation and protection of the domestic [computer] industry'.
- US side: Doug Pfenninger (LA programmer, via LA Times) and Lou Citarella (AIG, displaced by Syntel workers) for the backlash; Ch. 13 material.
