**Chapter question.** What did India lose and gain when IBM left in 1978, and how did the private hardware firms that followed shape the programmers the software industry later sold abroad?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Why IBM left.** In 1973 India amended the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act. Foreign firms not deemed essential had to cut their equity to 40% and seek fresh approval (S0001, p. 29; S0056).

IBM held about 80% of the Indian market (S0055†). It made its money renting refurbished second-hand machines, and parliament's Public Accounts Committee accused it of transfer pricing and dumping obsolete machines (S0056). IBM offered Indian majority ownership of its card factory and data centres, but not of its core marketing and maintenance business (S0056).

After the Janata government took office in 1977, the industry minister, George Fernandes, a former leader of anti-computer agitations, ended the talks. In his own later telling he told IBM: 'You get out' (S0056). IBM closed in June 1978 (S0055†; S0237†).

**Whose story.** The sources disagree on this:
- Rajaraman and Shiv Nadar say IBM chose to leave (S0001, p. 29; S0081).
- Sharma calls it a 'power struggle' (S0055†).
- Banerjee's 2026 book, not yet read, speaks of an 'expulsion' (S0221).

**What happened to IBM's business.**
- The state-owned Computer Maintenance Corporation (CMC) took over maintenance.
- Ex-IBM staff formed IDM to run card making and the data centres (S0001, pp. 29-30).
- How many of IBM's employees found work is disputed (S0001; S0236†).

**The private makers.** The 1978 minicomputer policy let private Indian firms make small computers under licence (S0001, pp. 30-31). HCL, DCM, Wipro, ORG, PSI, Zenith and Patni built machines from imported chips. Because software could not be imported, they wrote their own operating systems and compilers, mostly Unix and C (S0001, pp. 39-40).

Rajaraman credits this with producing 'a large number of systems programmers' expert in Unix, who later served the export trade (S0001, p. 40).

**The hardware path.** It faded after 1984, when assembled boards could be imported and 'self-reliance' was 'truly and completely buried' (S0001, p. 41). India never built a chip foundry (S0001, p. 42).

**Gaps.**
- Rank-and-file voices are thin: one laid-off IBM employee (S0236†) and one IISc student (S0069).
- The archival account of the negotiations (Grieco) is unread.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 IBM in India: market maker and target

IBM entered India in 1951 (S0055†; S0056). By the late 1960s it dominated the market: Sharma gives an 80% share (S0055†). A 1974 survey cited by Bassett counted 217 computers, 65% of them IBM (S0003, p. 800).

Its business was distinctive, and Rajaraman describes it clearly. IBM made 029 key punches with 80% local content for export. It used most of the foreign exchange earned to import used 1401s at low inter-company prices, refurbished them in Bombay and rented them out (S0001, p. 18). It also made punch cards and ran data centres. Its maintenance and systems engineering were bundled into the rent (S0001, pp. 18-19).

Rajaraman, no admirer of monopolies, still wrote that 'A whole generation of maintenance engineers and programmers were trained and nurtured by IBM' (S0001, p. 19; Ch. 3). Sharma agrees that IBM 'created ... a pool of highly trained computer professionals in systems engineering, programming and maintenance' (S0055†).

The same model made IBM a target. Sharma dates the trouble to 1967, when the Electronics Committee under Vikram Sarabhai wanted IBM to stop 'bringing in old machines' and 'refurbishing and leasing them out at inflated rates to government departments' (S0055†). In 2019 he described IBM's ''As is'' policy as one that 'kept the technology in Indian market one or two generations behind the latest' (S0056). Rajaraman records that IBM 'was in fact accused of profiteering', and gives IBM's reply that the rent covered service and software help (S0001, p. 19).

Parliament added a formal charge. After observations by the Comptroller and Auditor General on IBM's dealings with the Railways, the Public Accounts Committee under Hirendra Nath Mukherjee opened 'a full-scale enquiry into the functioning of IBM in November 1975' (S0056). Sharma reports its finding that IBM had 'defrauded the country of enormous revenues by resorting to various unfair practices like transfer pricing under the garb of inter-company billing system, misuse of import entitlements' (S0056). The PAC report itself is not yet seen (Ch. 3, S0022 is a related PAC report). The finding should be quoted from the original before use.

### 3.2 FERA and the negotiation

The legal hinge was the 1973 amendment to the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act. Rajaraman says the act was meant 'to conserve foreign exchange'. Under it, foreign companies 'except those considered essential were to dilute equity to 40% and take an Indian partner' (S0001, p. 29). Sharma adds that firms above 40% 'had to obtain fresh approval from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to continue their operations' (S0056).

The foreign-exchange logic matters for the book's thesis. FERA was one more device, like import licensing and export obligations (Ch. 4), for rationing scarce dollars.

IBM's response, as described by Rajaraman, was to bargain:
- a 100% export-oriented manufacturing unit, whose earnings would pay for imported computers to sell in India;
- a promise that '26% of its profit would be invested in an R and D Centre' (S0001, p. 29).

Sharma describes the offer more precisely: split the Indian operation into two. One part, for card manufacturing and data-centre services, would have '60% Indian equity as required under FERA'; the other would be a fully owned export unit. 'But it was not willing to dilute its equity in its marketing and maintenance business – which was its core business' (S0056).

IBM, Rajaraman says, was 'very confident that it would be considered "essential"'. It had 'over 150 IBM installations in India' among the Railways, the Planning Commission, textile and petrochemical firms and research laboratories (S0001, p. 29).

Rajaraman says IBM refused full dilution 'as it was not its global policy' (S0001, p. 29). This is consistent with IBM's behaviour elsewhere, but the book has no IBM corporate source for it. Grieco's archival study (S0026), which is not read, is the place to test it.

Sharma says 'DoE was hopeful of a negotiated solution' when 'the government changed in March 1977' (S0056). His 2011 piece adds a framing: 'The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA) only came in handy to facilitate IBM's exit', and 'The real reason for IBM's exit was a power struggle between a multinational firm and the government' (S0055†).

### 3.3 Fernandes

The Janata government made George Fernandes communications minister and then, within weeks, industry minister, replacing Brij Lal Verma (S0056). Two facts about him bear on this book:
- 'As a trade Union leader in Bombay, Fernandes had led anti-computer agitation of workers in the 1960s' (S0056). He is the human link between Chapter 3's labour resistance and Chapter 5's exit.
- In the 1977 campaign he had said India did not need 'foreign technology for making brassieres and tooth paste' (S0056).

IBM's executives, by Sharma's account, sought a review at the highest level. Prime Minister Morarji Desai sent them to Fernandes, and the meeting 'turned out to be decisive' (S0056). Fernandes described it to Sharma in 2006:
- 'IBM was very cocky. They went to the extent of telling me that they have refused to accept what the French President, General Charles de Gaulle, had told them'.
- Then: 'if you think the General succumbed to you, I am telling you that I am not succumbing to you. You get out.' Yes, I said 'You get out'.' (S0056)

He also said that IBM's computers 'were taking away jobs of Indian workers' (S0056).

This is a politician's recollection nearly 30 years later, given to a journalist. The de Gaulle exchange is unverified and must be presented as Fernandes's claim. Sharma's own judgement is careful: 'The file on IBM and Coca Cola came to him as FERA cases, and he took the decision based on recommendations of the FERA committee. It was well within the purview of policy and law, but helped Fernandes score a political brownie point' (S0056).

Other multinationals left in the same years. Forbes India lists Coca-Cola, IBM, Mobil and Kodak as having quit or applied to quit by 1978 (S0238†). ICL took the other path: it diluted to 40%, formed ICIM with an Indian partner and began making the ICL 2900 series in 1977 (S0001, p. 30). IBM was therefore not the only foreign computer firm in India, and its exit was a choice its British rival did not make. This comparison is the strongest evidence that leaving was IBM's decision. It does not settle whether the government wanted it to stay.

### 3.4 Who left whom? (dispute (q))

The book must present the disagreement fairly. Four positions are on record:
1. **IBM chose to leave rather than dilute.** Rajaraman: 'the government was unwilling to relax FERA for IBM. Consequently IBM decided to withdraw' (S0001, p. 29). Dataquest's retrospective: IBM left 'as it could not agree with FERA regulations' (S0237†).
2. **A power struggle,** in which FERA was the instrument (Sharma, S0055†).
3. **A source-code rule,** not equity (Nadar). Shiv Nadar, whose firm grew in the gap, said in 2006: 'IBM wasn't evicted. The real story was different.' In his account the government required that the source code of products made in India 'reside in the country', and 'That was the main reason why IBM decided to leave India, as did Coca-Cola' (S0081). No other source seen mentions such a rule. The parallel with Coca-Cola's secret formula, which Fernandes did demand (S0056; S0238†), suggests Nadar may be blending the two cases. Treat it as his claim, to be tested against Grieco and Subramanian.
4. **Expulsion.** The publisher's description of Banerjee's 2026 history speaks of 'the decisive expulsion of IBM' as an act of technological independence (S0221, via ch01.md). Fernandes's own 'You get out' supports this at the level of political intent (S0056).

Provisional reading: the policy (FERA) made IBM's model untenable unless it gave up control of its core business. IBM declined, and the minister closed the door loudly. 'Expelled' and 'chose to leave' describe the same event from different sides. Myth M03 should move from MOSTLY FALSE to MIXED.

### 3.5 The exit and its people

IBM closed its Indian operations in June 1978 (S0055†). Dataquest's retrospective gives 1 June (S0237†). Rajaraman describes the unwinding:
- 'the refurbishing operations stopped';
- the rented computers 'were sold to the customers at their book value which was almost zero';
- 'The card manufacture and data centre operations were taken over by a group of ex-IBM employees who formed a company called International Data Machines (IDM)';
- maintenance went to the Computer Maintenance Corporation, 'set up by the DoE in 1976 to maintain imported computers foreseeing such a contingency';
- spare parts went to CMC 'at nominal price';
- 'Most employees of IBM joined CMC. There was thus no disruption of operations of computers' (S0001, pp. 29-30).

Dataquest says CMC took over 'maintenance of 800 IBM installations' (S0237†). That conflicts with Rajaraman's 'over 150' installations (dispute (s)). The difference may lie in counting unit-record equipment.

The workers' side looks less smooth. Dataquest says T.B. Finn, who became IBM India's general manager in 1976, laid off 'one-third of the existing workforce' within four months. The magazine's comment: 'Obviously, IBM had already made up its mind to exit India' (S0237†). A 2018 report on a reunion of former IBM India staff says there were 'around 150 employees' at the exit, and that 'Many were left out with almost nowhere to go' (S0236†). Some went home to look for work, some found jobs, some started businesses (S0236†).

The 150 figure may echo Rajaraman's 150 installations. A firm with data centres in several cities and a card factory probably employed more. This is dispute (r).

One named worker appears. Kamlaksha Rama (K.R.) Naik was laid off by IBM, later worked at ORG Systems, and organised the 40-year reunion (S0236†). ORG Systems of Baroda was one of the private minicomputer makers licensed after 1978 (S0001, p. 40). Naik's path, from IBM to an Indian hardware firm, is the chapter's argument in one career. His quoted words are thin: 'we share a strong bond and love for IBM' (S0236†). Finding a fuller account from him or the reunion group is a priority.

CMC's own record (S0066, CAG audit material) and its staff numbers in 1978 are not seen.

### 3.6 The 1978 minicomputer policy

IBM's departure coincided with a policy opening. The Janata government's Sondhi committee, with B. Nag as secretary, found 'long delays in importing computers due to the cumbersome procedures of the DoE' and 'suggested liberalization of the manufacture of computers by private manufacturers' (S0001, pp. 30-31).

The resulting minicomputer policy favoured 'systems engineering companies' that need not make CPUs or peripherals. It preferred small entrepreneurs for dedicated machines (data entry, accounting, typesetting) (S0001, p. 31).

The policy was still tightly controlled (S0001, p. 31):
- licences were required;
- production value was capped at Rs 20 million, with limits on numbers;
- foreign collaboration was generally barred, with up to 40% foreign equity in special cases;
- 'Only the public sector company ECIL would be allowed to manufacture 32 bit computers';
- the policy 'would continue to be restrictive in allowing use of computers in areas where labour displacement was likely to be involved'.

The last point carries Chapter 3's labour settlement into the new era.

Rajaraman's verdict is that the policy 'did not completely free the industry from the "licence-permit raj"' but allowed 'the entry of private companies to this hitherto reserved green field area' (S0001, pp. 39-40). Many licences went to traders who opted out. Those who manufactured were DCM Data Products, ORG Systems, Hindustan Computers Ltd, PSI Data Systems, Wipro Systems, Patni Computers, IDM and Zenith (S0001, p. 40).

### 3.7 Founders' accounts: HCL and Wipro

**HCL.** Shiv Nadar's 2006 account shows how the licence regime shaped the firms. 'My colleagues and I were engineers who worked for DCM's calculator division.' The government's Electronics Commission 'was interested in making entrepreneurs out of executives and engineers. They asked us to write a report — and we did — saying that we could build an eight-bit machine' (S0081). Here the state recruits entrepreneurs, not only blocks them.

HCL launched in August 1976. 'Our most difficult challenge was getting permission from the government to make computers'. The founders got it by a joint venture with the Uttar Pradesh government, which held a licence: 'our toughest challenge was not designing or making computers but getting the government's approval to produce them' (S0081).

Capital was Rs 20 lakh, a quarter of it from the UP government, raised partly by selling calculators through a marketing company (S0081). Dataquest says Nadar started 'Microcomp' with six friends and Rs 1.75 lakh (S0237†). This is probably the marketing company, not HCL itself (dispute (u)).

Nadar's view of IBM's exit is the opposite of the usual nationalist story: 'if IBM had stayed on, we would have grown faster. See, IBM was fundamentally creating the market for computers in India.' Without IBM, 'we had to create the market all by ourselves. At that time no one saw the computer as a necessity. It was a convenience' (S0081). He is a founder speaking 30 years later, but he is not speaking in his own commercial interest here, which gives the remark weight.

**Wipro.** Wipro's entry is documented by the Indian Institute of Science, which designed its first machine. Wipro was then 'in the business of producing vegetable and refined oils' (S0069).

In July 1979 it signed a consultancy agreement with IISc worth about Rs 14 lakh. On 5 October 1979 the Registrar wrote that IISc 'has undertaken a project for the design and development of microprocessor-based minicomputer systems for M/S Wipro Products Limited', with know-how to be transferred 'in another fifteen months' (S0069). N.J. Rao, who ran IISc's Digital Systems Lab, recalled that importing Intel's development system 'would have been really difficult for a company... So IISc procured it' (S0069). The university was a way round the import licence.

The Wipro Series-86 appeared in 1981, built on the Intel 8086 and Multibus, with an operating system licensed from the US firm Sentinel (S0069). Its first-year revenue was Rs 2 crore, by Sridhar Mitta's account (S0069). Mitta, who joined in 1980 as R&D manager, explained the global shift that made such firms possible: from 'expensive vertically integrated systems' to 'more open and cheaper, horizontally integrated systems' in which vendors 'could buy, integrate sub-systems and peripherals from different vendors' (S0069).

Azim Premji told IISc's 2019 convocation that 'the Wipro IT business was born in IISc' (S0069). That is a founder's tribute on a ceremonial occasion, so label it.

### 3.8 Programmers made by scarcity: Unix and C

The chapter's central claim for the book's story of the profession comes from Rajaraman. The new firms 'designed their systems from scratch using imported microprocessors, support chips, peripherals, and local components', and 'designed and fabricated printed circuit boards, mounted and soldered all the components and tested the systems' (S0001, p. 40).

Then the key passage: 'As software could not be imported they had to develop operating systems, device drivers, compilers and application programs. UNIX was the operating system of choice as it was easily available' (S0001, p. 40). Firms adopted Berkeley Unix, and the Reserve Bank's Rangarajan committee standardised Unix for bank computers, creating a market that 'could not be ignored' (S0001, pp. 36, 40).

'Thus during 1978-1984 a large number of systems programmers in India became experts in UNIX development and usage. They also adopted C widely as UNIX was written in C. This expertise came in handy when India entered the software services export market in the late 80s.' (S0001, p. 40) Rajaraman's source for this is a personal communication from Vinay Deshpande in 2012 (ref. 73). It is a participant judgement, not a measured fact.

The argument is attractive for this book. Import restrictions on software forced Indian engineers to write systems software, and that skill was what American clients later bought. But no source gives numbers of Unix programmers, or traces individuals from these firms into export work. HCL's own later database, Genesis, written 'from scratch' (S0081), is a founder's example. Treat the causal link as plausible and partly evidenced.

Rajaraman also notes the cost to the state sector: 'ECIL found it difficult to compete with these companies and lost many of their designers to the private sector companies' (S0001, p. 40). The engineers ECIL had trained (Ch. 3: 'over a thousand', S0001, p. 26) became the private firms' staff.

### 3.9 The education response

Two decisions in this period built the pipeline that later chapters examine:
- **IIT Kanpur's undergraduate CS programme, 1978.** Rajaraman, who led it, recalls that the senate 'grudgingly allowed 20 students' because computer science was not 'mainstream' engineering. Yet 'the rank of the last student admitted to the IITK CSc course was 40!', out of over 100,000 candidates for 1,500 IIT seats (S0001, p. 34). The other IITs followed.
- **The Master of Computer Applications (MCA), proposed in 1980.** Rajaraman's Panel on Computer Manpower Development proposed it as a three-year programme for B.Sc and B.Com graduates in computing, mathematics and management, with a semester inside an organisation. The government first funded ten institutions (S0001, pp. 34-35). Rajaraman calls it 'uniquely Indian'. It opened software careers to graduates who were not engineers, a theme for Chapters 7 and 12.

NIIT, founded in 1981, began the private training market (S0001, p. 42; S0237†; Ch. 7).

### 3.10 Still scarce: licences, phones and labour

The regime remained hard on new firms. Infosys's founders needed 'about three years and fifty visits to Delhi to get a license to import a computer worth 300,000 US dollars' (S0034, p. 5). Telephones took over two years in 1982 by Rajaraman's account (S0001, p. 33). Murthy put it at 'about seven years' (S0034, p. 5); his figure is a joke-laden recollection.

In 1981 the Electronics Commission set up a committee under Rajaraman 'to promote software export by allowing import of computers', which 'recommended liberalization of import of computers for genuine exporters' (S0001, p. 35).

Labour resistance did not end with the 1972 report (Ch. 3). Unions declared 1984 an 'anti-computerization year' (S0001, p. 36). Dataquest names the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh and an 'Anti-Computer Day' on 1 March (S0237†). Rajaraman says attitudes changed only after the 1986 railway reservation system (Ch. 8).

### 3.11 The hardware path that faded

The period 1978-84 was India's most serious attempt at a private computer-hardware industry. In Rajaraman's account, there was 'considerable local design and development of computer hardware systems', firms 'designed mother boards and device drivers, modified the UNIX OS and wrote compilers from scratch', and 'Due to competition the prices came down' (S0001, p. 40).

The 1984 policy (Ch. 8) allowed imports of assembled boards. That 'changed manufacturing to assembly and system integration'. 'Thus local manufacturing of boards slowly disappeared' (S0001, pp. 31-32). The 'old-guard derisively called such manufacturing "screw driver" manufacturing' (S0001, p. 40, citing S0025).

Rajaraman's own judgement is unusually sharp. The emphasis on self-reliance, 'the cornerstone of the Bhabha Committee report was truly and completely buried' (S0001, p. 41). And: 'The only major failing was not investing enough resources to develop a local integrated circuit manufacturing industry. Indian engineers could design large scale integrated circuits... for TI and other overseas companies but had no foundry to fabricate them in India' (S0001, p. 42). This sets up the sidebar comparison with Korea and Taiwan. It also sets up the book's question about why India's engineers ended up selling design and code rather than products.

### 3.12 Interpretations

- **Nationalist or dependency reading.** Expelling IBM broke a monopoly that kept India a generation behind and opened space for Indian firms (S0056; S0221 description).
- **Market reading.** Nadar: the exit slowed the market, because IBM had been creating demand (S0081).
- **Bargaining reading.** Grieco, by title and abstract: India's bargaining with multinationals is better explained by the 'bargaining school' than by dependency theory (S0027, register note). Not read; the key acquisition.
- **Participant-historian reading.** Rajaraman: the private hardware firms of 1978-84 produced the Unix and C programmers the export industry later used. The 1984 import liberalisation then ended local hardware (S0001, pp. 40-42).

The readings are compatible on the facts and differ on causation. For the book's thesis, the relevant point is that each version runs through foreign-exchange rules: FERA, import licences, export obligations.

### 3.13 Pay and the profession

The first salary datum for the book's pay series comes from this chapter. In 1976 HCL offered recruits 'a monthly salary of Rs. 2,000 [$180], which was more than what Citibank was paying at that time' (S0081). It hired 'five students from the Indian Institute of Management in Calcutta' (S0081). Nadar was 31 and 'the oldest' (S0081). This is a founder's recollection, but it is specific and checkable against contemporary pay data (Phase 3, dataset plan).

At Wipro, IISc's Digital Systems Lab trained 'several' of the first employees (Narahari in S0069). Named early hires include Victor Jayakaran, Vallab Kulkarni, Srikant Seshadri, Anand Talwai and Ravi Bail (S0069). They are the chapter's rank-and-file, but none speaks in the sources found.
