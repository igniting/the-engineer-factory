# Dossier: Epilogue, India in 2035 (2026-2035)

**Status:** v1, 2026-10-12. Built from `epilogue-notes.md` and the chapter dossiers. All material was live-checked on 2026-10-12.

**Ground rule.** The outline requires 'three or four scenarios stated as scenarios, each tied to evidence in the book ... No predictions presented as fact.' This dossier therefore keeps three things apart:
- **what has happened**: facts with sources, mostly from Ch. 15-19;
- **what others project**: each projection labelled with who made it, when and with what interest;
- **what the book's scenarios suppose**: the author's framing, clearly marked.

Each scenario ends with 'signposts': observable things that would show it unfolding. A reader in 2030 could check them.

**Epilogue question.** Given what the book has shown about how the engineer factory was built, which futures are possible by 2035, and what would tell us which one is arriving?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**No one has published a credible forecast to 2035 for Indian software employment.** The most detailed official projection runs to 2031. NITI Aayog's *Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy* (October 2025, written with Nasscom and BCG) gives two endpoints (S0316):
- **Downside.** On a 'business-as-usual' path, the tech workforce falls from 7.5-8 million in 2023 to 6 million by 2031.
- **Upside.** With a national AI talent mission, it rises to 10 million.

Both figures come from a report co-written by the industry's lobby, which argues for public spending on skills.

**Four scenarios follow from the book's evidence.** Each takes a force documented in Chapters 15-19 and asks what happens if it dominates.
1. **'The thinning pyramid'.** AI-driven price deflation (S0142) and clients' demands for productivity commitments (S0296, S0297) shrink entry-level hiring. The services firms survive with fewer, more senior people.
2. **'The diamond'.** AI services revenue (S0306, S0142) and higher-value work raise revenue per worker. NITI expects price per FTE to rise 5-8% a year (S0316). Employment holds or grows, but its shape changes.
3. **'The client moves in'.** Global capability centres, already at $98.4 billion in FY26 by Zinnov-Nasscom's count (S0318), absorb the work that clients once outsourced. Infosys itself told investors that clients were 'selectively insourcing' (S0297).
4. **'The builder state'.** With the H-1B route narrowed (S0136, S0300) and AI models built for Indian languages (S0187), the domestic market and the state become the main buyers. This extends the precedent of Aadhaar and UPI (Ch. 16, 17).

**These scenarios are not exclusive.** The most likely 2035 combines them in proportions no source can predict. The epilogue should end on the question the prologue opened: what a joining date means when the factory's product is no longer hours.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 What forecasts exist, and whose they are

The book has to be careful with forecasts. Most published numbers about the future of Indian IT come from organisations with an interest in the answer.

The most detailed is NITI Aayog's *Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy*, released in October 2025 (S0316). NITI is the government's policy think-tank. The report was prepared by its Frontier Tech Hub 'in partnership' with Nasscom, the industry's trade body, and the consultancy BCG. Its foreword is signed by Debjani Ghosh, a NITI Distinguished Fellow and formerly Nasscom's president. Ghosh's earlier role is from the book's background knowledge and should be confirmed before it is printed. The foreword says: 'By 2031, India's technology sector stands at a crossroads: we could lose 1.5 million jobs or create up to 4 million new opportunities' (S0316, PDF p. 5).

The executive summary gives the two ends. On a 'business-as-usual' path, 'the headcount in the tech services sector could go down from 7.5 to 8 million in 2023 to 6 million by 2031'. Customer-service jobs would fall from 2-2.5 million to 1.8 million. With action, the totals 'could swell to 10 million and 3.1 million, respectively' (S0316, PDF p. 6).

Three cautions are needed.
- **Base.** The 7.5-8 million base is not Nasscom's industry headcount (5.8 million in FY25, S0140). It is a 'total tech workforce' that includes 'tech jobs in other sectors' (S0316, printed p. 12). The two must never be compared directly.
- **Internal consistency.** The report contradicts itself on its upside. One page says 'by 2030, we can add 2 million more new jobs' (PDF p. 11); others give up to 4 million by 2031.
- **Interest.** The report's purpose is to argue for an 'India AI Talent Mission'. Its scenarios are framed to show the cost of not adopting it. That does not make the numbers wrong. But they are advocacy in a government document, and the book should say so.

The report contains two illustrative characters, 'Rekha', a junior QA engineer in Pune, and 'Aman', an L1 support agent in Chennai (S0316, printed p. 7). These are invented examples in a policy document. The book must not quote them as if they were real people. If it mentions them at all, it should say that the government's own report had to invent its workers.

The second family of forecasts concerns GCCs. In September 2024 Nasscom and Zinnov projected GCC revenue of $99-105 billion by 2030, from $64.6 billion in FY24, with 2,100-2,200 companies employing 2.5-2.8 million (Reuters, S0317). By May 2026, *Business Today* reported the Zinnov-Nasscom FY26 report's figures: $98.4 billion in revenue and 2,117 GCCs employing 2.36 million (S0318). On those figures the 2030 revenue and count projections were nearly met four years early, though headcount was still below the projected range. Both sets of numbers come from the same industry body and consultancy, which sell services to GCCs.

The third is GitHub's projection that India's developers on its platform will reach 57.5 million or more by 2030, passing the US's projected 54.7 million (S0146). That counts accounts on a company's platform, not jobs.

Nothing comparable runs to 2035. The only 2035 date found is NITI's proposed vision 'To position India as the global epicentre of AI talent by 2035' (S0316, printed p. 14). That is a goal, not a forecast. The epilogue's 2035 is therefore the book's own horizon, and the scenarios must be presented as reasoning from evidence, not as anyone's prediction.

### 3.2 Scenario 1: the thinning pyramid

**The force.** Clients pay for outcomes, not hours, and take AI productivity gains as lower prices.

**The evidence.**
- Infosys's risk factor moved from clients that 'have demanded' AI-linked productivity commitments (FY2024, S0291) to clients 'selectively insourcing ... as AI improves productivity' (FY2026, S0297).
- HCLTech's chief executive put a $100 million deal at 'maybe $80 million' for the same scope (S0142).
- Motilal Oswal estimated 12-15% of sector revenue at 'direct AI-driven displacement risk' (S0142, a broker's estimate).
- The NITI report describes the same mechanism in its pyramid archetypes: 'a shrinking of entry-level roles involving QA and support' and, in sum, 'fewer layers, faster releases, and leaner teams' (S0316, printed p. 8).
- Wipro's FY2026 report says 'Hiring will shift from volume to skills' (S0298).

**What it means for the book's subject.** The engineer factory was built to turn out large numbers of entry-level engineers whose hours could be sold (Ch. 7, 12). In this scenario the bottom of the pyramid is what shrinks. Colleges keep producing graduates the firms no longer need in volume. The prologue's joining date becomes the normal condition, not the exception.

**Signposts.**
- Fresher offers as a share of headcount at the top five firms, falling year after year.
- Revenue per employee rising while headcount falls.
- More fixed-price and outcome-based contracts in the filings.
- AICTE computer-science intake staying high while placement rates fall.

**Counter-evidence.** Infosys's offers recovered in FY2025-26 (S0296, S0297). TCS onboarded 24,000 freshers in the first half of FY27 (S0138, S0310). Nasscom still reports net hiring (S0306). The 2022-24 delays came before agentic tools (S0133, S0134). So the pyramid had not visibly thinned by 2026.

### 3.3 Scenario 2: the diamond

**The force.** AI creates new paid work, and the price of each remaining worker rises.

**The evidence.**
- Nasscom estimates AI revenue at $10-12 billion in FY26 (S0306).
- TCS reports annualised AI revenue above $2.3 billion. Its chief executive expects AI to be 'net accretive', overcompensating for traditional revenue that would 'slowly taper down' (S0142).
- NITI/BCG/Nasscom expect 'the price-per-FTE for the industry to grow 5-8% annually' and industry growth of 3-9% a year (S0316, printed p. 13). Their upside path takes the tech workforce to 10 million.
- In Breakingviews' paraphrase, the staffing executive Kamal Karanth described a 'diamond shape' with a big middle replacing the pyramid (S0301).

**What it means.** Fewer people enter at the bottom, but more of those in the middle are paid more for judgement, domain knowledge and supervising AI-written code. Wipro's 'AI fluency, domain expertise' (S0298) is the job description.

**Signposts.**
- Revenue per employee and average pay both rising.
- Lateral hiring outpacing fresher hiring (S0139: 'a lateral can deliver immediately').
- AI revenue rising faster than total revenue, and reported on comparable definitions.

**Counter-evidence.** The price-per-FTE forecast dates from October 2025. By May 2026 firms and brokers were describing deflation (S0142). This is dispute (lll), and the book should show it as a live disagreement.

### 3.4 Scenario 3: the client moves in

**The force.** Multinationals build their own Indian centres instead of buying from Indian vendors.

**The evidence.**
- Chapter 15 traces foreign-owned engineering centres in India from Texas Instruments in 1985 to over 1,700 GCCs in FY24 (S0141).
- By FY26 Zinnov-Nasscom count 2,117 GCCs employing 2.36 million, with revenue of $98.4 billion (S0318). That is close to the 2030 projection (S0317).
- The same report, as reported, says India holds 'nearly 28% of the global GCC AI talent pool' (S0318).
- Cognizant, a US company, has 256,900 of its 351,600 employees in India (S0299).
- Infosys's FY2025 20-F lists among its risks that clients may 'increase their in-house IT capabilities ... by setting up or expanding their own GCC in India' (S0296, Item 3.D). Its FY2026 report says clients are 'selectively insourcing' (S0297).
- Karnataka's GCC policy targets 500 new GCCs by 2029 (S0204, via summaries).

**What it means.** In this scenario Indian engineers keep their jobs but change employers. The national champions (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) matter less, and foreign firms' Indian subsidiaries matter more. That revives Chapter 5's question about who owns the capability. It also connects to myth M25, that GCCs are new.

**Signposts.**
- GCC headcount growing while the top five services firms' headcount is flat.
- More services-firm revenue from building and running GCCs for clients.
- Bengaluru and Hyderabad office absorption led by GCCs.
- State policies beyond Karnataka (the national framework, S0205).

**Counter-evidence.** GCC figures come from an industry body and a consultancy that sells to GCCs. Definitions have been restated before (S0140). An independent count was not found.

### 3.5 Scenario 4: the builder state

**The force.** Barriers abroad and sovereign-AI ambitions turn the industry inward, with the Indian state as builder and buyer.

**The evidence.**
- The H-1B route that carried Indian engineers to client sites since the 1990s (Ch. 13) was narrowed by the $100,000 payment of September 2025 (S0136), extended to 2027 (S0300).
- The administration claims the largest IT outsourcers' registrations fell 92% (S0300).
- The courts had blocked the fee by October 2026 (S0137), so this route is contested, not closed.
- At home, the state has a record of building at population scale: Aadhaar (Ch. 16) and UPI (Ch. 17).
- IndiaAI committed INR 10,372 crore in 2024 and selected Sarvam and others to build foundation models (S0187, S0308). Sarvam released 30B and 105B models in February 2026 (S0187).
- NITI proposes an 'India AI Talent Mission', an 'India Open-Source AI Commons' and a 'federated national compute and innovation grid' (S0316, PDF p. 7).

**What it means.** More engineers work on Indian problems for Indian users, through the state or through product firms (Ch. 17). This is the answer to the book's first central question (why India built services, not products) that the 2020s may begin to change.

**Signposts.**
- Government AI and digital-infrastructure spending sustained beyond one budget cycle.
- Indian-language models used in state services.
- The domestic share of industry revenue rising (Nasscom reported domestic revenue growing 7.9% in FY26, faster than exports, S0306).
- A rising share of new graduates employed by domestic product firms and the state.

**Counter-evidence.** *Inc42* judged that Sarvam's models trail the frontier models in 'raw reasoning depth' (S0187). India's share of granted AI patents fell to under 5% (S0316, printed p. 9, citing the AI Index). NITI itself reports net negative AI talent migration (S0316, printed p. 10). The builder-state record in payments and identity may not transfer to frontier AI, which needs far more capital.

### 3.6 What the book can and cannot say

The epilogue can say:
- what the companies told investors;
- what the government and lobby projected, and with what interest;
- what the scenarios assume, and what would show each one arriving.

It cannot say which will happen. It should not use the NITI personas, the WEF figures (not read), or any projection without its author's name. It should state that the AI model assisting the book is made by a company whose products appear in this story (see the Ch. 19 conflict note), so the book has an interest in how AI is portrayed.

## 6. Disputes

- **(lll) Price per worker.** NITI/BCG/Nasscom (October 2025) expect price per FTE to grow 5-8% a year (S0316). Firms and brokers in 2026 describe AI deflation, with deal values about a fifth lower for the same scope (S0142). The measures differ, but the directions are opposite.
- **(mmm) Workforce base.** NITI's 7.5-8 million (2023, all tech jobs in all sectors) vs Nasscom's 5.67-5.8 million (industry only) (S0316, S0140). Not comparable.
- **(nnn) NITI's upside.** '2 million more new jobs' 'by 2030' (PDF p. 11) vs up to 4 million by 2031 (forewords and summary) (S0316).
