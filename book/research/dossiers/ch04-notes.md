## S0036 Kohli, CHM oral history (Kapoor, 15 January 2020). Full transcript read 2026-10-12.

Transcript is 8 pages. The footer misprints every page as 'Page 17 of 17', so pages below are counted in order from the title page (title = p. 1).

- Kapoor introduces Kohli as born in Peshawar in 1924. Kohli corrects him: he went to Canada in 1946, 'before the partition'. Path: BSc Honours in Physics at Government College Lahore; Queen's University (BSE Honours, EE); about 11-12 months at General Electric; MIT master's on scholarship; New England Power Company, where he learned load dispatch (pp. 2-3).
- Returned 1951 to Tata Electric to set up 'the first load dispatch in India'. 'in '69, I had started first computer controlling the power grid. And then Mr. J. R. D. Tata asked me to setup Tata Consultancy.' (p. 3)
  - Contrast: his 2009 speech (S0231) dates digital load dispatch to 1968.
- On Burroughs: 'Burroughs, I built up relationship when I was there in United States.' Also: 'First partnership came-- partnership not legal, though-- with Burroughs, which was number two computer company at that time. IBM came much later.' (pp. 4-5)
- On growth strategy: 'I had built up very strong interaction with the engineering colleges, especially the IITs, starting from Kanpur IIT. So people knew about us.' (p. 4)
  - Also: 'There was no question of my going for business. The people came to us, "Could you do this for us?"' (p. 4)
  - This is self-description; S0038 and S0228 describe him travelling to the US repeatedly to win work.
- 'Because you take Tata Consultancy Services. First few years it was built only on exports. People didn't understand.' (p. 5)
  - Contradicts the domestic-first record: the Tata group's own data processing, bank reconciliation and the telephone directory (S0038, S0039, S0235). Treat it as a 95-year-old's compression. It is still useful as his framing.
- Retirement: 'I worked till I was 75 with Tata Consultancy Services.' Left in 1999, not 1996 (p. 5).
- On being called 'Father' of the industry: 'Father is a misnomer. I'm not a-- I'm father of my three children. That's all.' (p. 7)
- Advice: 'I had to see all those people who do not know English still can work with the computers.' (p. 7)
  - Relevant to the English-language thread (Ch. 6, Ch. 19).
- Assessment: thin on facts and halting. Its value is in a few exact first-person lines and in his corrections of dates.

## S0231 Kohli, *Towards Inclusive Information Technology Revolution in India* (Forum of Free Enterprise booklet, 2009). Full text read.

The booklet reproduces Kohli's acceptance speech for the Bombay Management Association Lifetime Achievement Award (2008-09). Its pages are imposed, so the citations below give PDF page numbers.

- 'I have worked for over 50 years -the first 18 years with Tata Electric Companies (TEC), and over 30 years with Tata Consultancy Services (TCS)' (PDF p. 4).
  - 'We implemented digital computers for system control and load dispatching in 1968.' (PDF p. 4)
- 'TCS was set up in 1968 to partake in the new technology' (PDF p. 4).
  - Kohli's own dating is therefore 1968, against Sharma's 1967 (S0228) and Bassett's 1967 (S0003).
- **The 1975 address.** January 1975, Kohli's presidential address to the Computer Society of India's annual convention at Ahmedabad, as Kohli himself quoted it in 2009:
  - 'Many years ago, there was an industrial revolution. We missed it due to reasons over which we had no control.' (PDF p. 4)
  - 'We have an opportunity to participate in this revolution on an equal footing; we even have the opportunity to be leaders.' (PDF p. 4)
  - The full quotation runs to about 60 words, so split it or quote under 50.
  - This is a 34-year-later self-quotation; the original 1975 text is not seen (try S0234, or CSI Communications 1975).
- Same page: technology obsolete in 'less than 24 months', hence continuing education.
  - Industry figures: 'revenue exceeding US $60 billion, with $50 billion coming from exports', 'over two million jobs' (PDF p. 4).
  - These are 2008-09 claims by an industry founder; Ch. 12 and Ch. 13 will use NASSCOM data instead.

## S0038 Venkatraman, 'India's IT Guy', MIT Technology Review (Jan/Feb 2013). Read via WebFetch †; the original PDF on the author's site was blocked (502).

- In 1969 Kohli was general manager at Tata Electric, having just computerised control of the Bombay grid. The Tata group asked him to launch TCS as general manager.
  - His task: computerise operations for willing Tata companies and win outside clients 'in a country with very few computers' (paraphrase †).
- **First mainframe.** An ICL 1903, bought cheaply from a government-owned insurance company after a labour union blocked its installation, calling it a 'job-eating monster' †.
  - The insurer is not named. If it was LIC, this ties directly to the LIC computerisation agitation (Ch. 3, S0020, S0226, S0227).
  - Life insurance was nationalised in 1956; general insurance was nationalised in 1971-73.
  - Need a second source naming the insurer and the year.
- Two leased IBM machines supplemented the ICL.
  - Rajaraman says TCS's first computer was an IBM 1401 (S0001, p. 19). The two accounts can both be true if 'first mainframe' means first owned machine. Dispute (m) below.
- Early projects: in-house systems; inter-branch reconciliation for a nationalised bank; Bombay's telephone directory.
- Kohli recruited programmers from IIT Kanpur's computer-science graduates.
- 1974: when the TCS director died, Kohli succeeded him. The company had struggled and took management-consulting work to stay afloat.
  - Kohli: 'TCS was up, but it wasn't running.' †
  - The director is not named in any source seen. The PTI obituary (via search summary) says GM 1969, director-in-charge 1974, later chief executive. Sharma (S0228) says Kohli 'succeeded Kanodia in 1969', a different succession.
  - Hypothesis to test: Kanodia was the first manager (1968-69), Kohli GM from 1969, and an unnamed Tata director was the board-level head until 1974. Unconfirmed.
- **Burroughs (1974 per S0038).** Burroughs, the Detroit computer maker, wanted a healthcare software package to sell with its new computers. TCS had no Burroughs machine.
  - Kohli: 'But we didn't have a Burroughs machine in India' †.
  - TCS wrote the software on the ICL and built a 'filter' program to convert it to run on Burroughs hardware.
- The work made Burroughs a large repeat client.
- **Machine and export obligation.** TCS needed its own Burroughs mainframe at $340,000, more than twice that with tariffs.
  - The government allowed the import on condition that TCS export twice the machine's value within five years. TCS did.
  - Ambiguity: 'twice the value' may mean twice the $340,000 or twice the landed cost. Which machine is not stated here; S0001 says B6700.

## S0228 Sharma, 'F.C. Kohli and the Eureka Moment...', The Wire Science (27 November 2020). Read via WebFetch †.

- 'TCS had been established in 1967 as Tata Computer Centre to handle the data-processing work of all companies of the Tata group' †.
- Founding team †:
  - three MIT management graduates who had interned with the Tatas: Lalit Surajmal Kanodia, Nitin Patel and Ashok Malhotra;
  - 'Yashpal Sahni, a postgraduate of the Indian Statistical Institute, was another founding member.'
- Kohli, deputy general manager at Tata Electric, 'was shifted in late 1969 to Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) as general manager'. 'Kohli succeeded Kanodia in 1969.' †
- IBM and ICL 'followed the practice of importing used machines'. After a CAG audit, the government reined them in and handed the task to the new Department of Electronics †.
  - Links Ch. 3 (PAC/CAG) and Ch. 5.
- 'He decided to work with Burroughs – IBM's closest rival.'
  - Breakthrough: a Burroughs contract for 'writing OS software for a new series of mainframes' †.
  - Note: this is systems software, not the healthcare application S0038 describes. Different projects, or a different emphasis.
- Clients that followed: Detroit Police, City of Detroit, State University of New York, banks †.
- Kohli 'visited the US at least nine times between 1972 and 1974' †. This is the counter-evidence to Kohli's 'the people came to us'.
- TCS's first external customer: 1971 (per summary; detail to confirm).
- Tata Burroughs Limited, 'which along with TCS remained India's top software exporter for a long time' †.
- The search snippet attributes the filter program to 'young engineers' †. That is a possible collective rank-and-file angle, but none are named.
- Sharma is the author of S0005/S0006. His books probably carry the fuller version with notes; priority acquisition for this chapter.

## S0039 Tata Group TCS timeline (company self-description). Read via WebFetch earlier.

- 1968: founded; bank reconciliation, payroll, accounting.
- 1971: power utility in Iran, the first overseas project.
- 1973: partnership with Burroughs.
  - Also 1973: conversion of a hospital-accounting system for a Detroit client, which TCS calls the 'first recorded instance of offshore software delivery' (company claim).
- 1974: full-lifecycle project for a UK housing society, for Burroughs.
- 1976: revenue over $1 million; Kohli receives an EEPC top-exporter award.
- 1978: partnership with Burroughs ends.
- 1979: New York office; IGIC contract (Institutional Group Information Corporation) using an onsite-offshore model with a 24-hour cycle.
- 1981: TRDDC Pune.
- 1983: Swiss National Bank, the first offshore project from TCS Delhi.

## S0001 Rajaraman (2012), relevant pages (from ch03-notes)

- p. 19: TCS's first computer was an IBM 1401; most Tata companies had 1401s.
- p. 23: import clearances took one to three years, which 'hurt software exporters'. The 1971 and 1974 embargoes.
- p. 28: 'Recognizing the potential of software export DoE allowed import of computers exclusively meant for software export. The first policy paper on this was published in 1974.'
  - TCS imported a Burroughs B6700 and converted other makers' software to Burroughs. It scouted work in Iran, Switzerland, South Africa, Canada and the USA.
  - A Tata-Burroughs manufacturing company was set up in 1975 and closed after four years.

## S0230 Aspray, Rutherford Journal (c. 2010). Read via WebFetch.

- 'In 1970 the Indian government created a Department of Electronics, whose mandate included the stimulation and regulation of the software export industry.'
- '1972 bill that created a Software Export Scheme, which relaxed the import of hardware for companies that would use the hardware to create software for export.'
  - **Dispute (n):** 1972 (Aspray; World Bank blog via search summary) against 1974 (Rajaraman's 'first policy paper').
  - Possible reconciliation: the scheme was announced in 1972 and the first formal policy paper came in 1974. Needs a primary DoE document (DoE annual report 1972-73 or 1974-75; Subramanian S0025; Heeks S0070).
- 'By 1980, however, it was clear that most of the hardware being imported to India was being used for domestic purposes, not for creating software exports.'
- 'In 1970 TCS purchased a Burroughs mainframe computer'; TCS became Burroughs' exclusive agent in India; 'The following year, Burroughs asked TCS to send programmers to the United States to install systems software for Burroughs customers. Thus was the Indian export software business born.'
  - The 1970 purchase date conflicts with every other source (1973-74). Aspray's piece has other slips: FERA is misnamed, and a TCS move into a Mumbai zone in 1973. Do not use Aspray for dates.
  - Useful as the cleanest statement of the onsite 'body shopping' origin. Aspray's line that the service 'came derisively to be known' as body shopping is relevant to Ch. 6.
- 1980: 21 firms, '$4 million of export business annually'; 'As of 1980, the Indian software industry was predominantly a body-shopping industry.' Check against Heeks (S0070) and DoE figures before use.

## S0003 Bassett (2009), pp. 800-802 (from ch02-notes)

- Tata 'established a computing enterprise in 1967'; Kohli moved to head it in 1969.
- Kohli learned computing on Kanpur's 1620. He sat on Kanpur's EE faculty-selection committee and arrived for the ten-day course by cycle rickshaw (n. 43). First Indian on the IEEE board (1973).
- Kohli often hired Kanpur's computing master's graduates. Mahabala summered at TCS as a consultant (p. 801).
- Murthy went from IIM Ahmedabad and Krishnayya's think tank to Patni's firm (Bassett calls it 'Patni Data Systems'). In 1981 he left with six other Patni employees to found Infosys (p. 801).
- American-oriented elites (Patni, Kohli) 'gave a special potency to the computing work of IIT Kanpur graduates' (p. 802).
- Bassett interviewed Kohli and Patni, so the footnotes point to interview dates.
