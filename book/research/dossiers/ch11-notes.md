## 1. Where Infosys stood at the start of the period

F-1 p. 27: 'From fiscal 1994 through fiscal 1998, revenues increased from $9.5 million to $63.8 million, the number of the Company's IT professionals worldwide increased from approximately 480 to approximately 2,200, and the number of its India software development centers increased from two to nine.' p. 5 gives the CAGRs: revenue 63.6%, net income 46.8%, FY1994-98.

F-1 p. 12: dollar-denominated revenue was 90.0% of total in FY1998 and 89.2% in the nine months to December 1998. The rupee fell about 39.5% against the dollar between December 1994 and December 1998. Point for the chapter: costs in rupees, revenue in dollars, a falling rupee. The margin came from the exchange rate as well as the work.

## 2. Price and the GE episode (reuse from Ch. 9)

Murthy p. 16: 'at least until 2007, 2008, probably 2009, Infosys always got 20-25 percent higher price compared to our competitors in India.' Self-account; no independent price data. His explanation is 'Business Value Leverage': sell on value to the client, not on technology. Use only as his claim.

The GE renegotiation of May 1995 is told in Ch. 9 (S0034, pp. 18-20). In Ch. 11 it should be a short bridge: the lesson Infosys took was not to depend on one client. F-1 p. 10 lists client concentration and fixed-price estimation among the causes of fluctuating results.

## 3. Process: ISO 9001 and CMM

Rajaraman p. 45: all companies entering the export market obtained ISO 9001; many also sought SEI's CMM rating. 'The Motorola (India) software team was the first team in the world to attain this maturity level in 1993.' p. 46: ISO 9001 matters for Europe, CMM for the USA; 'only 250 companies in the world have this certification in 2012 out of which 166 are in India. Correct statistics are hard to obtain.'

The key point for the thesis is on p. 46: 'The process orientation allowed companies which were recruiting large numbers of employees each year to quickly train them to become productive members of a development team. Staff attrition also could be managed better due to the disciplined software development process.' So process was a way to make large numbers of new graduates interchangeable and productive. It was a labour-management technology as much as a quality badge. This links to Ch. 12 (mass hiring) and to the AI chapters (what happens when the documented, process-bound work can be automated).

F-1 p. 6: 'Infosys has been certified at Level 4, a level achieved by only 2% of the more than 1,000 software companies tested under the Capability Maturity Model.' Company claim in a sworn filing. 'Certified' is the company's word; SEI appraisals were assessments, not certifications. Flag for the drafting.

Counts at the end of 1999:
- Saxenian p. 11: 'In December 1999, 137 Indian companies had obtained either ISO 9000 or SEI-CMM Level 2 certification, and ten companies were certified at Level 5 (the highest level, at which only six U.S. companies are certified.)'
- Rajaraman p. 52: 'In 1999, 6 out of 12 CMM level 5 certified companies in the world were in India.'

These conflict (10 Indian Level 5 firms vs 6 of 12 worldwide). They may be counting different things (organisations vs companies, or different months). Log as dispute (gg).

Motorola: GSG media kit (S0199) says its India centre was the 'first commercial organization in the world to reach SEI PMM Level 5', 1993. Rajaraman repeats it (p. 45) without a separate source. Business Today, per the register note on S0199, says first in India, 1994. No SEI record in hand. Dispute (cc) stays open; M26 stays UNRESOLVED (company claim).

Athreye (S0094) argues that tight labour markets drove process capability. It is not read. Do not cite it beyond the register note.

## 4. The offshore model

F-1 p. 45, 'The Infosys Offshore Development Model'. 'The Indian offshore development model was initiated in the mid-1980's as a method of dividing IT project activities between a service provider's offshore software development facility and a client's on-site location.' Five attractions are listed: English-speaking pool, low labour costs, quality standards, a 24-hour working day across time zones, and parallel processing of project phases.

The typical project shape is on the same page. 'the Company assigns a small team of two to five IT professionals to visit a client's site'. The project managers then 'return to India to supervise a much larger team of 10 to 50 IT professionals'. A small team stays onsite to track changes. Systems are 'linked via satellite'. A team returns to install, and a maintenance agreement often follows. This is the pyramid, described by the company itself: a few senior people at the client and many juniors in India.

Rajaraman pp. 44-45 calls it 'what is known as the Global Delivery Model (GDM)': 20-30% of the time onsite in face-to-face work, 70-80% (mainly coding and testing) in India, in secure client-dedicated areas with dedicated satellite links. Then the '24 hour work day' model: bugs reported at the end of the US day are fixed in India's prime shift.

Naming: the F-1 (1999) says 'offshore development model'. 'Global Delivery Model' is a later Infosys brand. Do not put 'GDM' in Infosys's mouth before a dated source shows it.

## 5. Y2K

Rajaraman p. 46 gives the standard account. Two-digit years, mostly COBOL; labour-intensive fixing; 'not enough software engineers in the USA and Europe'; 'a godsend opportunity'. Firms 'earned the goodwill of clients by not only fixing the Y2K bug but also improving the software (when appropriate) at no extra cost'. The work gave 'entry to the Fortune 500 companies'. Then: 'It is estimated that 23% of the revenue of INFOSYS ... in 1997 was for fixing the Y2K bug [4, p.349].' His reference 4 is a secondary source; see dispute (dd).

F-1 p. 15, the primary figure. 'Year 2000 conversion projects represented 23.3% and 21.4% of the Company's revenues for fiscal 1998 and the nine months ended December 31, 1998, respectively.' Fiscal 1998 ended 31 March 1998, so Rajaraman's '1997' is mostly calendar 1997 and the two agree. The 7.5% figure logged in Ch. 9 is FY1997 (ended March 1997; from the F-1 MD&A, as recorded in the Ch. 9 notes). **Dispute (dd) resolved:** about 7.5% in FY1997, 23.3% in FY1998, 21.4% in April-December 1998. Y2K at Infosys rose sharply in 1997-98 and was already falling as a share by late 1998.

F-1 p. 15 risk factor quotes:
- 'The high demand for these time-sensitive projects results in pricing and margins that are favorable to the Company.'
- demand 'will begin to diminish rapidly after fiscal 1999'
- risk 'that the Company may be unable to retrain and redeploy IT professionals who are currently assigned to Year 2000 conversion projects involving legacy computer systems after such projects are completed'.

The last is the clearest documented statement that Y2K created a cohort of programmers trained on one kind of work that would end. Useful for the AI chapter's argument about skills tied to a single wave of demand.

Industry level (S0109, Computergram, June 1999, reporting NASSCOM; lobby figures):
- Indian firms earned $2.3bn from Y2K to date, perhaps $3bn by the end of 1999, then 'a few hundred million dollars next year';
- exports 1998/99 $2.65bn (+51.4%), Y2K $560m of that (21.1%);
- 203 of the Fortune 1000 used Indian software services;
- the top 25 firms had 63% of exports;
- Dewang Mehta's target was $50bn by 2007/08, and NASSCOM spoke of the 'three Es' (e-commerce, ERP, euro).

Note the internal inconsistency: a cumulative $2.3bn is hard to square with $560m in 1998/99 unless earlier years or other periods are counted. Present the 21.1% share, which is internally anchored to that year's exports, and attribute the cumulative figure as a NASSCOM estimate.

S0108 (Outlook, c. January 2000) frames the smooth rollover as a possible 'hoax'. It gives India's Y2K earnings as Rs 1,800 crore, with a wrong dollar conversion. Use only as evidence that the question was asked at the time. Original date to confirm.

**M02 assessment.** Exports grew 28-63% a year before Y2K (S0001). Y2K was about a fifth of exports in 1998/99 (S0109) and of Infosys revenue in FY1998 (S0089). The company itself expected the work to end quickly. Y2K accelerated growth and opened client doors (S0001, p. 46: 'entry to the Fortune 500'). It did not create the industry. Keep MIXED, with sharper evidence.

## 8. Nasdaq

**Infosys.** The F-1 was filed in February 1999 for 1.8 million ADSs (register note on S0090; press release not read). The 424B4 of 11 March 1999 (S0259) gives a price of $34.00 per ADS, a $1.68 underwriting discount and $61.2m gross. The Nasdaq symbol was INFY. The equivalent Mumbai price on 10 March 1999 was $37.18 per ADS at Rs 42.50/$. So the ADSs were priced slightly below the Indian market price. The common claim that Infosys was the 'first Indian company listed on Nasdaq' (or on a US exchange) has not been found in any source read. Gap.

Infosys's Indian IPO came first. Murthy p. 18: the IPO was 'between 24th and 27th of February but listing was in June!' (1993), and it 'opened pretty well at about 50 percent premium'.

**Satyam Infoway (Sify).** 20-F FY2002 (S0261):
- 'Our ADSs commenced trading on the Nasdaq National Market on October 19, 1999.'
- Since the IPO, and after a 4-for-1 ADS split in January 2000, the price 'has ranged from a high of $113 per ADS to a low of $0.87per ADS' (sic) to 31 March 2002.
- A class action filed on 5 November 2001 covers buyers between 20 October 1999 and 6 December 2000. It alleges undisclosed underwriter allocation arrangements; this is the IPO-allocation litigation that named many issuers of that period.
- Satyam Computer Services owned about 52.5% and controlled the 'Satyam' trademark licence.
- Competitor rediff.com 'completed its initial public offering in the United States in June 2000'.

Sify is the first link between this chapter and Ch. 16 (Satyam).

**Rediff.** Per the register note on S0179 (CNN Money, 14 June 2000; not re-read this session): 4.6m ADSs at $12, a rise of about 61% on the first day, $55.2m raised. Sify's 20-F confirms June 2000.

## 9. ESOP and the new wealth

F-1 p. 28:
- 'In 1994, the Company established the ESOP, one of the first employee stock option plans in India.'
- An employee welfare trust was issued warrants for 750,000 shares (2,000,000 after the 1997 and 1998 stock dividends).
- Stock purchase rights carry an exercise price of Rs 100 ($2.35 in February 1999), 'substantially below the market price'.
- Five-year vesting. Indian GAAP did not require an expense for this; US GAAP did, hence the disclosure.

S0262 (Business Today, 21 January 2001; the magazine named Infosys Best Employer in India):
- Stock options to '7,853 of its 7,925 employees (as on September 30, 2000) creating, in the process, 1,773 rupee millionaires and 213 dollar millionaires.'
- The share price rose from Rs 63.75 (early April 1996) to Rs 7,160 (15 December 2000).
- Sales rose from Rs 88.97 crore (1995-96) to Rs 884.35 crore (1999-2000).
- 'a couple of enterprising Bangalore-based real-estate developers make a living primarily from selling apartments to people who work for Infosys.'
- The Rs 320 crore campus was 50 acres.

'Rupee millionaire' means Rs 10 lakh or more. Note this when drafting so readers do not read it as a crore.

## 10. The bust

- **Sify.** The fall from $113 to under $1 (S0261).
- **Infosys.** It kept growing (FY1998-2002 revenue CAGR 68.1%; S0260), but warned that 'The economic slowdown in the U.S. has impacted the growth prospects of companies that are in the insurance, banking and financial services and telecom segments' and that clients 'may cut their IT spending or postpone decisions'.
- **Campuses (S0263, Business Line, 17 January 2002).**
  - Anna University placed 'only around 320 students', down from 1,200 a year earlier.
  - TCS took 54 (177), Infosys 46 (126), Cognizant 69 (105).
  - Baluswamy: 'A few companies have delayed the date of joining for last year students by over a year'.
  - Some IT companies, 'except big players such as TCS, Infosys and CTS', had cut the pay promised on campus 'by 50-60 per cent'.
  - At IIT Madras, about 30% of students stayed away from campus interviews because of 'shocking stories of lay-offs, delayed appointments and salary cuts'. 'most engineering students wanted to consider only engineering companies, rather than shift to IT.' Citibank gave the highest salary, Rs 6.5 lakh a year.

The bust hit new graduates and small firms hardest. The large firms slowed hiring but kept growing.

## 11. Pricing thread (for the thesis)

The thread runs through four documents:
- **F-1 p. 15 (1999):** Y2K's urgency gave 'pricing and margins that are favorable'.
- **F-1 p. 10:** fixed-price estimation is a risk.
- **F-1 p. 44:** Infosys is 'seeking to increase the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-time frame rather than a time-and-materials basis'.
- **20-F FY2002:** 'we have increased the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-timeframe, rather than a time-and-material basis, which reduce client risk, and if effectively structured and executed, provide opportunities for enhanced margins.'

So by 2002 the leading firm was explicitly trying to move from selling hours to selling outcomes, because outcomes let it keep productivity gains. This is the hinge for the AI chapters: under time-and-materials, a tool that halves the hours halves the revenue; under fixed price, it can raise the margin. The time-and-materials and fixed-price shares by year need the 20-F series (S0161) in Phase 3.
