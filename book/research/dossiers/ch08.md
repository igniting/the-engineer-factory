**Chapter question.** In the years when Rajiv Gandhi's government loosened the computer permit regime, which changes actually moved software work into India, and which only moved the paperwork?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The 1984 policy.** In November 1984, days after Rajiv Gandhi became prime minister, the government announced a new computer policy (S0237†). It let any firm make 32-bit machines, removed capacity limits, allowed imported circuit boards and cut duty on computers for export. Before that, duty had been 'over 100%' (S0001, pp. 31-32). It also recognised onsite work abroad as 'software export' (S0001, p. 32).

**The 1986 policy and the targets.** The December 1986 software policy allowed duty-free computer imports for exporters, against an obligation to earn 250% of the cost within four years. It also allowed exports by satellite (S0001, p. 32; S0241, p. 106). The 1984 plan expected software exports of $258 million by 1990 (S0241, p. 96). The actual figure for 1990/91 was about half that, $128-131 million (S0241, p. 100; S0085).

**TI and the satellite link.** In 1985 the Department of Electronics persuaded the telecom monopoly to let Texas Instruments run its own satellite link from Bangalore to Dallas (S0001, p. 36). An eyewitness recalls the equipment arriving on a bullock cart on Miller Road (S0249). Rajaraman calls the link 'the first step in breaking the monopoly' of the telecom department (S0001, p. 36).

**The railway system and union opinion.** In 1984 bank unions observed an 'anti-computerization year' (S0001, p. 36). The 1986 railway reservation system, built by Indian engineers at the state firm CMC, changed public and union opinion. It sold tickets faster and added counter clerks rather than cutting them (S0001, p. 38).

**Cray and C-DAC.** When the US failed to license a Cray supercomputer for IISc in time, India built parallel machines. C-DAC was founded in 1988 and its first machine completed in 1991 (S0001, pp. 36-37).

**Software Technology Parks.** The parks of 1990-91 offered shared satellite links and single-window clearance. That let small firms do export work from India rather than abroad (S0001, p. 39; S0241, p. 97; S0091†).

**Gaps.** Still missing: the policy texts themselves, and voices of TI's first engineers or the railway clerks.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 From the Asian Games to the 1984 policy

The participant-historian's account traces the policy shift to a sports event. In 1982 Rajiv Gandhi, then the prime minister's son, was put in charge of the Asian Games in Delhi. Rajaraman describes him as 'technically savvy, being an amateur electronics buff'. He decided computers should handle schedules, event records and 'all other clerical chores' (S0001, p. 31). Locally made DCM computers served as terminals linked to a Hewlett-Packard server. The National Informatics Centre's engineers built the software in six months. 'The computerization of the games was a resounding success', and it 'brought N.Seshagiri, the Director General of the NIC, close to Rajiv Gandhi' (S0001, p. 31).

Through the Games, Rajiv Gandhi heard from private manufacturers about 'their difficulties in manufacturing computers due to myriad rules and regulations'. Seshagiri became 'his informal advisor' (S0001, p. 31). After Indira Gandhi's assassination in October 1984, Rajiv became prime minister. On 19 November 1984 the New Computer Policy was announced (S0237†).

Rajaraman lists its main features (S0001, pp. 31-32):
- any manufacturer could make 32-bit machines;
- capacity limits were removed;
- fully assembled boards could be imported;
- application software could be imported at lower duty;
- mainframe imports were simplified.

State firms kept protections:
- superminicomputers and mainframes were reserved for ECIL;
- imported computers had to be maintained by CMC or by the users themselves.

For exporters (S0001, p. 32):
- software was recognised as an 'industry', which opened bank loans and duty exemptions;
- customs duty on computers for software export was cut, having been 'over 100%';
- 'Sending engineers abroad to develop and maintain software for clients at their sites and profits earned thereby was recognized as "software export"'.

The last point legitimised body shopping in law (Ch. 6).

The policy also set a target. According to the World Bank, the government 'announced the Computer Policy Plan in November 1984 and expected software exports to reach US$258 million (or 0.5 percent of the world market) by 1990' (S0241, p. 96). In 1990/91 exports were $128 million by NASSCOM's figures (S0241, p. 100) or $131.2 million by Heeks's (S0085, p. 2). That was about half the target.

The World Bank's verdict: 'policy making and implementation have fundamentally focused on ways to make export figures rise'. The pushing 'has been short-term oriented' (S0241, p. 96).

Myth M21 (that 1984 'cut duties and opened computing') is partly confirmed: duties on computers for export were cut in 1984. But the duty-free regime came in 1986.

### 3.2 The board-import paradox

The 1984 policy's most consequential clause was about circuit boards. Allowing imports of populated boards 'changed manufacturing to assembly and system integration. In other words fully populated boards could be imported which were more reliable and cheaper than boards fabricated using locally manufactured components and imported microprocessors... Thus local manufacturing of boards slowly disappeared' (S0001, p. 32).

Within two years the number of computers rose 100% and prices fell 50% (S0001, p. 40). The 'old-guard derisively called such manufacturing "screw driver" manufacturing' (S0001, p. 40). Sterling sold a PC clone, the Shiva, for Rs 29,000 in 1987 (S0001, p. 41).

Rajaraman's conclusion: self-reliance in hardware 'was truly and completely buried' (S0001, p. 41).

The same liberalisation that made computers cheap enough for software firms ended the local hardware design of 1978-84 (Ch. 5). That trade-off is the hinge between the hardware path and the software path in this book.

### 3.3 1986: duty-free machines, satellite exports, foreign capital

In December 1986 the government adopted the 'Policy on Computer Software Export, Software Development and Training'. The World Bank calls it 'A major reform in computer software' (S0241, p. 106).

Rajaraman summarises its incentives (S0001, p. 32):
- computers for software export could be imported duty-free, but the importer had to 'earn 250% of the cost of the imported computer within 4 years';
- the obligation was 150% where 'no foreign exchange was spent to import a computer (e.g., a non resident Indian bringing a computer with his earnings)';
- tools could be imported duty-free;
- 'Software developed in India could be exported using communication systems such as satellite and cable';
- CMC's Indonet network was available to exporters at a concessional tariff.

Sharma adds that software was brought under the Open General Licence, so new packages and tools 'started becoming available'. But 'the actual import of hardware still took time' (S0240).

The export obligation descends directly from TCS's 1974 Burroughs licence (2x in five years; Ch. 4). The foreign-exchange logic of the 1970s persisted through the liberalisation. Foreign capital was also eased: '100% foreign equity holdings have been permitted since 1986 in export oriented projects, but only 40% foreign equity is allowed for companies producing software for the domestic market' (S0241, p. 98). This was the same 40% that had driven IBM out in 1978 (Ch. 5), now waived for exporters.

### 3.4 Texas Instruments and the satellite dish

The policy change that most altered where work happened concerned telecommunications, not import duties. Rajaraman: 'The DoE negotiated with the Department of Telecommunication (DoT) in 1985 to allow Texas Instruments (TI) to set up a dedicated satellite communication link to its centre at Dallas, USA. TI's centre in Bangalore developed software tools and sent them to its Dallas centre via a dedicated satellite communication link. This was the first step in breaking the monopoly of the DoT in communications and had far reaching consequences later' for offshore centres and the STP programme (S0001, p. 36).

The World Bank adds that TI 'obtained a private Intelsat Business Service earth station so that its programmers can communicate with other TI operations using satellite links'. Its special approvals 'helped make India more attractive' to HP, DEC, British Telecom, Philips and Westinghouse (S0241, p. 98).

TI's own 2005 milestone list claims that in 1985 it became 'the first multinational to establish a software design center in India and the first to install and use a satellite dish for exporting software' (S0061). Heeks dates the start of operations to 1986 and calls it 'the first proper offshore software facility'. He adds that 'TI was known to have cut through many red tape barriers, particularly on imports' (S0085, p. 13).

The two dates can be reconciled: TI arrived in 1985 and its satellite link was working by 1986 (dispute (w)). The 'first offshore' claim is the subject of myth M01. The defensible form is TI's own: the first multinational design centre, and the first private satellite link for software.

The arrival is the chapter's best-documented scene. Janardhan Roye, whose office was in the same building, recalled in 2013: 'On a memorable day in 1985, my Ambassador car got stuck behind a heavily laden bullock-cart outside the office on Miller Road. The cart was negotiating a steep incline at the gate leading to the office complex, Sona Towers' (S0249). The cargo was TI's satellite equipment. Among the onlookers, Roye recalls, was Sam Pitroda, whose C-DOT office was a floor above, who said: 'This can happen only in India' (S0249). Roye calls the '64 kbps dish antenna' 'like a whale surfacing' (S0249).

The building's owner, the Sona Group, tells the same bullock-cart story (S0250). It is an interested party, but it corroborates the cart. The Pitroda quotation is a 28-year-old memory of a remark, and the book must present it as Roye's recollection.

TI's work in Bangalore was mainly design tools and, from 1989, mixed-signal chip design (S0061). It was not business-software services. TI showed that India could host high-skill design work for a multinational, which complicates the body-shopping story of Chapter 6.

### 3.5 Telecoms: the binding constraint

Telecommunications, more than computers, decided whether work could stay in India. Rajaraman: 'In 1982 there was a waiting time of over 2 years to get a land line telephone connection' (S0001, p. 33). Murthy's recollection was longer (Ch. 6).

Two reforms followed (S0001, p. 33):
- MTNL was created to run Bombay and Delhi telephones with 'operational autonomy';
- VSNL was set up in 1986 for overseas communication.

C-DOT, set up in 1984 under Sam Pitroda, built a rugged digital exchange for rural India 'in a record time of 3 years'. Microprocessor-billed public call offices spread so fast that 'Every nook and corner of India was connected within 5 years. The power of microcomputer was visible to all' (S0001, p. 33).

For software exporters, data links stayed costly. STPI's own history records 'a substantial cost involved in the data communication links', with gateway equipment owned and run by VSNL (S0091†). The World Bank in 1994 still listed 'local communications links are poor' among the industry's institutional weaknesses (S0241, p. 96). It argued that better data links would reduce 'dependence on body-shopping (on-site delivery of services) and the consequent brain-drain' (S0241, p. 28).

### 3.6 The railway counters and the unions

The labour resistance of Chapter 3 continued into the 1980s. When the Reserve Bank's Rangarajan committee recommended that banks computerise their back offices on Unix machines, 'The increase in computer use by banks was a red flag to the trade unions and they observed 1984 as "anti-computerization year"' (S0001, p. 36). Dataquest's retrospective names the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh and an 'Anti-Computer Day' on 1 March 1984 (S0237†). A labour-archive biography of a bank union leader records that a union 'reached the first ever agreement on computerisation' in 1983 (S0251). The union's name is garbled in the scan.

The turn came from the railways:
- In 1984 they carried 'over 5 million passengers travelling in over 600 long distance trains with around 50,000 reservation requests', and 'Clerks kept numerous ledgers, one for each train' (S0001, p. 38).
- CMC proposed a computerised system. 'by 1986 a reservation system was developed and implemented at the New Delhi booking office. It had 50 counters and customers could go to any counter' (S0001, p. 38).
- 'This was perceived in 1986 by the general public as almost a miracle', with confirmed reservations in 30 minutes.
- Clerks could reconcile cash 'within 30 minutes after closing time whereas in the manual system it used to take at least two hours' (S0001, p. 38).
- **The labour finding:** 'There was also no reduction of staff; in fact there was an increase as more counters were operated with more clerks working simultaneously' (S0001, p. 38).

The system ran on a VAX cluster and was written in FORTRAN. 'The entire software effort was by Indian software engineers with no involvement of foreign consultants' (S0001, p. 38). Rajaraman's claim that it was 'the first time in the world' such a system ran on a VAX cluster is unverified and should not be repeated without a source.

His conclusion: 'There was an attitudinal change among both the general public and the white collar workers about computerization', and some bank unions then accepted computerisation they had opposed (S0001, p. 38). A pilot in November 1985 is reported in secondary sources; the system was live at New Delhi in 1986 (dispute (g)).

The episode matters for the book's AI question. In 1986 a visible computer system increased clerical jobs at the counter, because demand was suppressed by queues. The labour debate of the 1960s and 1980s turned on whether that would hold.

### 3.7 Cray, C-DAC and PARAM

The supercomputer story is told with more certainty in popular sources than the record supports (myth M05):
- **IISc.** The Ministry of Human Resource Development gave IISc Rs 500 million in 1984 for a Supercomputer Education and Research Centre. A national committee chose a Cray Y-MP. 'Cray was confident of getting a licence', and a US State Department team negotiated conditions of use. But 'The US government did not give an export licence to Cray before the date specified in the contract signed by the IISc with Cray'. The order was cancelled (S0001, pp. 36-37). Rajaraman chaired SERC from 1982 to 1994 and 'participated in the entire exercise'. IISc bought a CDC Cyber 992, other mainframes and an IBM workstation cluster instead: 'The denial of Cray was a blessing in disguise' (S0001, p. 37).
- **Weather forecasting.** A separate sale for monsoon forecasting was debated in the American press in December 1986 (S0079). A Cray X-MP/14 was imported in October 1988 for the National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting (S0078, parliamentary answers, located but not read).

So India was both denied one Cray and sold another (dispute (aa)).

C-DAC's origin is documented by its founding committee's chairman. In 1986 the Science Advisory Committee to the Prime Minister set up a committee under Rajaraman 'as a consequence of the difficulties encountered by India in buying supercomputers from the USA and Japan'. It proposed 'a mission mode project to build parallel computers with a speed of a giga-flop or above' (S0001, p. 37). C-DAC was established at Pune in 1988 with Rs 300 million; 'The project was successfully completed in 1991' (S0001, p. 37).

Other parallel machines were built at the same time: FLOSOLVER at NAL, PACE at DRDO, ANUPAM at BARC (S0001, pp. 37-38). The popular 'Bhatkar told Rajiv Gandhi' version (S0080) is low-reliability and should be used only as a myth to examine.

### 3.7a Networks and visible machines

Three other state projects of these years built capability and public familiarity more than export revenue.

**ERNET.** In 1986 the DoE obtained '$6 million spread over 4 years from the UNDP', with Rs 41 million of Indian money, for Education and Research in Computer Networks (S0001, pp. 38-39). The five IITs, IISc, NCST and the DoE were linked 'for exchange of email and other documents'. The project trained people in networking. 'ERNET also brought the Internet to India under the leadership of S.Ramani of NCST, Mumbai' (S0001, p. 39). After the project ended it became the Internet provider for India's education and research institutions (S0001, p. 39). Many of the engineers who later built the export industry's data links learned networking here. That is an inference to test, not a sourced fact.

**Voting machines.** In 1982 a by-election in Kerala used 'microprocessor based electronic voting machines (for the first time probably in the world)', designed by the public-sector firms BEL and ECIL 'for easy use by the illiterate electorate' (S0001, p. 39). The 'first in the world' claim is Rajaraman's and should not be repeated without a check.

**NICNET.** The National Informatics Centre ran a satellite-based data network for government, operating, the World Bank said in 1994, 'for the past six years or so'. The Bank's view of NIC was critical: it called it 'Yet another hindrance', arguing that NIC's in-house projects for government 'created barriers to entry' for private software firms in the domestic market (S0241, p. 38).

Rajaraman's judgement on the decade's visible projects: 'The effectiveness of computers in three highly visible projects, namely, the reservation of train tickets, Public Call Offices, and computerized electronic voting, changed the perception of policy makers and the general public about computers and their relevance to India' (S0001, p. 39). For a book about a profession, the point is that by 1990 computing in India was no longer only a foreign-exchange question. It was also a public service the voter could see.

### 3.8 Software Technology Parks

The parks generalised TI's privilege. Their purpose was to give small and medium firms 'an opportunity to enter the international software export market' (S0241, p. 96). They offered (S0001, p. 39; S0091†):
- 'buildings, work stations and continuous uninterrupted power supply';
- satellite links through which firms could 'develop software on the computers of their overseas customers from access terminals located in their respective premises';
- 'single window' clearances under a 100% export-oriented-unit scheme.

Rajaraman: 'As the investment required to set up a company in STP was low, this initiative allowed many small entrepreneurs to enter the software services export business' (S0001, p. 39).

The dates are now settled (dispute (j)):
- The World Bank lists seven parks: Pune (August 1990), Bangalore (October 1990), Bhubaneswar (December 1990), Hyderabad (February 1991), Thiruvananthapuram, Gandhinagar and Noida (July 1991) (S0241, p. 97).
- STPI says three parks were set up as autonomous societies in 1990, and STPI was registered on 5 June 1991 (S0091†).
- Rajaraman's 'The first STP was established in Bangalore in 1990' (S0001, p. 39) is true for Bangalore but not for 'first'. Pune came two months earlier by the World Bank's dates.

About 160 companies had been approved by 1994, with estimated exports of '$7.28 million by 1994'. The World Bank thought 'it may be too early to judge' (S0241, p. 97).

Rajaraman credits N. Vittal, the IAS officer who took over the DoE in 1990, with being 'instrumental in setting up STPs and obtaining a host of fiscal concessions to the software industry by persuading the bureaucrats of the Ministry of Finance' (S0001, p. 42). The STPs belong to this chapter's end and Chapter 9's beginning.

### 3.9 NASSCOM and the lobby

The industry organised in this period. The Manufacturers' Association for Information Technology formed in 1982. NASSCOM was founded in 1988 with 38 members and grew to 464 by 1999 (S0001, p. 42). Rajaraman calls NASSCOM 'fortunate in employing Dewang Mehta as its President in 1991. Mehta was a superb lobbyist' (S0001, p. 42). The founding is otherwise documented only in insiders' memoirs (S0059, S0060), not read. NASSCOM's figures used elsewhere in the book (S0241, Annex 2) are lobby data and should be labelled.

### 3.10 Interpretations

- **'Rajiv's revolution' (M04).** The 1984 and 1986 policies came under Rajiv Gandhi, and Rajaraman credits his exposure through the Asian Games (S0001, p. 31). But exports reached only half the 1984 target by 1990 (S0241, p. 96).
- **Infrastructure reading.** The decisive changes were telecoms and the STPs, which let work stay in India: TI's link, VSNL, the parks. They came in pieces between 1985 and 1991 (S0001, pp. 33, 36, 39; S0241, p. 28).
- **Policy-as-exports reading (World Bank).** Policy aimed at export numbers, not capabilities. It 'pushed firms into exporting' in a 'short-term oriented' way (S0241, p. 96).

### 3.11 Education and the profession

Several of the chapter's state projects trained people more than they produced machines. The KBCS, CAD and ERNET programmes sent 'A large number of scientists' abroad and created pools of engineers in AI, chip design and networking (S0001, pp. 35-39). C-DAC and the parallel-computer projects 'generated a large pool of engineers adept in designing and programming parallel computers' (S0001, p. 38). TI's centre trained designers who, by 2005, had filed close to 300 patent applications (S0061; a company figure).

The railway project showed Indian engineers building a large transaction system at home (S0001, p. 38). The profession of this period was split. Programmers sent abroad on time-based contracts (Ch. 6) on one side; engineers on state and multinational design projects at home on the other.
