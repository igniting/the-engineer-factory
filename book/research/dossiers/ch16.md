## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**The confession.** On 7 January 2009 B. Ramalinga Raju wrote to Satyam's board that its balance sheet carried 'Inflated (non-existent) cash and bank balances of Rs.5,040 crore'. The latest quarter's 'operating margin of Rs. 649 crore (24% of revenues)' was really 'Rs. 61 Crore (3% of revenues)'. 'It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten' (S0122). A day earlier Merrill Lynch had dropped its engagement over 'material accounting irregularities' (S0279).

**The rescue.** Within two days the government suspended the board (S0279). Within three months a new board sold control to Tech Mahindra at Rs 58 a share (S0279). In 2011 the SEC fined Satyam $10 million. It fined PwC's Indian affiliates $6 million, 'the largest ever by a foreign-based accounting firm', for letting clients control their own cash confirmations (S0280). SEBI's findings against the founders were upheld on appeal (S0123), and a CBI court convicted Raju and others in April 2015 (S0123).

**The employees.** The 53,000 staff faced unpaid salaries and closed doors. NASSCOM and Infosys discouraged hiring them. A 22-year-old graduate, N. Kavitha, still awaited a joining date promised for September 2008 (S0281). The engineering survived the fraud; the governance did not.

**Aadhaar.** The state then borrowed the industry's methods and one of its founders. UIDAI was set up by notification on 28 January 2009. Infosys co-founder Nandan Nilekani became its chairman with Cabinet rank in July 2009 (S0127). By 2016 about 100 crore Aadhaar numbers had been generated (S0127, dissent).

**The courts.** In 2017 nine judges held privacy a fundamental right. In 2018 the Court upheld Aadhaar but struck down data retention beyond six months, compulsory bank and mobile linking, and private use under contract (S0127). The dissent's warning, that rights 'cannot be based on algorithms or probabilities', looks ahead to the AI chapters.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.0 The company on paper

Satyam's last annual report before the confession, its Form 20-F for the year to March 2008, filed with the SEC in August 2008, shows what investors and employees believed. 'As of March 31, 2008, we had 50,570 employees, whom we refer to as associates, worldwide as compared to 39,018 associates as of March 31, 2007', of whom 47,405 were technical staff in IT services (S0283). 'Our total revenues for fiscal 2008 were $2,138.1 million and over the past three fiscal years our revenues have grown at a compound annual growth rate of 39.1%' (S0283). It reported 654 active customers, 'including 185 Fortune Global 500 or Fortune U.S. 500' companies (S0283).

The balance sheet is where the fraud lay. The 20-F reported 'cash and cash equivalents of $290.5 million' at March 2008. It explained that cash used in investing in fiscal 2007 had risen 'primarily due to investment in bank deposits amounting to $745.6 million' (S0283). The filing carried the consent of Price Waterhouse as the independent registered public accounting firm (S0283). Raju's letter five months later said that cash and bank balances of Rs 5,361 crore in the books included Rs 5,040 crore that did not exist (S0122).

Two points follow for the drafting. First, the employee and customer numbers in the 20-F are close to those in Raju's letter (53,000 people; 185 Fortune 500 customers) and in the board's later statements. The business was real; the cash was not. Second, the deposits that the auditors were later found not to have independently confirmed (S0280) were the large balance-sheet items reported as bank deposits. The SEC's finding that PW India 'routinely relinquished control of the delivery and receipt of cash confirmations entirely to their audit clients' (S0280) explains how a fiction on that scale survived several annual audits. A company growing at nearly 40% a year, hiring more than 11,000 people in a year, and reporting margins close to those of its rivals did not look unusual from outside. That was the problem.

### 3.1 The letter

Satyam Computer Services, founded and run by B. Ramalinga Raju, was one of India's four largest software firms and the parent of the Nasdaq-listed Satyam Infoway (Chapter 11). On 7 January 2009 it filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission a letter from Raju to its board. The SEC copy is the version to use, because press reproductions differ in small ways (S0122; the register notes 'accrued' and 'acquired' interest).

The letter begins: 'It is with deep regret, and tremendous burden that I am carrying on my conscience, that I would like to bring the following facts to your notice' (S0122). Its first list is short and devastating. The balance sheet of 30 September 2008 carried:
- 'Inflated (non-existent) cash and bank balances of Rs.5,040 crore (as against Rs. 5361 crore reflected in the books)';
- 'An accrued interest of Rs. 376 crore which is non-existent';
- 'An understated liability of Rs. 1,230 crore on account of funds arranged by me';
- 'An over stated debtors position of Rs. 490 crore (as against Rs. 2651 reflected in the books)'.

For the September quarter the company had reported 'a revenue of Rs.2,700 crore and an operating margin of Rs. 649 crore (24% 0f revenues) as against the actual revenues of Rs. 2,112 crore and an actual operating margin of Rs. 61 Crore ( 3% of revenues)' (S0122).

Raju's account of how this happened is the most quoted passage: 'What started as a marginal gap between actual operating profit and the one reflected in the books of accounts continued to grow over the years. It has attained unmanageable proportions as the size of company operations grew significantly.' The company 'had to carry additional resources and assets to justify higher level of operations - thereby significantly increasing the costs'. 'It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten.' The aborted purchase of Maytas, a construction company controlled by his family, 'was the last attempt to fill the fictitious assets with real ones' (S0122).

He then defended himself. He claimed that neither he nor the managing director, his brother, had sold shares in eight years except for philanthropy, and that 'neither me, nor the Managing Director took even one rupee/dollar from the company'. He said Rs 1,230 crore had been 'arranged to Satyam ... by resorting to pledging all the promoter shares', and that 'Every attempt was made to keep the wheel moving and to ensure prompt payment of salaries to the associates.' He claimed that 'None of the board members, past or present, had any knowledge of the situation', and named senior executives who, he said, were also unaware (S0122). These are his claims. SEBI and the courts later found that he and others had made illegal gains (S0123), and his lawyers later disputed that he wrote the letter (register; new dispute ss). The drafting should quote the letter as the document filed with the SEC and note both points.

He ended with the scale of what he had built: 'I have seen it grow from few people to 53,000 people, with 185 Fortune 500 companies as customers and operations in 66 countries.' 'I sincerely apologize to all Satyamites and stakeholders' (S0122).

### 3.2 Two days

The same SEC filing contains the evidence that someone outside had already seen the problem. DSP Merrill Lynch wrote to the chairman of SEBI on 7 January that it had terminated its advisory engagement with Satyam the day before: 'In the course of such engagement, we came to understand that there were material accounting irregularities, which prompted our aforesaid decision' (S0279). Raju's letter suggested that Merrill Lynch 'can be entrusted with the task of quickly exploring some Merger opportunities' (S0122). It had already left.

The company's own release that day quoted the interim chief executive, Ram Mynampati: 'We are obviously shocked by the contents of the letter.' It promised 'to protect the careers and security of its approximately 53,000 associates' (S0279).

The state moved fast. On the afternoon of 8 January the Registrar of Companies petitioned the Special Judge for Economic Offences in Hyderabad, 'at 04.30 PM at my residence', for permission to search and seize Satyam's books (S0279). SEBI appointed an investigating authority. On 9 January the government announced it would nominate up to ten directors and suspended the board. The Company Law Board issued its order in *Union of India v. Satyam Computer Services* the same day (S0279). Satyam welcomed the 'landmark decision', which it said would restore 'the confidence of 53,000 employees and their families, 300,000 shareholders and 650 customers across the globe' (S0279). The SEC later summarised: 'the India government seized control of the company by dissolving its board of directors and appointing new government-nominated directors' (S0280). For a government that had spent two decades withdrawing from the economy, it was an unusual step, and it was taken because of the 53,000 jobs and the industry's reputation abroad.

### 3.3 The employees

The people who had done the work found themselves stranded. *Business Today* reported in February 2009 that a European client had moved a project, with its team, to another Indian vendor, and that '95 per cent of the team had agreed to join the competing vendor' (S0281). A Hyderabad recruiter, T. Sreedhar, described employees as 'stuck between the devil and the deep sea. They cannot quit the job because there is no option being made available by the large companies (who have chosen not to hire them) and while staying back, they are not certain if they will get the salaries due to them this month'. NASSCOM and Infosys 'issued statements against recruiting people from Satyam, not because they are tainted but to ensure that the company doesn't suffer because of an exodus' (S0281). A recruiter's estimate put 70% of the workforce in the job market. Insiders said about 7,000 employees had signed two-year contracts with 'Rs 2 lakh bonds'. Satyam had issued 'about 6,000 offer letters in campuses' in 2007, and BT judged 'Those offers are not worth the paper they are written on' (S0281).

The magazine printed short first-person accounts, 'as told to' its reporters. N. Kavitha, 22, an engineering graduate from a college near Chennai, had been selected on campus in 2007-08: 'I was told that I would get a joining letter by September 2008. This did not happen. The company HR said that I could definitely expect to join in January 2009.' She would still join if asked: 'In this recession, a fresher needs to grab any opportunity. I am not worried too much about Satyam's reputation in the market; I want the work experience first' (S0281). S. Mastanamma, 24, two years at the Chennai centre after an engineering degree in Guntur, was 'very positive about the company's future' and 'not worried about lay-offs' (S0281). An anonymous consultant in Europe said: 'We, as employees, are very clear we want to save our livelihoods', and 'Raju was always elusive, I have never seen or heard from him in the two years I have been with the firm' (S0281). A marketing head, Kulwinder Singh, recalled that in three and a half years his salary had never been late (S0281).

A second *Business Today* piece two weeks later is presented as 'an imaginary briefing of the new directors, by one of them, based on real interactions' (S0282). It is a composite and must not be quoted as anyone's words. Only its factual note is usable: 'As BT went to print, it learnt that employees had started getting their pay credited into their accounts' (S0282). A claim there that a prosecutor had alleged 13,000 fictitious employees needs a primary source before use (new dispute tt).

Kavitha's position echoes the 2001-02 campus collapse (Chapter 11) and the deferred joiners of 2009 at other firms (Chapter 14). The factory's newest products bore the risk first.

### 3.4 Rescue, auditors and punishment

The government-appointed board ran an auction under the supervision of a former Chief Justice, S.P. Bharucha. On 13 April 2009 it announced that Venturbay Consultants, controlled by Tech Mahindra, was the highest bidder. The financial bids were 'Rs. 58 (Tech Mahindra), Rs. 45.90 (Larsen & Toubro Limited) and Rs. 20 (consortium of WL Ross funds) per share' (S0279). The chairman of the new board, Kiran Karnik, a former NASSCOM president, said the result 'signals a new stage for the Company in its progress towards stabilization and growth'. He claimed that customers 'continue to be happy with Satyam's excellent service delivery' (S0279). The process 'reached this significant stage within three months of the new Board's first meeting' (S0279).

That speed is the point the chapter should draw. The fraud was in the accounts, not the delivery. Clients, projects and engineers had value that a rival would pay for within weeks. The asset of an Indian services firm was its people and its client relationships. Its weakness was the governance of a founder-controlled company and the audit that should have checked it.

On the audit, the SEC's findings of April 2011 are blunt. Five Indian affiliates of PricewaterhouseCoopers paid 'a $6 million penalty, the largest ever by a foreign-based accounting firm in an SEC enforcement action'. They were barred from new US clients for six months, and two of them paid the PCAOB a further $1.5 million (S0280). The SEC found that PW India staff 'failed to conduct procedures to confirm Satyam's cash and cash equivalent balances or its accounts receivables'. They 'routinely relinquished control of the delivery and receipt of cash confirmations entirely to their audit clients and rarely, if ever, questioned the integrity of the confirmation responses' (S0280). The SEC's enforcement director, Robert Khuzami: 'PW India violated its most fundamental duty as a public watchdog'. Satyam itself settled fraud charges with a $10 million penalty (S0280).

In India, SEBI's order of 15 July 2014 held the Raju brothers and others liable under the securities laws. The Securities Appellate Tribunal upheld its findings 'on merits' on 12 May 2017 but sent back the amounts and periods of debarment. A fresh order of 16 October 2018 recomputed the gains of three former officials and debarred two of them for seven years, with interest at 12% a year from 7 January 2009 (S0123). That order refers to the 'order of CBI Special Court dated April 09, 2015', which convicted Raju and others; the register's press source gives seven years' rigorous imprisonment (S0124; judgment not read).

**Myth M27 ('Satyam was India's first big corporate-governance failure').** Not tested. Earlier scandals (the 1992 securities scam, the 2001 Ketan Parekh affair) need sources. The safe claims are that it was the largest accounting fraud in Indian IT, that it led to a record SEC penalty on a foreign audit firm (S0280), and that the government took the unusual step of replacing a private company's board (S0279).

### 3.5 Aadhaar: the state borrows the factory

In the same month as Raju's letter the Indian state began building the largest project ever run by people from the industry. The Supreme Court's 2018 judgment gives the official history. An Empowered Group of Ministers had considered a unique identity scheme. At its fourth meeting, on 22 December 2006, 'The numbering format of 11 digits was approved', and the need for an authority 'created by an executive order under the aegis of the Planning Commission' was accepted (S0127, Sikri J., p. 10). 'UIDAI was established in the year 2009 by an administrative order i.e. by resolution of the Govt. of India, Planning Commission, vide notification dated January 28, 2009' (S0127, p. 25). By 'Notification dated July 02, 2009 ... Mr. Nandan Nilekani was appointed as the Chairman of UIDAI for an initial tenure of five years in the rank and status of a Cabinet Minister. He assumed charge on July 24, 2009' (S0127, p. 13).

Nilekani had co-founded Infosys in 1981 and was its chief executive from 2002 to 2007 (Chapter 6; chronology). His appointment marks the moment the industry's managerial methods were turned inward, on the Indian state's own problem of identifying its residents. The first numbers were issued on 29 September 2010 at Tembhli, a village in Nandurbar district, Maharashtra (S0128, register; the audit report could not be retrieved). Justice Chandrachud's dissent records that by 29 September 2011 'the Aadhaar project completed one year. An announcement was made of the generation of ten crore enrolments and of more than 3.75 crore Aadhaar numbers'. Citing the Press Information Bureau, it says that by the time the Aadhaar Act was notified, 'UIDAI had generated about 100 crore Aadhaar numbers' (S0127, dissent).

The legal basis came late. For seven years Aadhaar rested on an executive notification. The Aadhaar Act of 2016 was passed as a Money Bill, which let the government avoid the upper house. The petitioners argued that this was 'colourable' and the Act should be struck down (S0127, p. 94). The majority disagreed; Chandrachud J. agreed with the petitioners in dissent.

The people who built it are not yet documented in the sources in hand. Nilekani's own account (S0126) and the role of engineers from private firms and volunteer networks need sources before the chapter can name them. The Court also noted that Nilekani chaired a task force on an IT strategy for the public distribution system, mentioned in an order of 16 March 2012 (S0127, Sikri J., p. 24).

### 3.6 The limits: privacy and the 2018 judgment

The challenge to Aadhaar produced two of the most important judgments in Indian constitutional law. On 24 August 2017 a bench of nine judges unanimously overruled the earlier decisions in *M P Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* insofar as they denied a right to privacy. It held: 'The right to privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and as a part of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution' (S0127, 2017, order of the Court).

The judges wrote several separate opinions (signature blocks in the judgment include Chandrachud, Chelameswar, Bobde, Nariman, Sapre and Kaul JJ.; the exact grouping is to be confirmed), but the order of the Court states the holding for all nine, so it is unanimous. It is the foundation on which the 2018 Aadhaar challenge, and any later challenge to data-driven or algorithmic systems run by the state, has to be argued. The case began as a challenge to Aadhaar itself, filed in 2012 by Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) as Writ Petition (Civil) No. 494 of 2012 (S0127).

On 26 September 2018 a five-judge bench upheld the Aadhaar Act but cut it back (S0127, Sikri J. for the majority):
- **Data retention.** 'Retention of data beyond the period of six months is impermissible. Therefore, Regulation 27 of Aadhaar (Authentication) Regulations, 2016 which provides archiving a data for a period of five years is struck down' (p. 558).
- **Private use.** 'some portion of Section 57' was declared 'unconstitutional' (p. 282). Section 57 had allowed 'any body corporate or person, pursuant to any law ... or any contract' to use Aadhaar for identification.
- **Bank accounts.** The rule requiring Aadhaar linking of bank accounts 'does not meet the test of proportionality and is also violative of right to privacy of a person which extends to banking details' (p. 512).
- **Mobile numbers.** The telecoms department's circular of 23 March 2017 'mandating linking of mobile number with Aadhaar is held to be illegal and unconstitutional as it is not backed by any law and is hereby quashed' (p. 567).
- **National security disclosure.** Section 33(2) was struck down.

Justice Chandrachud's dissent would have struck down the Act. Its most quoted lines are directly relevant to the book's final part: 'Dignity and rights of individuals cannot be based on algorithms or probabilities. Constitutional guarantees cannot be subject to the vicissitudes of technology' (S0127, dissent; paragraph to confirm).

For the book's argument, the significance is a change of direction. For thirty years Indian software engineers had mostly built systems for foreign clients under foreign law. Aadhaar was a system built by Indians for the Indian state, applied to Indians. The Supreme Court's limits on retention, linking and private use were the first time Indian institutions had to decide what Indian software could be made to do to Indian citizens. The AI chapters return to this question, with models in place of databases.
