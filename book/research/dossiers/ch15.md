**Chapter question.** What did multinational companies' own Indian centres add to the Indian engineering profession, and how did they change from outposts in the 1980s to the 'global capability centres' of the 2020s?

---

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Two industries.** Beside the Indian services firms grew a second employer class: the Indian centres of foreign companies.

**The first wave.**
- TI came in 1985 as, by its own account, 'the first multinational to establish a software design center in India' (S0061), and moved into chip design.
- HP's centre had 400 people by 1989 (S0097).
- American Express and GE built captive back offices (S0206).
- Microsoft announced a 25-person Hyderabad centre in 1998 as 'an extension to our product team in Redmond' (S0195).
- In 2004 Google's founders said Bangalore would run 'a mirror exactly' of US development (S0200).

**The boundary was porous.** Cognizant began in 1994 as Dun & Bradstreet's in-house centre in India. By 1998 74% of its technical staff were there (S0274), and by 2024, 241,500 of its 336,800 employees (S0192). Accenture expected more staff in India than in the US by August 2007 (S0193). IBM, which left India in 1978, had 53,000 staff there by early 2007 (S0194).

**Counts.** Zinnov counted 594 multinational R&D centres with 146,760 staff in 2008 (S0207†). By FY2024, by Zinnov and NASSCOM's count, more than 1,700 'GCCs' employed over 1.9 million (S0141†), perhaps a third of India's tech workforce. These are consultant and lobby figures.

**Myth M25.** The GCC is not new; the label and the scale are.

**The circle closes.** By 2024 Cognizant, born a captive, named clients' GCCs as competitors for both work and staff, 'a lower cost alternative to our services' (S0192).

**Gap.** Whether the other employers offered a different profession (pay, ladders, product rather than service work) is plausible but thinly evidenced. Captive call centres offered longer career paths and lower turnover than third-party ones (S0278). No pay data and no engineer's own account are in hand.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The outposts

The first foreign technology centres in India were small, specialised and run by people with one foot in each country. Texas Instruments describes its own start plainly: '1985 TI begins operation in India as the first multinational to establish a software design center in India and the first to install and use a satellite dish for exporting software' (S0061). The claim of being first is the company's. Its own milestone list shows the centre moving steadily up the technical ladder: a design centre for mixed-signal integrated circuits in 1989; design of 'application specific products' from 1990; disk-drive signal-processing cores in 1995; 3G and wireless LAN chipsets in 2000; and a new seven-acre Bangalore facility in 2004 (S0061). This was not services work billed by the hour. It was engineering for a product company's own products, done in India. The story of how TI got its satellite link, by 'removing or breaking 25 different government rules', is told in Chapters 8 and 10 (S0255, p. 6).

Hewlett-Packard followed through a returning engineer. Radha Basu, who had left for graduate study in California in the early 1970s, spent four years from the mid-1980s setting up HP's software centre in Bangalore. When she returned to Silicon Valley in 1989 it employed 400 people (S0097, pp. 62-63; Chapter 13). Motorola's Bangalore centre claimed the world's first CMM Level 5 rating in 1993, a company claim discussed in Chapter 11 (S0199). American Express and GE built captive back offices in the late 1980s and 1990s, the start of the BPO industry (S0206; Chapter 14). Oracle (1994), SAP Labs (1998) and Intel (1999) set up development centres in the same decade. Their dates are in the register, but the primary sources have not yet been read (S0196, S0197, S0198).

The Microsoft announcement of 24 March 1998 shows how cautiously a product company approached India at the end of the 1990s (S0195). It announced 'plans to establish a software development center in Hyderabad', 'Subject to clearance by regulatory agencies', to work on 'interoperability of the Microsoft Windows NT operating system and the BackOffice family with non-Microsoft platforms'. 'The development center initially will employ about 25 people.' The group vice-president, Paul Maritz, was careful about its status: 'The majority of our development work will always be based in the United States, but we have a great deal of faith in the IT skills of India. Although this center is initially relatively small, the work will be highly valued and strategically important.' Another vice-president, Moshe Dunie, described it as 'an extension to our product team in Redmond'. Its first general manager, Srini Koppolu, had been at Microsoft for more than nine years and relocated from Redmond. The decision followed 'a long evaluation process that began over a year ago' (S0195). The pattern of a returning Indian-born engineer running a small, subordinate team doing a defined slice of product work recurs across the first wave.

### 3.2 A captive becomes a vendor

The most revealing single case is a company usually counted among the services firms. Cognizant's 1998 prospectus states: 'The Company began its software development and maintenance services business in early 1994 as an in-house technology development center for The Dun & Bradstreet Corporation and its operating units' (S0274). In November 1996 D&B spun off a group of businesses, including this one, to form Cognizant Corporation. The technology unit then sold its services to outside clients and went public in June 1998.

At 31 March 1998 Cognizant employed 'approximately 270 persons' in North America, '15 of whom are United States citizens or permanent residents', about 30 in Europe, and 'approximately 870 persons on a full-time basis in its offshore software development centers in India', where about 74% of its technical professionals worked (S0274). It explained its model in the same terms as the Indian firms: 'By basing its technical operations in India, the Company has access to a large pool of skilled, English-speaking IT professionals with which to service customers on a cost basis significantly lower than in developed countries' (S0274). It also warned that 'wages in India are increasing at a faster rate than in the United States' (S0274).

The numbers then grew like those of Infosys or Wipro:
- **31 December 2002:** about 1,425 people in North America, 240 in Europe and 'approximately 4,500 persons ... in its offshore IT development centers in India' (S0275).
- **End of 2007:** about 55,400, of whom about 9,800 were in the Americas (S0276).
- **End of 2012:** about 124,100 in Asia Pacific out of 156,700 (S0277).
- **End of 2024:** 'approximately 336,800 employees ... with 241,500 in India, 42,800 in North America, 15,700 in Continental Europe, 8,200 in the United Kingdom' (S0192).

It was the largest single sponsor of H-1B petitions in 2012 (S0254, Table 1).

The lesson for the book is that the line between a foreign company's captive centre and an 'Indian' services firm was never fixed. A captive could be spun out to sell to others. A services firm could be bought by a client (IBM bought the BPO firm Daksh; S0278, p. 14). A multinational consultancy could become, by headcount, an Indian company. What stayed constant was the work: engineers in India, managed from and billing to clients abroad.

### 3.3 The multinationals move in at scale

In the 2000s the multinationals' Indian workforces grew to the size of the Indian firms. In January 2007 Accenture's chief executive, William D. Green, told reporters in Bangalore that the company would increase its Indian staff to 35,000 by 31 August, 'up 52 percent', giving it 'more employees there than in the U.S., where it will have just over 30,000' (S0193). Most would work in the firm's 'Global Delivery Network' of about 65,000, out of a total headcount of 160,000. 'There is more outsourcing work than we can do,' Green said (S0193).

Three days later *InformationWeek* reported that IBM's 'Indian workforce now stands at 53,000, up from 39,000 a year ago and 23,000 two years ago', with more than 70,000 expected in 2007, 'almost 25% of its current worldwide workforce of 300,000' (S0194). 'IBM workers in India are engaged in everything from routine coding to high-end system design.' The company had consolidated 'all development of software for service-oriented architectures' in Bangalore (S0194). The article is dated 1 February 2007, not 2006 as the register had it (new dispute pp, resolved). For a company that had shut its Indian operations in 1978 rather than accept Indian equity (Chapter 5), the reversal is a scene in itself.

Product companies came for engineers rather than for services. In October 2004 Google's founders visited Bangalore, Hyderabad and Delhi. Sergey Brin said: 'One approach we are taking is that Bangalore is where we run a mirror exactly of what we have in the United States in terms of development.' Researchers there would 'conceptualize new services and develop software', including search by speech and drawings, search in more languages, personalisation and features for Gmail (S0200). Brin added that Google had been '"too late" in tapping Indian talent. "We would have preferred to do it sooner"' (S0200).

The consultancy Zinnov produced the first aggregate count. As reported in *EE Times* in March 2008: 'There are an estimated 594 multinational R&D centers in India employing 146,760 workers.' They were concentrated in Bangalore (312), then Pune (96), the Delhi area (87), Hyderabad (55) and Chennai (39). They focused on telecoms and networking (115), semiconductors and design tools (75) and consumer electronics (48) (S0207†). The headline was 'India's R&D drive slows': 'Ninety-three new centers were established in 2004, but that total dropped to just 24 in 2007', and costs and attrition (8-20% a year) were rising (S0207†). These are a consultant's figures, and the summariser-read wording must be checked.

### 3.4 Captives and careers

Was work at the other employers different? The evidence in hand is mostly indirect. Brin's 'mirror' (S0200), TI's chip design (S0061) and IBM's 'high-end system design' (S0194) describe product engineering rather than client services. An executive at a US multinational's offshore centre told Saxenian's sources in 1996: 'Technologically, there's zero difference' between the work in India and in the US (S0255, p. 11; anonymous, second-hand). TI reports that *Business World* named its Indian unit the 'Best Company to Work for in India' in 2003 (S0061).

The one comparative study in hand concerns back offices. Kuruvilla and Ranganathan, writing from fieldwork in 2004-07, distinguished 'captives' ('BPO firms owned by U.S. client firms such as Dell, EDS, and HP that service only their owners') from 'third-party firms' serving many clients, and noted that consolidation was 'eroding the distinction' (S0278, manuscript p. 14). Turnover in five captive firms was 15-20%, 'although even some captives reported rates as high as 30%'. A survey of seven large third-party call centres found an average of 30%, ranging from 12% to 62% (S0278, p. 19, citing Remesh 2004). On careers: 'captive call centers are able to provide more promotions to higher levels than "third-party" call centers, because the market stability they enjoy fosters longer career paths' (S0278, p. 21). The manuscript is a pre-publication version containing an unfilled placeholder, so its pages and wording must be checked against the published article.

A pay premium at multinational centres is widely asserted but not documented in the sources in hand. The chapter must not claim one until compensation survey data or a study is found.

### 3.5 One labour market

However different their work, the multinational centres and the Indian services firms recruited from the same pool, and in the 2000s that pool was tight. Kuruvilla and Ranganathan, writing in mid-2008, put turnover 'in 2007' at 'about 20% in the software segment and 50-60% in the business process outsourcing segment' (S0278, manuscript p. 15). They give NASSCOM's counts of software professionals as 297,000 in 2005, 398,000 in 2006 and 562,000 in 2007. 'the top three software firms hired more than 72,000 in FY 2006-2007', and 'Infosys alone recruited over 25,000 in 2006' (p. 15).

Lateral hiring rather than graduate intake drove the competition. 'In 2003, 77% of the organizations met their recruiting needs through "lateral" hires', and 32% of Infosys's planned 25,000 hires in 2006-07 were lateral. The result was 'a high degree of "poaching" at middle management levels in the software segment' (S0278, p. 17). The share of software employees with less than two years' experience rose from 19% in 2003 to 33% in 2004 (NASSCOM, via S0278, p. 17). On research and development work the authors found 'relatively few current shortages' in mid-2008, except 'desperate shortages of visionaries' in software R&D (p. 18).

That last observation bears on the idea of a parallel profession. If multinational R&D centres could fill most posts from the same graduate pool as the services firms, then the difference between the two careers lay in the work and the employer, not in who was hired. The 2008 Zinnov count gives the other side: attrition at R&D centres of 8-20% a year and rising costs (S0207†). That is about the same range as the services firms. The services firms said so themselves. Infosys's annual report for fiscal 2002 stated: 'We have historically experienced significant competition for employees from large multinational companies that have established and continue to establish offshore operations in India, as well as from companies within India' (S0260). By fiscal 2009 the language had widened: 'Increased hiring by technology companies, particularly in India, and increasing worldwide competition for skilled technology professionals may lead to a shortage in the availability of qualified personnel in the markets in which we operate and hire' (S0271). Cognizant's 2024 report names clients' GCCs (S0192). The labour market was one market throughout.

This matters for how the chapter frames the 'parallel profession'. The two kinds of employer did not draw on separate pools of people. They drew on the same graduates, and many engineers moved between them. What differed was the relationship to the work. At a multinational's own centre, the engineer built or ran the parent's products and systems. Gains in productivity stayed inside the company that owned them. At a services firm, the engineer's time was sold to a client, and the contract decided who kept any gain. A career could run through both. The industry's growth from the 2000s onward was in large part the growth of this second, in-house kind of employment, which by FY2024 the consultants counted at 1.9 million (S0141†). Whether engineers in the two settings were paid differently, promoted differently or valued their work differently is the question the chapter most needs evidence for and least has.

### 3.6 From 'captive' to 'GCC'

The term 'global capability centre' arrived in the 2010s. The consultancy Zinnov and NASSCOM's 2024 report counts 'over 1,700 Global Capability Centers (GCCs) and more than 2,975 units as of FY2024'. 'These centers have collectively generated $64.6 billion in revenue and employ over 1.9 million professionals' (S0141†). It counts 'over 400 new GCCs' in five years and claims that multinationals now have 'nearly one-third of their global Engineering activities' in India. It also reports 'over 500 centers now dedicated to AI and Machine Learning' and 'a talent pool exceeding 120,000 AI professionals', and 'more than 6,500 global roles' held in Indian GCCs, 'including over 1,100 women leaders'. It projects 2,100-2,200 centres and 2.5-2.8 million staff by 2030 (S0141†). The page is dated 11 September 2024 but refers to the 2025 Union Budget, so it has been updated (new dispute qq). These are consultant and lobby figures. The 'revenue' of a captive is largely its parent's cost reimbursed at a transfer price, not market sales, which the drafting must explain.

Set against NASSCOM's estimate of about 5.95 million in the whole technology industry in FY2026 (S0140), the GCC count suggests that roughly a third of India's tech workforce now works for foreign companies' own centres. The years and definitions differ, so this is an order of magnitude, not a ratio.

The physical form changed too. In 2019 Amazon opened a Hyderabad campus it described as 'our first owned campus outside USA and the single largest building in terms of area and head count for Amazon globally', with '15,000 work points across 1.8 million square feet' (S0201, company blog). Banks and retailers (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Walmart) built large centres too; those sources are in the register but not yet read (S0202, S0203).

States now compete for GCCs. Karnataka's GCC policy for 2024-29 aims, in Zinnov's summary, 'to double the number of GCCs to 1,000 and generate 350,000 jobs by 2029' (S0141†; the government order itself, S0204, is not read). The Union Budget of February 2025 announced a framework for GCCs in tier-2 cities (S0205, register only).

### 3.7 The nearshore comparison: EPAM

The clearest test of whether India's advantage lay in scale is a firm built elsewhere. EPAM Systems, a US-listed services company whose delivery base was in the former Soviet bloc, reported in its 10-K for 2021 that 'Our largest delivery centers are located in Ukraine, Belarus and Russia'. It had 12,389 delivery professionals in Ukraine, 9,416 in Belarus and 8,933 in Russia, out of 52,617. 'Our other significant locations with delivery professionals are India with 4,349, Poland with 3,055, the United States with 2,757, Hungary with 1,991 and Mexico with 1,018' (S0170, 10-K FY2021).

Then the war came. The 2024 report records 'business continuity plans, relocation costs, our humanitarian commitment to our people in Ukraine, and the cost of our phased exit from Russia', which 'resulted in materially increased expenses'. It records the 'sale of the Company's remaining holdings in Russia to a third-party on July 26, 2023' (S0170, 10-K FY2024). And: 'During 2024, India became our largest delivery location, measured by the number of delivery professionals, and as of December 31, 2024, we had 10,072 delivery professionals in this location. We focused on growing India as a key delivery location and added 3,006 delivery professionals since December 31, 2023' (S0170, 10-K FY2024). Total delivery staff were about 55,100.

The sidebar's question is why Eastern Europe could not scale as India did. EPAM's filings cannot answer that alone; they describe one firm, and a war is not a market test. But they show that even the leading nearshore firm, after the shock of 2022, turned to India for growth. EPAM's Indian delivery staff more than doubled between 2021 and 2024. The 2024 count for Ukraine has not been found in the extracted text; the register's figure (8,764) needs checking against the filing.

### 3.8 The circle closes

The final document in this chapter's arc is a services firm's annual report. Cognizant's 10-K for 2024 lists among its competitors 'clients' in-house technology resources, such as GCCs'. It warns: 'we face competition from clients' in-house technology resources, such as GCCs, which may provide a lower cost alternative to our services' and 'We compete for employees not only with other companies in our industry but also with ... our clients' GCCs' (S0192). A company that began in 1994 as one American firm's captive centre in India (S0274) now names other firms' captive centres as a threat to its business and its hiring.

For the book's argument, this matters in two ways. First, it shows the services model being squeezed from a second side, before AI: clients who once outsourced to Indian vendors increasingly build their own Indian teams. Second, it suggests where AI's effects may differ. A GCC employs engineers to build its parent's own products and systems. A services firm sells their hours to clients. When AI tools raise productivity, the GCC's parent keeps the gain directly, while the services firm must negotiate who keeps it (Chapters 9, 11 and Part V). Zinnov's claim that 500 GCCs are now 'dedicated to AI and Machine Learning' (S0141†) is a consultant's figure, but it points the same way.

**Myth M25 ('GCCs are a new phenomenon').** FALSE. The institution goes back to TI in 1985, the captives of the late 1980s and 1990s, and the R&D centres counted in 2008 (S0061, S0206, S0207†). Cognizant's own origin is a captive (S0274). What is new is the name, the scale (1.9 million), the spread beyond technology companies to banks and retailers, and the claim to global roles.
