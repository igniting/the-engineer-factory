## 1. How many engineers? (M08)

**College counts (S0248).** Engineering and technology colleges numbered 486 in 1997-98, 678 in 2000-01, 1,077 in 2001-02 and 1,562 in 2005-06. That is more than a tripling in eight years, after forty years of slow growth (289 in 1990-91). Institutions, not seats.

**Rajaraman p. 56.** 'There was a rapid expansion of engineering colleges between 1998 and 2010.' Private corporate bodies were allowed to set up universities in 2002 (p. 51). 'In 1998 the number of engineering students was around 135,000 and in 2010 it grew to 1,300,000, a growth of almost 10 times. The number of MCAs graduating in 1998 was around 7000 whereas it jumped to 120,000 by 2010 [108].' On p. 12 he says 'Currently India graduates around 1.3 million engineers per year with 32% in Computer Engineering and IT from about 3000 colleges [17].'

So the same 1.3 million is 'students' on p. 56 and 'graduates per year' on p. 12. Neither is defined. The 135,000 for 1998 is close to annual intake at the time, not total enrolment (four-year enrolment would be several times larger). The 1.3 million for 2010 is closer to approved intake (15.49 lakh in 2012-13; see below) than to graduates. Probable reading: both figures are annual intake or approved seats. Do not present 1.3 million as graduates. **New dispute (ii).**

**Duke 2005 (S0213).**
- 2004 counts (table, p. 5): India 215,000 engineering, CS and IT degrees, of which 112,000 were four-year bachelor's and 103,000 three-year diplomas.
- Comparators: the US 222,335 (137,437 bachelor's, 84,898 sub-baccalaureate) and China 644,106 (351,537 bachelor's).
- The press figure of '350,000' for India was not comparable because it included diplomas and set them against US four-year degrees (pp. 2-3).

The India figures are NASSCOM projections from IAMR and MHRD data with a 'three- to four-year lag'. Duke notes 'the most recent official Indian graduation data are from 1993' (p. 8). Duke also reports NASSCOM's figure that 84.8% of four-year engineering graduates took IT-related branches (p. 8). This includes electrical and electronics, so it is broad. Per million people, Duke estimates the US produced about 750 technology graduates, China 500 and India 200 (p. 6).

**Aspiring Minds 2014 (S0153, p. 7).** It refers to 'the six hundred thousand engineers that graduate annually' (c. 2013), with no source given.

**Avanti Fellows (secondary, 2026).** Approved undergraduate engineering intake was 15.49 lakh in 2012-13, peaked at 17.05 lakh in 2014-15 and fell to 12.53 lakh in 2021-22. The vacancy rate (share of approved seats empty) was 37.6% in 2012-13, peaked at 49.5% in 2016-17 and was 34.1% in 2021-22. These derive from AICTE and AISHE data; check against the regulators in Phase 3.

**Reconciliation for the drafting.**
- Graduates: about 112,000 bachelor's in 2004 (NASSCOM via Duke) and about 600,000 a year by 2013 (Aspiring Minds, unsourced).
- Approved intake: about 1.5-1.7 million by 2012-15, with a third to half of seats empty.

Growth in bachelor's graduates of roughly five times in a decade is plausible from the college counts. M08 ('1.5 million engineers a year') mixes intake with output. Verdict stays MIXED to MOSTLY FALSE.

## 2. The factory floor: hiring and training at scale

Infosys recruiting funnel (company figures):

| Fiscal year | Applications | Tested | Interviewed | Offers | Accepted/joined | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FY1998 | 70,870 | 21,350 | 5,800 | 1,790 | 1,300 | S0089 p. 6 |
| FY2002 | 193,640 | | 6,730 | 1,710 | 1,550 | S0260 |
| FY2005 | 1,348,800 'screened' | 122,000 | 36,600 | 14,500 | | 20-F FY2005 |
| FY2008 | 885,000 'screened' | 227,600 | 97,500 | 46,300 | | 20-F FY2008 |
| FY2012 | 622,970 | | 60,860 | 41,460 | | 20-F FY2012 |

Offers rose about 26-fold from FY1998 to FY2008. Applications behave oddly: 1.35 million in FY2005 but 885,000 in FY2008, probably because of a definitional change ('screened' vs 'received'). Use offers as the scale measure.

**Headcount.** About 91,200 employees on 31 March 2008, about 18,700 of them outside India (20-F FY2008), and about 150,000 on 31 March 2012 (20-F FY2012). Net additions were about 19,174 in FY2012, 17,000 in FY2011 and 8,900 in FY2010. Compare 9,400 IT professionals in March 2002 (S0260).

**Training at Mysore.**
- FY2002: a 200-acre campus 'recently set up' (S0260).
- FY2005: 'The Mysore facility is able to house 4,500 trainees at any one time, and is able to provide the facilities required for the training of approximately 12,000 employees annually.' New hires take a '14-week training course' (20-F FY2005).
- FY2008: 4,800 at a time and about 15,200 a year, on a 336-acre campus (20-F FY2008).
- FY2012: 'We train the new engineering graduates that join us at our Global Education Center in Mysore. With a total built-up area of 1.44 million square feet, the Infosys Global Education Center can train approximately 14,000 employees at a time' (20-F FY2012).

S0115 (press releases: opened by the Prime Minister in February 2005, 4,500 trainees, 14.5-week programme) is consistent but not read.

**Rajaraman p. 56.**
- 'The IT companies' common complaint was that there were not enough employable graduates with computer science degrees. Instead they recruited all engineering graduates with aptitude in computing; however they could not be directly placed as members of project teams. All major IT companies had in-house training programmes ranging from 12 weeks to 6 months.'
- 'every major software company was recruiting around 8000 graduates each year'.
- Attrition was 15-20%, and 'With many of these companies having a work force of the order of 120,000 the replacement numbers themselves were over 15,000 each year.'

Key point: the firms hired across branches (mechanical, civil, electrical) for 'aptitude' and turned them into programmers in three months. This is the factory. It also explains why the firms did not care which branch a graduate came from, and why colleges expanded IT-adjacent branches and seats.

## 3. The pyramid, the bench and utilisation

The pyramid is defined in the F-1 (S0089, p. 45; Ch. 11): 'two to five' onsite, '10 to 50' in India.

20-F FY2005 and FY2008: 'We define employee utilization as the proportion of total billed person months to total available person months excluding support personnel.' Both say trainees are not utilised during the 14-week course.

20-F FY2012: utilisation of billable employees including trainees was 69.0% in FY2012 and 72.1% in FY2011; excluding trainees, 75.9% and 79.6%. 'This decrease in employee utilization rates adversely affected our profitability for fiscal 2012'.

So in FY2012 nearly a third of Infosys's technical staff, counting trainees, was not billed at any moment. This is the bench: a stock of trained graduates kept so that projects can be staffed at once. The bench is part of the business model, because time-and-materials billing needs people ready to bill. It is also the first thing cut in a slowdown (Ch. 11, 2001-02; Ch. 14, 2008-09).

Attrition at Infosys was 10.5% in FY2004-05 and 13.4% in FY2006-08, against an industry estimate of 'approximately 20%' (20-F FY2005, FY2008). A senior Infosys executive gave 7% for 2003 (Business Today 2003, cited in S0117, p. 136 n. 11).

The utilisation series for FY2005-08 was not found by keyword. Search the MD&A tables in Phase 3.

## 4. Who got hired (class, caste, gender, region)

**Fuller and Narasimhan 2007 (S0117).** Fieldwork in Chennai, August 2003 to February 2005, mainly by Narasimhan. Most interviews were in one of the top six software companies, given the pseudonym 'Indian Computer Services' (ICS). 'All names used are pseudonyms' (p. 127). Nineteen ICS staff were interviewed, described as a 'tiny sample ... the haphazard product of the problems of access' (p. 127).

Profile of ICS (p. 125):
- Applicants need 'a consistent record of high examination marks before they can sit the company's aptitude test and proceed to an interview, where only one out of twelve is offered a job'.
- 'Approximately one-fifth of new recruits are women'.
- Starting salaries are 'around 15,000 rupees per month' and 'can easily double within a few years'.
- NASSCOM: 79% of software professionals were men in 2001-02, projected to be 65% by 2005 (p. 125 n. 8).
- NCAER: only about 6% of households had incomes over Rs 180,000 a year in 2001-02, while IT recruits earn around this level individually (pp. 125-126).

Caste (p. 126): ICS 'is said to be dominated by the high-status "Forward Castes" - especially Tamil Brahmans, who form about 2.5 per cent of Tamilnadu's population - and it is our strong impression that this is true'. But 'no accurate figures exist'.

Mechanism (pp. 142-143):
- Tamil Nadu reserves 68% of state posts and college seats for OBCs, SCs and STs, 'and many people say that this is why many private companies employ a lot of Brahmans. Yet reservations alone cannot fully explain upper-caste predominance'.
- Brahmans are urban and more likely to have 'the social skills and cultural knowledge, including fluency in English, needed to work in global IT companies and to pass their selection tests'.
- 'Because ICS allows only candidates with consistently high marks to apply for posts, its selection policy in practice favours Forward Caste candidates'.
- The idea that Brahmans have a special aptitude for software from traditional learning is 'fanciful' (pp. 142-143).
- 2004 Anna University cut-off marks for electronics and communication: 293.28/300 (Forward Caste), 290.57 (Backward), 283.67 (Most Backward), 276.13 (Scheduled Caste) (p. 143 n. 13).

So merit-based selection reproduced existing advantage through marks, English and urban background. This is the core argument for 'who got hired'. It is a scholarly interpretation from one city and one firm, not a statistic.

Family background (p. 141): 'Most younger people we have interviewed have fathers (and occasionally mothers) who are or were employed in professional or managerial positions in government, or public- or private-sector firms, and the only exceptional fathers are a few small businessmen or farmers.' So IT in this sample drew on the existing salaried middle class; it did not create one from below. Evidence for M09.

**Fuller and Narasimhan 2008 (S0118).**
- p. 170: Tamil Brahmans are 'disproportionately well represented' in south Indian IT, but 'no figures to confirm this assertion exist'.
- p. 189: 'Because software engineering in companies like ICS or TCS has progressively become more routinized and reliant on standard packages, IIT graduates are over-qualified and can secure more technically demanding, better-paid jobs. Nowadays, most new recruits in major software companies have only a bachelor's degree in engineering, usually from one of the leading engineering colleges in Tamilnadu.'
- p. 189: of twenty software engineers under thirty in their data, only three studied outside Tamil Nadu.
- p. 190: seven of the twenty had been on overseas assignments by 2005, most for 'only a few months'. 'the very logic of "offshoring" means that flows of people may diminish as the work travels to the site of labor'.

The p. 189 sentence is central for the thesis. Routinised work drew on state engineering colleges rather than the IITs. That is the work most exposed to AI.

**Gender (S0117, pp. 137-141).**
- Almost everyone insisted 'that both sexes have equal technical skills' (p. 138), in contrast to Woodfield's English software firm.
- The difference lies in family: 'Married women professionals consistently give their families higher priority than their work' (p. 138).
- Many men want 'home-loving' wives; women IT professionals prefer husbands in IT, while men tend to prefer wives who are not (p. 138).
- One leading company reportedly started an in-house matrimonial service to stop women resigning to join husbands elsewhere (p. 138 n. 12).
- Small-company managers 'admitted to reluctance about recruiting women, precisely because their family duties take them away from work' (p. 139).
- Real change against Patricia Caplan's 1970s Chennai: young women IT professionals 'command greater respect and have more bargaining power within their families - particularly over their own marriages' (pp. 140-141).
- 'Plainly, the IT industry has not revolutionised the position of Indian women. Yet ... real change has occurred' (p. 140).

**Region.** Tamil Nadu students stayed in-state because good engineering education was available there (S0118, p. 190). Saxenian: 60% of CS graduates came from AP, Karnataka and TN, c. 2000 (S0255, p. 14; Ch. 10). The south's private colleges fed the factory.

**Quantitative caste and gender data (S0211, S0212)** are not verified. Do not use them until read.

**Aspiring Minds on tier (S0153, p. 7).** 'colleges in tier 1 cities have 18.26% employable software engineers, whereas for those in tier 2 cities, it goes down to 14.17%.' '53% of employable candidates for IT services companies and 25% of employable candidates for IT product companies are studying beyond the top 750 colleges, and thus end up being invisible to most employers.' p. 8: 'in the typical resume shortlisting process, the college name is a key signal, and resumes from unknown colleges aren't shortlisted.' Vendor claim, but it points to selection by college tier.

## 5. Employability (M07)

Aspiring Minds 2014 (S0153):
- Sample of 'more than 1,20,000 engineering students from 520+ engineering colleges', all graduating in 2013, tested on its AMCAT test (p. 9).
- Benchmarks are the vendor's own, 'used by more than 650 companies, including six of the top-ten IT services companies' (p. 9).
- p. 7: 'only 18.43% of them are employable for the Software Engineer-IT services role, while a dismal 3.95% are appropriately trained to be directly deployed on projects.'
- p. 12: the IT services figure is calculated under the firms' 'current hiring philosophy', where the candidate 'is imparted the training over a period of 3 to 6 months', so 18.43% means 'trainable into software engineers within a period of 3 to 6 months'. IT product role: 3.21% (computer and IT branches only).
- p. 13: 'The IT services industry is not growing at the same pace as before and the growth of entry-level jobs is diminishing', and 'there is a slow but definite trend towards hiring candidates who already have decent expertise in programming'.

The '25%' version of M07 traces to McKinsey Global Institute (2005), via Duke (S0213, p. 9): 'McKinsey concluded that only 10% of Chinese engineers and 25% of Indian engineers can compete in the global outsourcing arena'. McKinsey not read; register as a new ID if obtainable.

Assessment: both are suitability estimates for specific roles by interested parties, a test vendor and a consultancy. M07 stays MOSTLY FALSE as a labour-market claim. Add the McKinsey origin.

The 2014 report's own note about entry-level growth slowing and firms seeking candidates who can already code is the earliest documented sign, in this research, of the shift that AI later accelerates. Useful for the bridge to Part V.

## 6. What the work was like (voices)

All from S0117, pseudonyms, paraphrase unless quoted.

- **Raghu**, 22, software engineer, Tamil Brahman, electronics and communication degree from Coimbatore (p. 132).
  - Works on production support for a British client; 'every day a new problem has to be solved', but 'he thinks he is not learning much - except how to say "no" to demanding, foreign clients'.
  - Wants an MBA.
  - After one year he earned Rs 200,000 a year, against his father's Rs 400,000 after thirty years.
- **Sarojini**, mid-20s, Nayar, electrical and electronics degree from Coimbatore, joined as a trainee in 2002 (p. 131).
  - Found the work 'exciting and challenging'; her first ambition was medicine.
  - Sent to New Jersey in 2004.
- **Parvati**, same age, Tamil Brahman, BSc and MSc in IT (pp. 131-132).
  - Found the work 'boring' after six months; a year later she was a team leader seeking a US posting.
  - Both said they worked 40-45 hours a week.
- **Shoaib**, 29, runs ICS's intranet, MCA, from a Muslim college in Chennai (p. 130).
  - Turned down an electricity board post: in the public sector you 'have to bow your head to somebody'.
  - 'there is no security in this job'.
  - Claimed 'any schoolleaver with reasonable intelligence could learn to do the job' (p. 137, the authors' paraphrase; they say he was exaggerating).
- **Jayashree**, about 35, assistant project manager (pp. 130-131).
  - Childcare 'an endlessly negotiated series of arrangements'.
  - Joked that household chores had been 'outsourced'.
  - Later resigned and did contract work from home.
- **Anuradha**, early 30s, project manager (pp. 129-130). 'firmly believes that the IT industry has helped to empower women'.
- **Ravi**, 33, project manager (p. 128). Finds the industry 'empowering'.
- **Sampath**, about 50, personnel manager at a vehicle maker (pseudonym 'Chakra Vehicles') (p. 133). IT has 'disturbed the social fabric' with 'quick promotions', 'fancy designations' and 'phenomenal compensation'. The authors report a colleague saying that by the late 1990s 'only fourth-rate men wanted to join Chakra' at campus interviews (p. 134; their paraphrase).

**Rank-and-file requirement.** Raghu, Sarojini, Parvati and Shoaib are rank-and-file and named, but by pseudonym, in a peer-reviewed ethnography. Honesty rule: always say 'whom the anthropologists call X'. They are real people, not composites, and quotations are given as the authors report them. This meets the requirement better than any chapter so far, with the pseudonym caveat. Still missing: a trainee at Mysore in their own words, and a graduate from a lower-tier private college who was not hired.
