## 1. The backlash in the US, 2003-06

**The 2004 law (S0111, GCN, 20 February 2004).**
- 'A provision in the fiscal 2004 omnibus budget bill that President Bush signed last month bars companies that win federal jobs through A-76 competitions from shifting the work to other countries.'
- 'The measure, known as the Thomas-Voinovich Amendment after its two Republican sponsors, Sen. Craig Thomas of Wyoming and Sen. George Voinovich of Ohio, is part of a swell of legislation against the growing trend of businesses and governments seeking software and IT services from companies and workers in countries such as India and China.'
- It prohibits 'for one year' any work won by a vendor from being 'performed by the contractor at a location outside the United States'.
- The National Conference of State Legislatures 'has identified 29 bills pending in 19 state legislatures that would require contractors to hire only U.S. citizens or legal residents, regulate agencies' use of offshore call centers and otherwise restrict foreign purchases'.
- Justin Marks (NCSL): 'Over the past three years, state legislatures have had to fill a $200 billion budget gap ... now they see white-collar jobs going abroad.'
- John Kost (Gartner): 'People are naive if they think it is not already happening in large quantities'; 'Once the economy improves and politicians move to a different topic, I doubt it will be a potent political issue.'

Signed 'last month' means January 2004 (register: 23 January 2004). Scope: limited to work won in A-76 competitions, for one year. Symbolic more than material.

**Mankiw, February 2004 (via Blinder, S0113, 'A Controversy Reconsidered').** Offshoring is 'the latest manifestation of the gains from trade that economists have talked about at least since Adam Smith. ... More things are tradable than were tradable in the past, and that's a good thing.' 'Democratic and Republican politicians alike excoriated Mankiw'.

**Blinder 2006 (S0113).**
- 'well under a million service-sector jobs in the United States have been lost to offshoring to date'. That is 'less than two weeks' worth of normal gross job losses'.
- The future: 'much more offshoring of "impersonal services" - that is, services that can be delivered electronically over long distances with little or no degradation in quality.'
- 'We are now in the early stages of a third Industrial Revolution - the information age.'
- The distinction is not skill: 'Janitors and crane operators are probably immune to foreign competition; accountants and computer programmers are not.'
- 'typing services (a low-skill job) and security analysis (a high-skill job) are already being delivered electronically from India'.
- Estimate: jobs 'susceptible to offshoring in the electronic future is two to three times the total number of current manufacturing jobs (which is about 14 million)', i.e. roughly 28-42 million. The 'up to a third' wording in the register is a derived gloss; use Blinder's own terms.
- Critical for the AI thesis: 'the dividing line between personal and impersonal services will move over time. As information technology improves, more and more personal services will become impersonal services.'
- Remedy: the supposed remedy of 'more education and a general "upskilling"' is insufficient, because the line is not about education.

**Thesis link.** Blinder's test (can the work be delivered electronically without loss of quality?) is the same test AI applies, with the destination being a model rather than Bangalore. The work India took was the work that could be sent down a wire. That makes it the first candidate for the next move.

**Dobbs (S0112).** Not read. Mention only as a fact of the period ('Exporting America', 2004), from the register, if at all.

## 2. BPO and the voice era

**Raman Roy (S0206, Wharton, 4 June 2003). Self-account.**
- At American Express India in the mid-1980s, 'the cost per transaction from India was significantly lower than those of the more advanced markets', 'lower almost by 40% to 50%'. Head office doubted it: 'These guys in India obviously don't know how to make their reports; how could these numbers be true?'
- Amex consolidated in Phoenix, Brighton and India.
- Infrastructure: VSNL staff 'looked at us as though we were from some other world because 90% up-time was considered outstanding'.
- People: 'you could get a full CPA or a chartered accountant in India for marginally more than what you would pay for an associate in the US'. A US associate earned '$15,000 to $18,000 or perhaps $20,000' and had 'just completed high school'; in India he 'was able to field professionals who were at least college graduates and who sometimes were CPAs'.
- On the savings from prompt-payment discounts: 'You've got us for free because the incremental discounts that we got alone were more than the salary bill in India.'
- Joined GE in 1996; 'the Five Sigma beta site for GE Capital'.
- Call centres: a senior GE person crossed out the investment, saying 'call centers would never happen out of India'. Roy: 'It was nationalistic pride that drove me to see that a call center came into being.' 'Today, I believe that more than 50% of GE Capital's India revenue comes from its call centers.'
- Founded Spectramind, acquired by Wipro in 2002.
- The founder dispute over GECIS (Roy vs Pramod Bhasin; 1996 vs 1997) remains (S0110 note).

**Thesis link.** BPO was the same arbitrage as software (educated Indian graduates doing work done by less-educated Americans) applied to back offices and voice. The work was even more codified (scripts, reconciliations, Six Sigma), and so even more exposed to automation later.

**Scale (S0107, NASSCOM 2008; lobby figures).**
- Slide 2: 'Over 1200 members including 250 global companies'; members are 'over 90% of exports'; '2 million professionals employed in the sector'.
- Slide 9: 'Direct employment to reach nearly 2 million; indirect job creation estimated at 7-8 million'. NASSCOM's own multiplier; treat with caution.
- Slide 10: total industry revenue 'US$ 64 billion' (FY2008E); exports 62-66%. The sourcing model includes Indian providers, global captives and global providers. The BPO share is about 28% (the slide is hard to read in text).
- Slide 12: FY2007 exports $31.4bn (excluding hardware); US 61%, UK 18%, Continental Europe 12%, APAC 6%. 'Indian IT-BPO Exports grew at a CAGR of 35% over FY2004-07'.
- Slide 18: net employee additions 234,000 (FY05), 328,000 (FY06), 375,000 (FY08E). Pressure points: 'Talent is trainable - need to make them employable'; 'High lateral hiring leading to attrition'; 'Tier 3 colleges yields low'.
- Background checks (National Skills Registry slides): 'Estimated cost of recruitment and initial training is about 6 to 8 months of employee wages'; 'Cases of CV fudging being detected - estimate ~ 30% of all CVs'; 'With increasing levels of outsourcing, employees were viewed as a significant weak link'; 'No law addressing privacy or employee rights'.

**RBI (S0106 †, 2010-11 survey).**
- 760 companies, 'nearly 78 per cent' of exports.
- 2010-11 exports Rs 2,170.1bn (US$47.6bn); 2009-10 Rs 1,836.9bn (US$38.7bn).
- Computer services 73.7% and ITES/BPO 26.3% (2010-11), against 69.0% and 31.0% (2009-10). ITES/BPO was 'nearly flat at Rs 571.7 billion'.
- Offsite 79.3% and onsite 20.7%.
- USA 63.6%; US and Canada 65.0%; Europe 23.5% (from 26.5%).
- 2007-08 (register): 74.6% offsite.

Note: the RBI and NASSCOM series differ in definition. RBI is the official balance-of-payments series. Use RBI for 2002-03 onward in the data appendix.

**Workers' voices (S0273; activist account; low reliability).** A foreign worker's report from a Gurgaon research and call centre, 2005-06.
- 'The only language allowed for internal communication in the call centre is English.'
- Co-workers given by first name only: Priti from Bhopal has 'worked in fourteen call centres already'. Pravesh, 28, 'has not seen his two year old boy for thirteen days'.
- Maneesh on his first job: 'the team leader told us every morning that we have to treat the customer like a king'. The rest of the quote is profane; use only this part, or paraphrase.
- Cab drivers 'tired after seventeen hours shifts'; a co-worker killed in a car crash on her way from work.
- 'Not a single call centre employee I talked to had experience with manual labour.'

Use only with the label 'an anonymous activist who worked in a Gurgaon call centre in 2005-06 wrote ...'. Do not treat first names as verified identities.

**Night shifts and transport.** The December 2005 murder of HP Globalsoft employee Pratibha Srikanth Murthy by a company cab driver in Bangalore led to scrutiny of night-shift transport for women. The trial court convicted in October 2010; the High Court upheld. Sources found are press only (News Minute, Oneindia). Handle with restraint; a court record is needed before drafting. Not registered yet.

Attrition: Infosys 10.5% (FY2004-05) and 13.4% (FY2006-08), against the industry's 'approximately 20%' (S0264, S0265). NASSCOM 2008 names attrition as the first 'common question' clients ask: 'High attrition and wage inflation... is India's talent pool running dry?' (slide 17).

**The story of 2008-09.** Offers fell from 46,300 to 16,900, salaries were frozen and utilisation fell. Yet headcount still rose by about 13,700 in FY2009 and 8,900 in FY2010. The big firms bent but did not break. The crisis showed the bench as a buffer: hiring slowed, the bench absorbed the shock, and fixed costs were cut through wages, not layoffs. Compare 2001-02 (Ch. 11).

## 4. Policy: SEZs and the end of the STPI holiday

- SEZ Act 2005 (assent 23 June 2005; register) and s. 10AA: units in SEZs got tax holidays after STPI's s. 10A expired.
- S0266 (FY2012): 'Some of our software development centers located at Chandigarh, Chennai, Hyderabad, Mangalore, Mysore, Pune, Trivandrum and Jaipur currently operate in SEZs and many of our proposed development centers are likely to operate in SEZs.'
- The 10A sunset: holiday available up to AY 2011-12 (ITAT Pune order, search snippet; not read). The statutory text and the Finance Act amendments are still to be read.

**Thesis link.** The state's subsidy for the industry moved from STPs (1990s) to SEZs (2005-). Each move pushed firms to build large campuses. The SEZ era favoured big firms able to build their own zones.
