## 1. Tally (S0178, company page)

Timeline on Tally's own site:
- 1986: 'Peutronics Financial Accountant' was founded by Bharat Goenka, 'inspired by his father, Shyam Sundar Goenka's vision to simplify business operations'.
- 1988: 'Launched Tally 3.0, pioneering code-less accounting'.
- 1991: 'Tally Solutions Private Limited was officially established'. The register says incorporated 8 November 1991 and renamed in 1999; the company page says 1991 established. **New dispute (vv).**
- 1999: Tally 6.3.
- 2005: Tally 7.2 with VAT compliance.

Today: 'For nearly four decades, we've helped millions across 100+ countries ... supported by 28,000+ partners'. Company claim.

Point: a product for Indian small business, sold through partners and tied to Indian tax law (VAT, later GST). The domestic market sustained it. It is the counter-case to the export services model.

## 2. i-flex and FLEXCUBE (S0176, Hukku, 2007)

- In 1992 Hukku, 'then head of Citicorp Overseas Software, spun off Citicorp Information Technology Industries Ltd. (CITIL), now called i-flex solutions'. 'Citi had decided that software wasn't its business, but invested $400,000'.
- 'Five years later, i-flex launched FLEXCUBE'; 'sold to more than 300 banks in 120 countries'.
- 'In 2005, Oracle bought out Citicorp's 42% interest for $592 million'. The register (S0176 note, and the chronology for August 2005) says 41% and $593m. **New dispute (ww).** Use the Wharton figures with attribution, or find the filing.
- Listed in 2002 on the NSE and BSE.
- December quarter FY2007 revenue Rs 5.5bn ($126m).
- Hukku: BITS Pilani; worked at TCS before Citicorp's in-house unit.

Quotes:
- On the industry: 'almost all software companies followed the model of [Faqir Chand] Kohli, who pioneered the concept of the Indian software exports industry with TCS in the early 1970's, of exporting the Indian brain, or doing software services projects offshore.' 'The not-so-great news was that hundreds of companies that came after copied him. Basically it is a human tendency to take the route which is already a proven success.'
- On 'Made in Spite of India': 'It's not in spite of India, but it's in spite of whatever was successful when we came into existence.'
- FLEXCUBE ranking (International Banking Systems league table): 16th in 1997, 9th in the second year, 2nd in 1999, '1 since 2002'. Company-cited third-party ranking.
- 'We are a product company. We're also a services company.'
- Citi's deal: 'you get the money and you don't get controlled.'
- Prediction: 'I can guarantee you that five years from now, there will be many famous product companies growing out of India.'

Point: India's most successful early global product came out of a foreign bank's captive (link to Ch. 15, the Cognizant pattern). It was a hybrid of product and services.

More from S0176:
- 'on day one we were selling professional services'; they went to Africa because 'we couldn't even afford the airfare' to the UK or US.
- MicroBanker (legacy Citicorp product) sold to '117 banks, mostly in Africa', number 1 in 1995; services and MicroBanker money funded FLEXCUBE.
- Citi's condition: 'Please don't come back asking for business from Citibank.'
- 'We're perhaps the only IT company from India who made $0 from Y2K.'
- '10% to 15% of the staff have to be bankers'.
- On dependence on the US: 'a very big limitation for the whole industry to be dependent on one country'.

## 4. The iSPIRT break (S0188, 14 March 2013)

- 'A group of 30 software product companies in India recently got together to form the Indian Software Product Industry Round Table (iSpirt).'
- The industry 'has grown from US$100 million in 1992 to US$108 billion in 2012'. NASSCOM figures via Wharton.
- Bharat Goenka (via *Business Standard*): 'At Nasscom, the product companies do not have the same weightage as the services companies. The policy interventions Nasscom is trying to make are more in favor of the services industry.'
- Sharad Sharma: 'We believe that with this think-tank, we will get a louder voice in policy making and other initiatives.'
- N. Chandrasekaran (NASSCOM chairman, TCS CEO): 'When you are on a growth paradigm, you cannot do everything that you want to do. I don't see any conflict [with iSpirt].'
- The Murthy committee's NASSCOM roadmap: 'US$300 billion by 2020'; software products 'around US$10 billion'; Internet and mobile US$100bn; domestic US$75bn. Murthy: 'we need to look at new areas of growth like Internet and mobile content and commerce, software products and the domestic market.'

Point: by 2013 products were a target of $10bn in a $300bn ambition. The lobby itself saw products as marginal.

## 5. Freshworks (S0173, S0284, S0285)

Founder letter in the S-1 (Girish Mathrubootham):
- 'We started in a 700 square foot warehouse in Chennai in 2010.'
- Doubts he heard: 'You can't find talent in Chennai; you can't win unless you move to Bangalore.' 'You really can't build a global SaaS company from India.'
- 'Freshworks was global from "day one" with our first few customers coming from four different continents.' Sales were 'search-driven online acquisition, powered by robust product-led growth'.

S-1 figures:
- Revenue $172.4m (2019), $249.7m (2020); net losses $31.1m and $57.3m.
- About 52,500 paying customers at 30 June 2021.
- 'A substantial portion of our operations and employees are located in India, including a majority of our software engineering resources.'
- Zoho sued Freshworks on 17 March 2020 in the Northern District of California (trade secrets; Computer Fraud and Abuse Act). Freshworks believes it has 'meritorious defenses'. Present as an allegation only; the outcome is to be found.

424B4 (S0284): 'The initial public offering price is $36.00' (22 September 2021). Over-allotment option 2,850,000 shares (15%, implying 19,000,000 base shares). The register says c. $1.03bn raised (press); compute in Phase 5 from the base-share figure.

10-K FY2024 (S0285):
- 'headquartered in San Mateo, CA ... with a majority of employees based in India, where we were founded'.
- 'As of December 31, 2024, approximately 3,700 of our employees reside in India, representing approximately 83% of our total employee population.' The total is therefore about 4,450; compute and confirm.
- 'On November 5, 2024, we committed to a plan ... that resulted in a 13% reduction in force.'

10-K FY2024 also: revenue $720.4m (2024), $596.4m (2023), $498.0m (2022). Generative AI products 'Freddy AI Agent and Freddy AI Copilot'; the Agent 'offers always-on, autonomous, personalized resolutions to customer and employee queries'. Risk: competitors' 'new AI offerings that disrupt our customers' needs'. Customers in 'more than 120 countries' (S-1).

Point: an Indian-built product company became a US company (Delaware, San Mateo, Nasdaq) with Indian engineers. Even in products, the work and the value capture were split across countries. Freshworks had 3,700 employees in India; TCS had over 600,000 (S0160). That is the scale gap between products and services.

## 8. Thesis links

- **Products capture productivity; services sell hours.** Products are the business model in which AI gains accrue to the owner. Freshworks' 'product-led growth' and low-touch sales are what services firms tried to approximate with fixed-price contracts (Ch. 9, 11).
- **Hukku's path dependence.** 'hundreds of companies that came after copied' Kohli's model is a participant's explanation for M29, not evidence by itself.
- **NASSCOM's 2013 roadmap** set products at $10bn of $300bn. The services frame was institutional.
- **Platforms.** UPI shows the state building infrastructure, with private builders on top.

## 9. M29

'Indian startups failed to build products because of a services mindset.' Evidence in hand:
- the participants' view: Hukku (path dependence); Goenka (lobby weighted to services);
- counter-examples: Tally (domestic), i-flex (captive spin-off), Finacle (inside a services firm), Freshworks (US-incorporated, India-built);
- the scale contrast: 3,700 at Freshworks India vs hundreds of thousands at the services firms.

The 'mindset' explanation is untested against structural ones: capital (VC funding only after 2000s); a domestic market for paid software (small until GST and UPI); exits; talent pull from well-paid services jobs. Verdict: still UNRESOLVED. Add the evidence; frame as competing explanations.
