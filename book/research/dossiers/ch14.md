**Chapter question.** How did the industry grow from a few hundred thousand to about two million workers in a decade of political backlash abroad and a financial crisis, and what did the strain reveal about the model?

## 1. Summary of findings (c. 300 words)

**Backlash.** In January 2004 Congress barred contractors who won certain federal work from moving it offshore for a year. The Thomas-Voinovich amendment was one of 'a swell of legislation', with 29 bills pending in 19 state legislatures (S0111). It was more symbol than barrier. A Gartner analyst predicted the issue would fade 'Once the economy improves' (S0111).

**Blinder's test.** The more lasting response was intellectual. In 2006 Alan Blinder argued that the dividing line in rich-country labour markets would run between work that 'can be delivered electronically' and work that cannot, not between skilled and unskilled (S0113). He added that 'more and more personal services will become impersonal services'. That is the test the AI chapters later apply.

**Scale.** By NASSCOM's estimate the industry had 'nearly 2 million' direct employees and $64bn in revenue by FY2008, with net additions of 234,000-375,000 a year (S0107; lobby figures). Business process outsourcing grew from captive back offices. Raman Roy describes Indian graduates doing work done in America by high-school leavers, at 40-50% lower cost per transaction (S0206, self-account).

**The 2008-09 crisis.** It tested the factory. Infosys cut offers from about 46,300 in FY2008 to 16,900 in FY2009 and froze salary increases. Utilisation including trainees fell to 66.9% in early 2009 (S0265, S0271). Yet headcount still rose from 91,200 to 113,800 by March 2010 (S0272). The bench absorbed the shock, wages took the cut, and the large firms avoided mass layoffs. Clients pushed currency risk and price benchmarking onto suppliers (S0271, S0272).

**State support.** Support moved from software technology parks to special economic zones. The STPI tax holiday ended in 2011, and by 2012 Infosys ran centres in eight SEZs (S0266).

**Gap.** No named Indian worker from these years speaks in their own words in the sources in hand.

## 3. Evidence notes

### 3.1 The backlash

The first serious American political reaction to Indian software and services came in the slow recovery after the dot-com bust. On 20 February 2004 the trade paper *GCN* reported: 'A provision in the fiscal 2004 omnibus budget bill that President Bush signed last month bars companies that win federal jobs through A-76 competitions from shifting the work to other countries' (S0111). The measure, 'known as the Thomas-Voinovich Amendment after its two Republican sponsors', was described as 'part of a swell of legislation against the growing trend of businesses and governments seeking software and IT services from companies and workers in countries such as India and China'. It barred, 'for one year', work won by a vendor from being 'performed by the contractor at a location outside the United States' (S0111).

The states went further in proposal, if not in law. The National Conference of State Legislatures had 'identified 29 bills pending in 19 state legislatures that would require contractors to hire only U.S. citizens or legal residents, regulate agencies' use of offshore call centers and otherwise restrict foreign purchases' (S0111). Its analyst Justin Marks tied the politics to state budgets: legislators 'have seen manufacturing jobs go overseas and now they see white-collar jobs going abroad' (S0111). A Gartner analyst, John Kost, was sceptical that any of it would matter: 'People are naive if they think it is not already happening in large quantities ... Once the economy improves and politicians move to a different topic, I doubt it will be a potent political issue' (S0111).

The national argument flared the same month. In February 2004 Gregory Mankiw, chairman of the President's Council of Economic Advisers, called offshoring 'the latest manifestation of the gains from trade that economists have talked about at least since Adam Smith ... More things are tradable than were tradable in the past, and that's a good thing.' As Blinder later recalled, 'Democratic and Republican politicians alike excoriated Mankiw' (S0113, 'A Controversy Reconsidered'). The television presenter Lou Dobbs's book *Exporting America* (2004, S0112, not read) belongs to the same moment and should be mentioned only as a fact of the period.

What came of it? The federal restriction was narrow (work won in A-76 competitions) and temporary. Kost's prediction is the best guide to its long-term effect. The chapter's data (sections 3.3-3.5) show the industry tripling its workforce in the years after 2004. The backlash mattered more for what it taught the firms than for what it stopped. It encouraged them to build American offices, hire locally for visible roles, and frame their work as 'partnership' rather than replacement. That last point is an inference and needs sources (company annual reports 2004-06) before it is drafted.

### 3.2 Blinder's line

The most important document of the backlash period for this book is not a law but an essay. In *Foreign Affairs* in March 2006, Alan Blinder, a former vice-chairman of the Federal Reserve, argued that economists had underrated what was happening (S0113). He began by measuring it modestly: 'well under a million service-sector jobs in the United States have been lost to offshoring to date', which is 'less than two weeks' worth of normal gross job losses' (S0113, 'A Controversy Reconsidered'). Then he looked forward: 'constant improvements in technology and global communications virtually guarantee that the future will bring much more offshoring of "impersonal services" - that is, services that can be delivered electronically over long distances with little or no degradation in quality' (S0113, 'A Controversy Reconsidered'). 'We are now in the early stages of a third Industrial Revolution - the information age' (S0113, 'The Three Industrial Revolutions').

His central claim was that the line would not fall between skilled and unskilled work. 'Janitors and crane operators are probably immune to foreign competition; accountants and computer programmers are not.' 'typing services (a low-skill job) and security analysis (a high-skill job) are already being delivered electronically from India' (S0113, 'This Time It's Personal'). His estimate of the jobs 'susceptible to offshoring in the electronic future' was 'two to three times the total number of current manufacturing jobs (which is about 14 million)', that is, roughly 28-42 million American jobs (S0113, 'This Time It's Personal'). And he warned that the usual remedy, 'more education and a general "upskilling" of the work force', would not be enough, because education was not the dividing line (S0113, 'This Time It's Personal').

One sentence in the essay carries the argument of this whole book forward: 'the dividing line between personal and impersonal services will move over time. As information technology improves, more and more personal services will become impersonal services' (S0113, 'This Time It's Personal'). Blinder's question was whether a task could be sent down a wire without losing quality. Indian firms built an industry by answering yes for a growing list of tasks. The AI chapters ask the same question with a different destination. Work that can be specified, documented and delivered electronically to Bangalore can also, increasingly, be delivered to a model. Blinder did not foresee that. His framework anticipated it, and the drafting can say so without overstatement.

### 3.3 From back office to call centre

Software was half the story of this decade. The other half was 'business process outsourcing': accounts, claims, collections and, most visibly, the voice call. Raman Roy, who helped build the captive centres of American Express and GE before founding his own firm, gave his account to Knowledge at Wharton in June 2003 (S0206). It is a self-account and should be presented as such. It is also the clearest description in hand of why the model worked.

At American Express in India in the mid-1980s, Roy said, 'the cost per transaction from India was significantly lower than those of the more advanced markets', 'lower almost by 40% to 50%'. Head office did not believe it: 'These guys in India obviously don't know how to make their reports; how could these numbers be true?' (S0206). The deeper advantage was who did the work. 'you could get a full CPA or a chartered accountant in India for marginally more than what you would pay for an associate in the US'. In America the associate doing bank reconciliations 'may have just completed high school' and earned '$15,000 to $18,000 or perhaps $20,000'. In India, at those salaries, he 'was able to field professionals who were at least college graduates and who sometimes were CPAs' (S0206). This is the same arbitrage as the software story: over-qualified Indian graduates doing work done in rich countries by people with less education, at a fraction of the cost.

The infrastructure was the obstacle. When the team asked the state telecoms company VSNL for 100% uptime, 'they looked at us as though we were from some other world because 90% up-time was considered outstanding' (S0206). Roy joined GE in 1996. His unit became 'the Five Sigma beta site for GE Capital'. He recalls proposing call centres and a senior executive crossing out the investment, saying 'call centers would never happen out of India'. 'It was nationalistic pride that drove me to see that a call center came into being.' By 2003, he believed, 'more than 50% of GE Capital's India revenue comes from its call centers' (S0206). He then founded Spectramind, which Wipro bought in 2002. The founding date and authorship of GE Capital International Services (GECIS) remain disputed between accounts crediting Roy and Pramod Bhasin, and between 1996 and 1997 (S0110 register note).

Roy's account also contains a small story about tools. His Delhi unit reconciled 'about 12,000 accounts' on Lotus 1-2-3. 'if you took a sip of coffee or someone tapped you on the shoulder, and you deleted the wrong line, the account went out of reconciliation.' Head office planned a standard platform that would take 15 months to reach India. Roy's team built an interim tool with a local firm run by his former head of technology, Sunil Gujral. When the American platform failed its tests, Roy ran the test files through the Indian tool: 'It took only 25% of the time to reconcile the file.' 'Within three months, this inexpensive, home-grown tool was nominated as the global reconciliation platform for American Express as an enterprise-wide standard' (S0206, self-account). The point for the book is that the back office was never only cheap labour. Once a process was moved and documented, the people running it also began to automate it. Productivity gains of this kind went to the client, not the worker, under the cost-centre and time-based arrangements of the period.

What the call-centre floor was like is poorly documented in the sources in hand. The scholarly studies (Taylor and Bain 2005; Noronha and D'Cruz 2009; Mirchandani 2012) could not be obtained. The one first-hand account is an anonymous activist's report, written after three months as a foreign agent in a Gurgaon centre in 2005-06 and posted in 2007 (S0273). It is partisan and its names cannot be verified. With that label, it records:
- 'The only language allowed for internal communication in the call centre is English';
- a co-worker from Bhopal who had 'worked in fourteen call centres already';
- a 28-year-old who 'has not seen his two year old boy for thirteen days';
- cab drivers 'tired after seventeen hours shifts', and a colleague killed in a car crash on her way home from work;
- the observation that 'Not a single call centre employee I talked to had experience with manual labour' (S0273).

The drafting should not rest anything on it alone. The night-shift transport problem it describes was real and became public after the murder of an HP Globalsoft employee, Pratibha Srikanth Murthy, by a company cab driver in Bangalore in December 2005. The driver was convicted in 2010. Only press sources are in hand; a court record is needed before the book mentions it, and it must be handled with care for the victim and her family.

### 3.4 Two million

The industry's own figures for 2008 give the scale, and must be labelled as NASSCOM's. A 2008 NASSCOM presentation described a membership of 'Over 1200 members including 250 global companies', accounting for 'over 90% of exports' and 'a majority of the 2 million professionals employed in the sector' (S0107, slide 2). It estimated direct employment at 'nearly 2 million' for FY2008, with 'indirect job creation estimated at 7-8 million' (slide 9; the multiplier is NASSCOM's own). Total revenue was put at $64bn, of which exports were 62-66% (slide 10). FY2007 exports were $31.4bn: 61% to the US, 18% to the UK, 12% to continental Europe. 'Indian IT-BPO Exports grew at a CAGR of 35% over FY2004-07' (slide 12). Net additions to employment were 234,000 in FY2005, 328,000 in FY2006 and an estimated 375,000 in FY2008 (slide 18).

The same deck lists the strains. 'High attrition and wage inflation... is India's talent pool running dry?' is the first question clients ask (slide 17). Under 'Pressure Points' it notes: 'Talent is trainable - need to make them employable', 'High lateral hiring leading to attrition', 'Tier 3 colleges yields low' (slide 18). In its section on the National Skills Registry, NASSCOM estimated 'cost of recruitment and initial training is about 6 to 8 months of employee wages' and 'CV fudging ... ~ 30% of all CVs'. It said 'With increasing levels of outsourcing, employees were viewed as a significant weak link', and noted that India had 'No law addressing privacy or employee rights' (S0107). The registry was a response to clients' fears: a database of verified employee histories, built by the employers' association.

The official series is the Reserve Bank's annual survey. For 2010-11, covering 760 companies and 'nearly 78 per cent' of exports, it estimated software and IT services exports at Rs 2,170.1 billion (US$47.6bn), against Rs 1,836.9 billion (US$38.7bn) in 2009-10 (S0106†). Computer services were 73.7% and ITES/BPO 26.3%, against 69.0% and 31.0% the year before, with ITES/BPO 'nearly flat'. The offsite share was 79.3% and onsite 20.7%; the US took 63.6% (S0106†). In 2007-08 the offsite share had been 74.6% (S0106, register). By the end of the period, four-fifths of export revenue was earned from work done in India. That is the reverse of the 1980s.

### 3.5 The factory under strain: 2008-09

Infosys's filings show how the largest firms absorbed the crisis. Employment rose from about 25,700 in March 2004 (S0270) to about 91,200 in March 2008 (S0265). Offers of employment, the best measure of intake, went from about 14,500 in FY2005 to 46,300 in FY2008 (S0264, S0265). Then they fell to 16,900 in FY2009, before recovering to 26,200 in FY2010 and 41,460 in FY2012 (S0271, S0272, S0266).

The filings name the instruments of adjustment. The first was wages. 'Until 2009, when we instituted a freeze in salary increases, we undertook a compensation review on an annual basis' (S0271). The next year's filing records average increases of 8% offshore and 2% onsite, against 11-13% and 4-5% in fiscal 2009. The filing dates the later rise to 'October 2010', probably an error for 2009 (S0272; new dispute oo). The second was the bench. Utilisation including trainees fell from 70.7% in FY2008 to 68.9% in FY2009, and to 66.9% in the quarter to March 2009, then 67.5% in FY2010 (S0271, S0272). That is, about a third of technical staff was unbilled at the trough, and the company kept them rather than lay them off. Headcount still rose, to about 104,900 in March 2009 and 113,800 in March 2010, with net additions of about 8,900 in FY2010 (S0271, S0272).

The third was pressure from clients, which the filings describe in general terms:
- 'there has been increased demand from our clients that all risks associated with foreign exchange fluctuations be borne by us' (S0271);
- 'a growing trend among consumers of IT services towards consolidation of technology service providers in order to improve efficiency and reduce costs' (S0271, S0272);
- benchmarking clauses, under which 'pricing pressure and the resulting decline in rates may lead to a reduction in fees that we charge to clients' (S0272).

The pattern echoes 2001-02 (Chapter 11), when the FY2004 filing reported that 'declines in demand, pricing pressures for our services, appreciation of the rupee against the dollar and increased wage pressures in India' had kept the company from 'historical levels of profitability' (S0270). The big firms bent in both crises without breaking. They slowed hiring, held the bench, froze pay and accepted lower prices. The cost fell on new graduates waiting for offers and on employees whose pay stood still. Press accounts of 2009's deferred joiners have not yet been found.

Attrition was the other strain. Infosys reported 10.5% for fiscal 2004-05 and 13.4% for fiscal 2006-08, while estimating the industry's at 'approximately 20%' (S0264, S0265). In the boom the problem was keeping people. In the crisis the problem disappeared for a while.

### 3.6 The leading firm at scale

The Infosys filings show what 'scale' meant for one company. Total revenues were $1,062.6m in fiscal 2004, of which 98.6% came 'from the export of our products and services out of India' (S0270). By fiscal 2008 revenues were $4,176m, rising to $4,663m in fiscal 2009 and $4,804m in fiscal 2010, with 98.7% still from exports (S0271, S0272). Revenue grew more than fourfold in six years, while the crisis cut growth to a few per cent.

Excluding its BPO subsidiary, Infosys added about 15,200 employees net of attrition in fiscal 2007, 13,700 in fiscal 2008 and 12,400 in fiscal 2009 (S0271). The BPO arm, Progeon, had about 1,900 employees in March 2004 (S0270). It later became Infosys BPO.

The filings also show how much the model still depended on visas. On 31 March 2009 'the majority of our technology professionals in the United States held either H-1B visas (approximately 8,200 persons ...) ... or L-1 visas (approximately 1,400 persons)'. A year later the figures were about 8,900 and 1,800 (S0271, S0272). Those 9,600 visa holders in 2009 compare with about 20,200 employees outside India and 104,900 in all (S0271). They were the narrow top of the onsite-offshore pyramid (Chapter 11), and its exposure to American politics (Chapter 13).

### 3.7 Where the work went

NASSCOM's 2008 deck shows the industry's map by location of delivery (S0107, slide 14):
- Bangalore 36%;
- New Delhi, Noida and Gurgaon 17%;
- Chennai 15%;
- Mumbai and Pune 15%;
- Hyderabad 14%;
- 'Others' 5%.

Its caption, 'The industry is well spread across multiple locations. Tier 2/3 cities emerging', is a claim the figures barely support. Five metropolitan regions held about 95% of the work. The same deck sizes the 'addressable market' at 'at least US$ 380 billion (2007-08)', split between IT and engineering services and BPO (S0107, slide 16). That is a lobby's estimate of opportunity and should be presented only as such.

The breakdown of BPO exports on slide 13 lists 'Customer Interaction & Support', finance and accounting, human resources, procurement and 'knowledge services'. The text extraction is ambiguous, but customer interaction appears to be the largest category; check the original slide before drafting. If confirmed, it means that by 2008 the largest part of Indian BPO was work done by voice and chat: the most scripted, most measured and most easily monitored work in the industry. It is also the work most directly exposed to conversational AI two decades later (Part V).

### 3.8 From STPs to SEZs

The state's support changed form in these years. The Special Economic Zones Act 2005 (S0114; text not yet read) created a new tax regime under section 10AA of the Income-tax Act. The STPI holiday under section 10A, the incentive of the 1990s (Chapters 8-9), was extended and then allowed to expire. Units could claim it only up to assessment year 2011-12, and the register dates its end to 31 March 2011 (S0114, secondary; an ITAT order seen only in a search snippet supports the date). By FY2012 Infosys reported that 'Some of our software development centers located at Chandigarh, Chennai, Hyderabad, Mangalore, Mysore, Pune, Trivandrum and Jaipur currently operate in SEZs and many of our proposed development centers are likely to operate in SEZs' (S0266). Rajaraman counts 257 software and services firms in SEZs by 2007 (S0001; chronology). The shift favoured large firms that could build or lease whole zones, and it moved the industry from the office tower to the walled campus.
