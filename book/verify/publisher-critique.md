# Publisher's critique (2026-10-15)

An acquiring editor's report on the full manuscript of *The Engineer Factory* (v2, body c. 114,650 words). Four readers each read about a quarter of the book in full, as a trade editor would. The coordinator read the prologue and checked the counts. This file brings their reports together.

## Verdict

**Not ready to publish. It is acquirable.** The research is unusually strong and the core argument is original, but the manuscript reads as a verified research report more than a trade book. A publisher would take it on with a revision letter. The revision has three parts:
- a structural edit, cutting about 15-20 per cent;
- a legal read, with a short list of fixes;
- a closing pass on a handful of sources the book leans on but has not read.

None of this means starting again. Ch. 2, 6, 9, 11 and 15 are close to publishable now.

## What would sell it

1. **An original thesis with documentary proof.** India sold engineers' time, so revenue grew with headcount and the factory had to keep hiring. AI shrinks exactly the thing being billed. The book then asks who keeps the saving: the client under time-and-materials, the vendor under fixed price, the parent company in a captive centre. It tracks one Infosys risk-factor sentence from 2024 to 2026 as clients start demanding the saving in writing. Readers called this "the book's best original contribution" and "a real, publishable thesis".
2. **Set pieces a reviewer will quote.** Among them:
   - the air conditioner that delayed TIFRAC;
   - the 60-cycle computer standing in monsoon water at Kanpur;
   - repainted IBM 1401s;
   - the 1968 tripartite fight over office automation;
   - Ahuja's $50 an hour, about half of it kept by HCL;
   - the Infosys "Only 64 brilliant young engineers" advertisement;
   - GE's separate hotel rooms;
   - the 6.20 am bus to Infosys in 2000;
   - Satyam's "riding a tiger";
   - the bench and the 235 billable days;
   - the closing line of Ch. 18: "Now they were being asked, in writing, to promise that the time would shrink."
3. **Foreshadowing that pays off.** The World Bank's 1994 automation warning, the 1986 railway counters (where demand absorbed the productivity gain), and "a firm that finished faster billed less" all seed the AI question without polemic.
4. **Trustworthiness.** The book labels lobby and consultancy figures, separates agreed facts from allegations (the Infosys settlement), and handles caste data with care. It corrects myths by showing the record: "IBM was expelled", "Rajiv Gandhi started IT", "the Cray refusal created PARAM", "1991 created the industry". Reviewers who know the field will notice.

## What stops publication now

### 1. Legal (for counsel before anything else)
- **Satyam (Ch. 16).**
  - Ten people are named as convicted on 9 April 2015, including two PwC partners.
  - The latest status in the book is a June 2022 record saying the appeals were pending.
  - Every named person's current appeal status must be re-checked and stated with a date ("convicted at first instance; appeal pending as of ...").
  - The second-hand B. Rama Raju email ("warning of sacking or demotion") should be dropped, or tied squarely to *Business Today*.
- **Living private individuals shown unflatteringly on a single source.**
  - Mali Subbiah, the Plano body-shop owner (Ch. 13, *Dallas Observer* 1998).
  - Kala Sivasubramanian, sued by TCS (Ch. 6).
  - Two named TCS HR managers in the 2025 retrenchment dispute (Ch. 19). Remove these names.
  - Ramadorai threading customs-seal wires back before inspection (Ch. 4, *The Economist*).
  - For each: anonymise, reduce, or add an explicit "the claims were the reporter's and untested".
- **Murder of Prathibha Srikanth Murthy (Ch. 14).** "The court record of the case has not been read for this book" cannot stand. Read the judgment, state the outcome, and check family-privacy aspects.
- **Allegations against named companies without outcome or response.**
  - Zoho v. Freshworks (Ch. 17): find and state the outcome.
  - The TCS coercion allegations (Ch. 19): put TCS's own statement beside them.
  - The Infosys trainee terminations (prologue): the wording is balanced; keep it that way.
- **Permissions and fair dealing.** Tally the cumulative quotation from these sources and seek permission or paraphrase where it is high:
  - the Computer History Museum oral histories (check CHM's terms of use);
  - the Knowledge@Wharton Hukku interview (Ch. 17);
  - Saxenian, Fuller and Narasimhan, Bassett, Blinder;
  - the *News Minute* report (about a dozen quotations in the prologue);
  - the 1993 Infosys advertisement.

### 2. Unfinished checks visible in the text
Some sentences say a check is pending. They must be closed or removed:
- Ch. 3: a Lok Sabha debate summary "not yet checked against the printed record".
- Ch. 4: Aspray's 1980 figure "not yet checked against official data".
- Ch. 8: the railway trial "not yet checked against the railway record". There is also a dangling quotation: "happy" is attributed to Rajaraman but never quoted.
- Ch. 9: the heading "The engineer factory at scale" collides with Ch. 12's title.

### 3. Key sources leaned on but not read
- **Banerjee, *Computing in the Age of Decolonization* (Princeton, 2026).** It carries the main counter-argument in Ch. 1 and 3, and is known only through reviews. A serious reviewer will not accept that. Read it or reduce it to one sentence.
- **TCS's own statements and quarterly fact sheets (Ch. 18, 19).** The largest employer is missing from the attrition series. "TCS's own statement could not be read for this book, because the company's website was blocked to the research tools used" is the single most damaging sentence in the book. It tells the reader the research was machine-limited at the most important company.
- **Others:**
  - Nilekani and Shah, *Rebooting India*: the Aadhaar half of Ch. 16 has no builders in it.
  - Zoho's history: Zoho is the obvious counter-example in Ch. 17, and it appears only as a plaintiff.
  - The Satyam trial judgment.
  - Nair and Heitzman (Ch. 10).
  - Sharma's two books (Part I and II).

### 4. Authorship and rights (a publisher's first question)
A trade publisher contracts with a named author or rights holder who gives warranties of originality and of having no legal problems. The book has no named author, and the front matter says it was drafted with an AI model. In the US, text generated by AI without enough human authorship may not be protected by copyright. Before submission, decide three things:
- who is the author of record (a name or a pen name);
- what human creative contribution the author can truthfully claim (selection, structure, revision);
- how the disclosure is worded for the contract and the cover copy.

Self-publishing or open publication (the current website) avoids the contract question but not the copyright one.

## The four structural problems

**1. The subject arrives too late.**
- Part I runs about 22,000 words before the book reaches its subject. That is vacuum tubes, the IITs' foreign patrons and import licensing.
- The phrase "by the hour" appears three times in the prologue and Part I together. Ch. 4 even says hourly billing cannot be shown to begin there.
- The prologue never states the bargain. Nasscom's "non-linearity" (jobs growing at 2.3 per cent against revenue at 6.1) is the whole thesis in one word, and it passes without comment.

Fixes:
- Add a plain 150-word paragraph stating the bargain in the prologue.
- Merge Ch. 1 and Ch. 3 ("machines and the state"), cutting about 3,000 words.
- Make Ch. 4's export-obligation licence the climax of Part I. A computer was licensed against twice its value in future programming exported.
- Pull forward the links that are already there:
  - the 1958 Scientific Policy Resolution, "skills which can be exported" (Ch. 1);
  - the 1968 automation committee as a rehearsal for 2026 (Ch. 3);
  - the rule that a software park firm must earn 1.5 times its wage bill in exports (Ch. 10).

**2. The research log is written into the prose.**
- A narrow search finds at least 75 sentences of the "has not been read / not found / not obtained for this book" kind, and the readers counted more variants (21 in Ch. 17 alone).
- Add to that the constant "X writes / Y says / Z's account should be read as one" scaffolding, and 261 "Chapter N" cross-references (34 in the epilogue).
- The front matter already explains the method. In the body this sounds defensive, and where it says "not yet" it sounds unfinished.

Fixes:
- State facts plainly. Flag real uncertainty in a clause. Move adjudication and gaps to the notes.
- Name each main source once per chapter.
- Cut cross-references by about half.
- Say the gap in rank-and-file voices once, strongly, in the front matter, and stop repeating it at chapter ends.
- Give the Anthropic disclosure once in the front matter and once at Claude Code's first mention, and remove the other repeats in Ch. 19 and the epilogue.

**3. Repetition across chapters.** Recurring material includes:
- Murthy's "three years, fifty visits" (Ch. 5, 6, 9);
- the 1984 policy counting onsite work as exports (Ch. 5, 6, 8);
- the MCA degree and NIIT (Ch. 5, 6, 7);
- the Sampath committee, COSL and TI, and Heeks's 10 per cent figure for women;
- the World Bank's "cannibalize" line;
- export series reconciled in four chapters;
- the Infosys recruiting funnel (Ch. 11, 12, 14);
- Saxenian's revenue-per-head material (Ch. 10, 11, 13);
- the Gayatri gate scene (prologue, Ch. 18).

Each recurrence is short. Read in sequence, the chapters feel like overlapping dossiers. One cross-chapter edit should save 3,000-4,000 words.

**4. The ending does not deliver the subtitle.**
- Ch. 19 ends in "open question", and the epilogue in four evenly weighted scenarios with "None of the evidence in this book says which future will come". It is titled "India in 2035" and says it will not predict.
- Readers who bought "What AI Does to That Bargain" will feel short-changed.
- The book need not forecast, but it should commit to an argued judgement. The material is already there, in Ch. 11's "Hours and outcomes", Ch. 15's captive-centre handoff, Ch. 17's Freshworks coda and Ch. 19's risk-factor sentence. For example: *the hourly bargain is ending; what replaces it depends on who owns the saving.*
- Ch. 19 is pinned to October 2026 (three courts, quarterly numbers, broker notes). Rebuild it around its three durable elements:
  - the risk-factor sentence;
  - the pricing mechanism;
  - the entry-level squeeze.
- Move dated events to an "As of autumn 2026" coda, and schedule an update before press.
- Open the epilogue on Gayatri or Rishabh Mishra rather than on the NITI Aayog report.

## Market and positioning (editor's view)

- **Readers.** The Indian English-language trade market (the IT workforce and its families, policy readers), plus a smaller international readership interested in outsourcing and AI and work.
- **Comparable books.** Dinesh C. Sharma's *The Outsourcer* (MIT Press, 2015) is the nearest history. This book's distinctive claim is the pricing-by-the-hour thesis carried into the AI present.
- **The title works.** The subtitle is a promise the ending must keep (see structural problem 4).
- **The website edition** is a reasonable open version now, as long as it is labelled a working draft until the legal items are cleared.

## Recommended revision order

1. Legal fixes and the four structural decisions:
   - merge Ch. 1 and Ch. 3;
   - state the bargain in the prologue;
   - commit to a judgement in the epilogue;
   - give Ch. 19 a dated coda.
2. Close the unfinished checks. Obtain the Banerjee book, the TCS statements and fact sheets, the Satyam appeal status, the Zoho v. Freshworks outcome and the Murthy case judgment.
3. A cross-chapter cut: repetition, source-talk moved to the notes, cross-references halved.
4. A line edit, re-verifying any sentence whose facts change.
5. Permissions tally; decide authorship of record; final rebuild.
