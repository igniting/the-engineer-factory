Central questions (from the brief): 1 services not hardware/products; 2 education and industry shaping each other; 3 who became an engineer and what it did to lives and cities; 4 the state; 5 migration and diaspora; 6 the business model and the craft; 7 AI and what replaces the model. Every chapter touches several; the column names those it must answer explicitly.

## Prologue: The joining date (2026)
A fresher waiting for an onboarding date that keeps moving, set against an industry reporting $315bn revenue (Nasscom FY26, S0140) and TCS onboarding 10,000 freshers in a quarter while cutting its annual target (S0138). **Condition:** the waiting fresher must be a named person in published reporting, or a documented case in a filed complaint (NITES, S0133, labelled as a party's account). If no such named case is found, open instead on a documented public moment (e.g. a results-day statement or a labour-department hearing) and say plainly that the waiting freshers are known through union figures and reporting. Myth tested: M30.

## Part I: Before software (1940s-1977)

**1. The statistician's machines (1931-1966).** Mahalanobis, ISI and the computer as a planning tool (HEC-2M 1955, URAL 1958, Cold War bargaining); Bhabha, Narasimhan and TIFRAC; CDC 3600 at TIFR. Education: who learned to program the first machines and how (a dozen people at ISI; TIFR's in-house group). Profession: computing as government science, not a job market. Ends on the gap between building one machine and building an industry. Myths: M13. Sources: S0001, S0007, S0008, S0010, S0012-S0015, S0029. Sidebar: *Why did the Soviet Union, with more engineers, fail to network its computers?* (S0165), used briefly as a contrast in state-led computing.

**2. Kanpur, 1963 (1946-1978).** The Sarker report, the IITs and their four foreign patrons; the IBM 1620's arrival and Huskey's night classes; KIAP; Rajaraman and the 1965 CS M.Tech option; India's first compiler (1970); the first B.Tech in CS (1978); the start of the brain drain (Sukhatme). Education-led chapter. Myths: M14. Sources: S0003, S0004, S0030, S0031, S0032, S0043, S0046, S0049, S0050.

**3. Self-reliance and its limits (1962-1977).** The 1962 war and the Bhabha Committee; DoE (1970) and the Electronics Commission; ECIL's 98 computers; import licensing; LIC, the automation committee and union resistance; NCSDCT and NIC. Who the state trained and employed. Ends with the 1977 election. Myths: M28. Sources: S0001, S0014, S0018-S0025, S0028, S0040, S0041.

**4. Tata's experiment (1968-1978).** TCS as a Tata computer-services unit; Kohli; the Burroughs deal and the export obligation (exports to pay for imports: the first link between foreign exchange and software work); Patni's Data Conversion; early offshore work. Profession-led: the first Indian programmers paid to work for foreign clients. Myths: M06, M01 (partial). Sources: S0036-S0039, S0044, S0045, S0001.

## Part II: Bodies and wires (1977-1991)

**5. The year IBM left (1973-1984).** FERA and the 40% rule; IBM's choice to leave (1978); CMC; the minicomputer policy and the private makers (HCL, Wipro, DCM); Unix-trained programmers; the hardware path that faded. Myths: M03, M23. Sources: S0001, S0026, S0027, S0055, S0056, S0066, S0069, S0081, S0082. Sidebar: *Why did Korea and Taiwan make chips while India wrote code?* (S0166, S0167, S0163).

**6. Body shopping (1978-1991).** Programmers sent to client sites abroad; contracts, visas (B-1, L-1), pay and lives; how firms priced people by the hour; Infosys founded (1981) and its first years; the economics of exporting labour when importing computers was hard. Myths: M15. Sources: S0034, S0035, S0057, S0058, S0070, S0071, S0073, S0085, S0089, S0210. Gap flagged: contemporaneous 1980s reporting on body shopping not yet found.

**7. The training economy (1980-1995).** MCA courses (1980) and the Rajaraman manpower panel; NIIT (1981) and Aptech (1986) and franchised training; capitation-fee colleges in Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Andhra; AICTE Act (1987); Mohini Jain (1992) and Unni Krishnan (1993); JEE coaching. Education-led. Sources: S0001, S0016, S0063-S0065, S0074-S0076, S0083.

**8. Satellite and permit (1984-1991).** The 1984 and 1986 policies; TI's satellite link (1985); railway reservations (1985-86) and the change in public perception; bank unions' 'anti-computerisation year' (1984); C-DOT, C-DAC and PARAM; the Cray episode; Nasscom (1988); the first STP (1990). Retitled from 'Rajiv's computers' (see log). Myths: M01, M04, M05, M21. Sources: S0001, S0059-S0062, S0067, S0068, S0078-S0080.

## Part III: The export machine (1991-2008)

**9. 1991 (1990-1997).** The crisis; STPI (June 1991) and the budget's software tax concession (July 1991); capital-market reform and the IPOs (Infosys 1993); telecom policy (1994); exports from $128m to $1.76bn. Myths: M22, M24, M16. Sources: S0001, S0034, S0048, S0088, S0089, S0091.

**10. Bangalore (1947-2010).** Why Bangalore (defence public sector, IISc, tenancy laws, TI), what the industry did to the city (land, traffic, housing, inequality); Hyderabad's HITEC City and Microsoft; Pune, Chennai, NCR. Profession and lives. Myths: M17. Sources: S0034, S0061, S0069, S0101, S0102, S0119, S0195, S0095. Sidebar: *Why did Ireland get the factories and India the programmers?* (S0162, S0163).

**11. Y2K and Nasdaq (1995-2002).** The GE price dispute (1995); CMM and process; Y2K and euro conversion; the global delivery model; Nasdaq listings (Satyam Infoway, Infosys, Rediff); ESOP wealth; the dot-com bust. Business-model chapter. Myths: M02, M26. Sources: S0034, S0089, S0090, S0094, S0108, S0109, S0121, S0179, S0199.

**12. The engineer factory (1998-2012).** The explosion of private engineering colleges (135,000 to 1.3 million students, S0001); campus mass hiring and training campuses (Mysore); the pyramid, bench and utilisation; who got hired and who did not (caste, class, gender, language, region); women in IT. Education and profession together; the book's title chapter. Myths: M07, M08, M09, M18. Sources: S0001, S0095, S0096, S0115, S0117, S0118, S0151-S0153, S0211-S0213. Sidebar: *Why did Britain lose its computer workforce?* (S0164), only if the gender argument needs it.

**13. H-1B (1990-2015).** The 1990 Immigration Act and the H-1B; Indians' share of approvals; outsourcing firms as sponsors; students and OPT; Silicon Valley Indians, TiE, returnees and brain circulation; what migration did to families and to the industry at home. Myths: M11, M12. Sources: S0046, S0097-S0100, S0104, S0105, S0116, S0158, S0159, S0172. Sidebar: *Did Israel's diaspora and army do for its software industry what the US did for India's?* (S0163).

**14. Backlash and scale (2002-2011).** US offshoring politics (2003-04 laws, Dobbs, Blinder); BPO and the voice-call era; SEZs (2005) and the end of STPI tax holidays (2011); attrition; the 2008 crisis. Sources: S0110-S0114, S0106, S0107. Sidebar: *Why did the Philippines win the call centres?* (S0168).

## Part IV: Beyond services? (1985-2024)

**15. The other employers (1985-2024).** *New chapter.* Foreign companies' Indian centres as a parallel engineering profession: TI (1985), Motorola, Oracle (1994), SAP and Microsoft (1998), Intel, Google (2004), the Amex and GE captives, banks and retailers, Amazon's Hyderabad campus; Cognizant and Accenture with most of their staff in India; MNC R&D centres (594 by 2008) becoming 'GCCs' (1,700+ by FY24); state GCC policies. Different pay, work and career ladders from the services firms. Myths: M25. Sources: S0061, S0110, S0141, S0170, S0192-S0207. Sidebar: *Why did Eastern Europe become a nearshore rival, and why couldn't it scale?* (S0170, S0171).

**16. Fraud and foundation (2008-2018).** Satyam's confession (2009), prosecution and the governance lesson; then the state as builder: Aadhaar (2010), privacy and the Supreme Court (2017-18), the people who moved from IT firms into public infrastructure. Retitled from 'Satyam and Aadhaar' (see log). Myths: M27. Sources: S0122-S0128.

**17. The product question (1986-2024).** Why so few products: Tally, i-flex/FLEXCUBE, Finacle, Zoho, Freshworks; the iSPIRT break with Nasscom (2013); the funding boom and bust (2021 peak; Byju's); UPI, India Stack and Jio as platforms for builders. Myths: M29. Sources: S0129, S0130, S0147, S0173-S0190. Sidebar: *How did China build domestic platforms India did not?* (S0169 and to find).

**18. The pandemic boom (2020-2023).** Record hiring and attrition; remote work; moonlighting (Wipro, 2022); the first visible unions (KITU, NITES); fresher offers delayed and revoked. Sources: S0131-S0134, S0160, S0161.

## Revision log

### v1 (2026-10-12), changes from v0 and reasons

1. **New Chapter 15, 'The other employers'.** Phase 1 (batch 5c) found a continuous thread of foreign-owned engineering centres from TI in 1985 to 1,700+ GCCs in FY24 (S0061, S0207, S0141), and foreign-registered firms with most staff in India (Cognizant: c. 241,500 of 336,800, S0192). v0 split this across Ch. 8 (TI), 14 (captives) and 16 (GCCs), which would hide a second career path that matters to central questions 1 and 6, and makes myth M25 ('GCCs are new') hard to test. The author also asked for this material (batch 5c).
2. **Chapters renumbered 15-19**; the book now has 19 chapters. To stay within 130,000 words, most chapter targets were cut by 500 words (total 129,000).
3. **Ch. 8 retitled 'Satellite and permit'** (was 'Rajiv's computers'). Myth M04 is provisionally MIXED: the 1984/86 policies mattered but Rajaraman places break points at 1970, 1978, 1991 and 1998 (S0001). A title crediting one leader would assert what the chapter must test.
4. **Ch. 16 retitled 'Fraud and foundation'** (was 'Satyam and Aadhaar'), to name the argument (the state as builder after a private-sector failure) rather than two topics.
5. **Prologue titled 'The joining date'**, with an explicit fallback if no named fresher can be documented (honesty rule: no composites). Delayed onboarding is documented from 2022 (S0133, S0134), so the prologue must not imply it began with AI (M30).
6. **Ch. 2 extended to 1978**, the year IIT Kanpur started the first undergraduate CS programme (S0001), so the education story closes on degree teaching rather than on KIAP's end.
7. **Ch. 3 and 5 kept separate, boundary fixed at 1977/78.** They overlap on the DoE, but Rajaraman's break points (1970 and 1978, S0001) support two distinct periods: state monopoly (ECIL) then the opening after IBM's exit.
8. **Ch. 6 and 13 kept separate, boundary at 1990.** Ch. 6 covers the pre-H-1B on-site era (B-1/L-1; body shopping) and Infosys's founding; Ch. 13 starts with the 1990 Immigration Act.
9. **Ch. 10 period widened to 1947-2010**, because Murthy's own account ties Bangalore's talent pool to post-1947 defence siting (S0034).
10. **Ch. 12 moved to 1998-2012**, matching Rajaraman's numbers on the engineering-college explosion (S0001) and AICTE/AISHE series.
11. **Ch. 17 starts in 1986** (Tally) so the product story is not framed as post-2008 only (S0178, S0176).
12. **Sidebars placed** (one per chapter at most): USSR (Ch. 1), Korea/Taiwan (Ch. 5), Ireland (Ch. 10), UK (Ch. 12, conditional), Israel (Ch. 13), Philippines (Ch. 14), Eastern Europe (Ch. 15), China (Ch. 17). US comparisons run through the text rather than in a sidebar.
13. **Standing requirement for rank-and-file voices** added, because the people index is dominated by founders, officials and scholars (Phase 1c finding).

## Title proposals (Phase 1d)

1. **The Engineer Factory: How India Built Software Engineering, and What AI Does to It** (working title). Names the education-and-industry machine that is the book's spine; strong and memorable. Risk: 'factory' may read as dismissive of engineers; the book must earn it in Ch. 12.
2. **Priced by the Hour: How India Made Software a Profession, and Why AI Threatens the Model.** States the thesis directly (billing by the hour, growth by headcount). Risk: sounds like a business book; undersells the 1950s-80s history.
3. **Bodies and Wires: The Making of India's Software Engineers.** Uses the Part II phrase; evocative of the move from on-site labour to networks. Risk: obscure without context.
4. **The Long Shift: India, Software and the End of the Billable Hour.** Plays on night shifts and time zones. Risk: 'end of' prejudges the AI question the book leaves open.
5. **Seven Founders, Five Million Engineers: How India Built a Profession.** Concrete numbers; but centres one firm (Infosys) and needs updated figures.

**Recommendation:** keep **The Engineer Factory** as the title, with a subtitle that states the thesis more exactly: *The Engineer Factory: How India Priced Its Engineers by the Hour, and What AI Does to That Bargain*. The main title carries both halves of the book (education and work); the subtitle carries the argument. Final choice is the author's.
