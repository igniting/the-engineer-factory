# Prologue. The joining date (2026)

On Thursday 6 February 2025 a 24-year-old trainee at the Infosys training campus in Mysuru, whom the reporter who told her story for *The News Minute* called Gayatri (not her real name), received an email summoning her to a meeting the next day.[1] The email concerned the assessment held at the end of her training, which she had failed a month earlier. It said: 'You are required to maintain confidentiality, hence do not discuss this, or share this calendar with anyone.'[2] 'I was devastated after receiving the email,' she told the reporter. Her seniors, she said, had explained that such an email meant she would be asked to leave.[3]

The next morning, according to her account, security staff at the campus entrance took her electronic devices and her bag, as they did with the other trainees summoned that day. She was taken into a cabin, where a member of the human resources staff asked her to sign a 'mutual separation agreement'.[4] The report says she was coerced. The agreement itself said the opposite: 'You have knowingly and voluntarily chosen to enter into this agreement and have not been forced or pressured in any way to sign it.'[5] She signed. 'I signed it unwillingly. I did not want to quit the company. They wasted two and a half years of my career. It was my dream job,' she said.[6]

What followed took a few hours. The company took back the trainees' laptops, put them on a bus to their accommodation and told them to leave the premises by 5 pm that day.[7] Gayatri, the report says, had no time to arrange travel, so she booked a hotel for the night before going home to Andhra Pradesh.[8] The separation payment Infosys offered was one month's salary, which the reporter put at Rs 25,000.[9] Another trainee, given the name Raju, described the day in six words: 'We were thrown on the road.'[10] A third, given the name Rishi, aged 25, said he had waited nearly two years to be onboarded and had been removed after four months: 'I regret joining the company, they have traumatised me.'[11]

The two and a half years she spoke of had begun with success. Gayatri graduated in 2022 and in the same year cleared Infosys's interview for the post of system engineer, the company's entry-level job for new graduates.[12] Then nothing happened. In the reporter's words she 'was left hanging for nearly two years' and was onboarded in October 2024 'only after numerous emails and a legal intervention'.[13] From July 2024 she and others in her position took part in online 'pre-training', for which they were not paid. 'Pre-training was unheard of earlier,' she said.[14] The unpaid sessions and assessments ran for four weeks in July and six weeks in August, before any of the graduates was an employee.[15]

The offer had mattered beyond her. 'My parents and teachers were very proud when I got the offer letter from Infosys,' she told the reporter. After she failed the test three times, she said, her parents asked whether she had cheated in her college exams.[16] Her own summary of what the termination did was brief: 'The termination has taken away all my self-esteem and confidence.'[17]

This book is about the system that made her offer letter worth waiting two years for. For half a century India built a machine for turning young people into software engineers: colleges that taught them, companies that hired them by the thousand and trained them on campuses like the one at Mysuru, and an export trade that paid for it all. Her story shows that machine from the bottom, at the moment when it seemed to need fewer of the people it had been built to produce.

## The waiting

Gayatri was not alone, and her wait did not begin with her. In October 2022 *Business Today* reported that freshers placed by Accenture, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech were seeing their joining dates pushed back, in some cases into the following year.[18] One graduate told the magazine that the company had postponed the date again two days before joining: 'I had packed my bags and booked all tickets to move to the new city.'[19] By June 2024 the Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), a union of IT workers based in Pune, claimed that more than 10,000 graduates were still waiting for joining dates from Wipro, Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), Zensar and LTIMindtree, a figure no company confirmed.[20]

Infosys's case is the best documented, because NITES took it to the government. On 20 August 2024 the union complained to the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment about more than 2,000 campus recruits from 2022 who had not been onboarded.[21] According to NITES, Infosys issued about 2,000 offer letters on 2 and 3 September with joining dates in October.[22] Infosys told the state's labour department that the union's allegations were 'patently false' and that the graduates had already received joining dates.[23]

The recruits arrived in Mysuru that October.[24] *Republic Business* reported that most had joined under the National Apprenticeship Training Scheme, a government programme for on-the-job training, which would matter later.[25] Trainees told the reporter that the pass mark had risen to 65 per cent from 50 per cent in earlier years, and that the syllabus had grown, to 200 hours of material by one magazine's account.[26] A trainee given the name Rohan said the earlier batches had been treated leniently: 'The fact that they made it difficult for us shows that, since the beginning, they never wanted to hire us.'[27] By the report's count, which is not a company figure, of 930 who joined in one October intake, 160 passed at the first attempt and 140 at the second.[28]

## Two accounts

Infosys's account deserves to be stated at its strongest. In a statement quoted in the report and by *Deccan Herald*, the company said that all freshers, after foundational training at Mysuru, are expected to clear internal assessments. 'All freshers get three attempts to clear the assessment, failing which they will not be able to continue with the organisation, as is also mentioned in their contract,' it said, adding that the process had been in place 'for over two decades' and ensured 'a high quality of talent availability for our clients'.[29] On this view the test is quality control, in a training system that, later chapters argue, the firms built because colleges did not teach what they needed. In a later round the termination email listed what the company had provided: 'additional preparation time, doubt-clearing sessions, several mock assessments and three attempts'.[30] Infosys offered those let go help with finding other work, courses through the training firms NIIT and UpGrad, and a route into its business-process arm.[31]

The trainees and the union saw a company reducing a batch it no longer wanted. *Outlook Business* reported that termination rates at the assessment had risen from under 10 per cent to between 30 and 40 per cent, and unnamed current Infosys employees told the magazine that 'an excess of resources and fewer projects' lay behind it.[32] The two accounts are not simply opposed: the process could be old, as the company said, and the bar could have been raised, as the trainees said. Alagunambi Welkin, general secretary of the Union of IT and ITES Employees, said that companies hire large numbers 'as a result of poor decision making by the management' and, when money is short, 'sack the employees instead of taking accountability'.[33]

Even the number let go is disputed: Infosys said about 300 in February, the report estimated 400, and NITES, in its complaint to the Labour Ministry, said about 700.[34] The departures did not stop. By one count in the financial press, about 35 more trainees went in March, 240 on 18 April and 195 later that month, almost 800 since February.[35]

The state did not agree with itself. On 13 February 2025 the Union Labour Ministry directed Karnataka's Labour Department to take 'urgent necessary actions to resolve the dispute'.[36] But the Karnataka department, according to an unnamed source quoted by the financial news site Moneycontrol, concluded that no layoff had occurred at all: 'They were all apprenticeship trainees, not employees.'[37] If that was right, the law on retrenchment did not apply. NITES then complained to the skills ministry that the apprenticeship law itself had been broken, and no outcome has been reported.[38] Whether the trainees were wronged in law is unsettled. What is clear is that two levels of the Indian state disagreed about whether this was a labour question at all.

## The same years, from the top

From the top, the same years looked like growth. In February 2026 Nasscom (the National Association of Software and Service Companies), the industry's lobby group, reported that India's technology industry would earn $315 billion in the year to March 2026, up 6.1 per cent, with 5.95 million direct employees after 135,000 net additions.[39] Its president, Rajesh Nambiar, acknowledged that jobs were growing more slowly than revenue, 2.3 per cent against 6.1, a gap he called a non-linearity.[40]

That word, in this book's reading, names the bargain on which the whole industry was built, at the moment it began to come apart. For half a century Indian software firms sold, above all, the time of their engineers. A firm that sells time can grow only by selling more of it, so revenue rose with headcount, and the factory had to keep hiring graduates like Gayatri by the tens of thousands. Colleges, training campuses and offer letters were all arranged around that fact. A non-linearity is the opposite: revenue rising faster than the number of people needed to earn it. A gap of 2.3 per cent against 6.1 is small, but it runs against the logic of everything that came before. Artificial intelligence tools press on the bargain at its centre, because what they promise to shrink is the time it takes to write and test code, and that time is exactly what was being billed.

The largest firm shows how unsettled the picture still is. In April 2026 K. Krithivasan, chief executive of TCS, said the company had made 25,000 offers to freshers for the coming year and that clarity on demand would lead to more hiring, and by the end of September, by the company's figures as reported, about 24,000 freshers had joined in the year's first two quarters.[41]

The factory, then, had not stopped. The waits that Gayatri and thousands of others endured began in 2022, in what the report called the 2022 lull in the IT industry.[42] Infosys's annual reports to the American regulator mentioned AI in general terms before then, but did not mention AI code generation until the one for the year to March 2024, filed in June 2024.[43] So the first answer to the obvious question, whether artificial intelligence caused all this, is that the waits began about two years before AI code generation appeared in Infosys's own account of the risks to its business. That does not make the question smaller. On this evidence the bargain was already under strain when the tools arrived, and the tools work on the very hours the bargain sold. How much they have added since is weighed in Chapter 19, and it is the question that makes the history worth telling.

## The question

The question this book asks is how India came to build software engineering, as an education and as a profession, and what AI does to what was built. Gayatri's offer letter was the product of a long chain of decisions, made in planning offices and boardrooms, in classrooms, on export contracts that priced Indian programmers by the hour, and in visa offices abroad. The training campus where her career ended is one of the last links. It exists because colleges and companies spent decades arguing over who should teach the engineer what the job required.

The chain starts long before there was an industry, in two research institutes where computers were scientific instruments and nobody yet thought of programming as a job. In 1955, the computer scientist V. Rajaraman records in his history of Indian computing, only a few dozen scientists and engineers in India knew about computers; by 2010 more than 2.4 million people were employed in computer-related jobs.[44] Between those two numbers lies a bargain that held for half a century: India came to train its engineers by the hundred thousand and sold their time to the world. Gayatri's offer letter was a promise that the selling would go on. Whether it does, now that machines can shorten the hours themselves, will decide what the next offer letter is worth, and whether a joining date comes with it.
