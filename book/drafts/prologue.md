# Prologue. The joining date (2026)

On Thursday 6 February 2025 a 24-year-old trainee at the Infosys training campus in Mysuru received an email summoning her to a meeting the next day. The reporters who told her story called her Gayatri. That is not her real name. It is the pseudonym that Balakrishna Ganeshan's report for *The News Minute*, republished by *Newslaundry*, gave her, and everything this book knows about her comes from that one report.[1] The email concerned the assessment held at the end of her training, which she had failed a month earlier. It said: 'You are required to maintain confidentiality, hence do not discuss this, or share this calendar with anyone.'[2] 'I was devastated after receiving the email,' she told the reporter. Her seniors, she said, had explained that such an email meant she would be asked to leave.[3]

The next morning, according to her account, security staff at the campus entrance took her electronic devices and her bag, as they did with the other trainees summoned that day. She was taken into a cabin, where a member of the human resources staff asked her to sign a 'mutual separation agreement'.[4] The report says she was coerced. The agreement itself said the opposite: 'You have knowingly and voluntarily chosen to enter into this agreement and have not been forced or pressured in any way to sign it.'[5] She signed. 'I signed it unwillingly. I did not want to quit the company. They wasted two and a half years of my career. It was my dream job,' she said.[6]

What followed took a few hours. The company took back the trainees' laptops, put them on a bus to their accommodation and told them to leave the premises by 5 pm that day.[7] (A *Deccan Herald* headline gave the deadline as 6 pm.)[8] Gayatri, the report says, had no time to arrange travel, so she booked a hotel for the night before going home to Andhra Pradesh.[9] The separation payment Infosys offered was one month's salary, which the reporter put at Rs 25,000.[10] Another trainee, given the name Raju, described the day in five words: 'We were thrown on the road.'[11] A third, called Rishi, aged 25, said he had waited nearly two years to be onboarded and had been removed after four months: 'I regret joining the company, they have traumatised me.'[12]

The two and a half years she spoke of had begun with success. Gayatri graduated in 2022 and in the same year cleared Infosys's interview for the post of system engineer, the company's entry-level job for new graduates.[13] Then nothing happened. In the reporter's words she 'was left hanging for nearly two years' and was onboarded in October 2024 'only after numerous emails and a legal intervention'.[14] From July 2024 she and others in her position took part in online 'pre-training', for which they were not paid. 'Pre-training was unheard of earlier,' she said.[15] *Deccan Herald* reported that the unpaid sessions and assessments ran for four weeks in July and six weeks in August, before any of the graduates was an employee.[16]

The offer had mattered beyond her. 'My parents and teachers were very proud when I got the offer letter from Infosys,' she told the reporter. After she failed the test three times, she said, her parents asked whether she had cheated in her college exams.[17] Her own summary of what the termination did was brief: 'The termination has taken away all my self-esteem and confidence.'[18]

This book is about the system that made her offer letter worth waiting two years for. For half a century India built a machine for turning young people into software engineers: colleges that taught them, companies that hired them by the thousand and trained them on campuses like the one at Mysuru, and an export trade that paid for it all. Her story shows that machine from the bottom, at the moment when it seemed to need fewer of the people it had been built to produce.

## The waiting

Gayatri was not alone, and her wait did not begin with her. In October 2022 *Business Today* reported that freshers placed by Accenture, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech were seeing their joining dates, the day a graduate who has accepted an offer is told to report for work, pushed back, in some cases into the following year.[19] One graduate told the magazine that the company had postponed the date again two days before joining: 'I had packed my bags and booked all tickets to move to the new city.'[20] The graduates in that reporting were anonymous. By June 2024 the Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), a union of IT workers based in Pune, claimed that more than 10,000 graduates were still waiting for joining dates from Wipro, Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), Zensar and LTIMindtree.[21] That is a union's estimate, and no company confirmed it. The same report said, citing other reports, that Infosys had hired only 11,900 new graduates in the year to March 2024, against 50,000 the year before.[22] Infosys's own filing with the American securities regulator gives a different measure for that year: 26,975 offers of employment of all kinds, from 2,436,929 applications.[23]

Infosys's case is the best documented, because NITES took it to the government. On 20 August 2024 the union complained to the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment about more than 2,000 campus recruits from 2022 who had not been onboarded.[24] The ministry's regional office wrote to the Karnataka government on 3 September.[25] According to NITES, Infosys issued about 2,000 offer letters on 2 and 3 September with joining dates in October.[26] Infosys, for its part, told the state's labour department that the union's allegations were 'patently false' and that the graduates had already received joining dates.[27] *Deccan Herald* reported at the start of October that about 500 of the group were still waiting for confirmation.[28]

The recruits arrived in Mysuru that month. The trainees who spoke to the reporters gave the joining date as 7 October; a trainee quoted by *Republic Business* gave 21 October, and there may have been two batches.[29] *Republic* reported that most had joined under the National Apprenticeship Training Scheme, a government programme for on-the-job training, which would matter later.[30] Their training covered Java, a programming language, and database management, with two tests at the end. Trainees told the reporters that the pass mark had risen to 65 per cent from 50 per cent in earlier years, and that the syllabus had grown.[31] *Outlook Business* reported the same changes, with 200 hours of material.[32] A trainee given the name Rohan said the earlier batches had been treated leniently: 'The fact that they made it difficult for us shows that, since the beginning, they never wanted to hire us.'[33] The trainees' own count was that of 930 who joined on 7 October, 160 passed at the first attempt and 140 at the second.[34] That is their claim, not a company figure.

## Two accounts

Infosys gave a different account, and it deserves to be stated at its strongest. In a statement quoted by both *The News Minute* and *Deccan Herald*, the company said that all freshers, after foundational training at Mysuru, are expected to clear internal assessments. 'All freshers get three attempts to clear the assessment, failing which they will not be able to continue with the organisation, as is also mentioned in their contract,' it said, adding that the process had been in place 'for over two decades' and ensured 'a high quality of talent availability for our clients'.[35] On this view the test is the quality control of a training system that the large firms built because colleges did not teach what they needed, a system later chapters describe. In a later round the termination email listed what the company had provided: 'additional preparation time, doubt-clearing sessions, several mock assessments and three attempts'.[36] Infosys offered those let go help with finding other work, courses through the training firms NIIT and UpGrad, and a route into its business-process arm.[37] About 250 of those let go enrolled in the courses and about 150 registered for help in finding jobs elsewhere, according to Moneycontrol.[38]

The trainees and the union saw a company reducing a batch it no longer wanted. *Outlook Business*, citing the financial news site Moneycontrol, reported that termination rates at the assessment had risen from under 10 per cent to between 30 and 40 per cent, and current Infosys employees, unnamed, told the magazine that 'an excess of resources and fewer projects' lay behind it.[39] The two accounts are not simply opposed. The firm's process could be old, as it said, and the bar could have been raised, as the trainees said. The evidence available does not settle which mattered more. Alagunambi Welkin, general secretary of the Union of IT and ITES Employees, put the union case in general terms. Companies, he said, hire large numbers 'as a result of poor decision making by the management' and, when money is short, 'sack the employees instead of taking accountability'.[40]

Even the number of people let go is disputed. Infosys said about 300 had left in February; NITES, in its complaint to the Labour Ministry, said about 700.[41] The *News Minute* report itself opened with an estimate of 400.[42] The departures did not stop. By the count of Moneycontrol, relayed by *Business Today*, about 35 more trainees went in March, 240 on 18 April and 195 later that month, almost 800 since February.[43] *Republic Business* put the 18 April figure at 370.[44]

The state did not agree with itself about what had happened. On 13 February 2025 the Union Labour Ministry directed Karnataka's Labour Department to take 'urgent necessary actions to resolve the dispute'.[45] NITES's complaint described recruits 'who had already suffered a delay of two years in their joining after being issued offer letters'.[46] But the Karnataka department, according to an unnamed source quoted by Moneycontrol, concluded that no layoff had occurred at all: 'They were all apprenticeship trainees, not employees.'[47] If that was right, the law on retrenchment did not apply. No written order has been found. NITES then complained to the skills ministry that the apprenticeship law itself had been broken, and no outcome has been reported.[48] The book cannot say whether the trainees were wronged in law. It can say that two levels of the Indian state disagreed about whether this was a labour question at all.

## The same years, from the top

From the top, the same years looked like growth. In February 2026 Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, reported that India's technology industry would earn $315 billion in the year to March 2026, up 6.1 per cent, with 5.95 million direct employees after 135,000 net additions.[49] Its president, Rajesh Nambiar, acknowledged that jobs were growing more slowly than revenue, 2.3 per cent against 6.1, a gap he called a non-linearity; the wire report of the announcement mentioned the changes being brought by artificial intelligence platforms.[50] Nasscom also said that more than 2 million of those employees had been trained in AI skills.[51] Infosys said in its annual filing with the American securities regulator that in the same year it had received 5,884,480 job applications and extended 87,286 offers of employment, adding that it had introduced AI into its recruitment process.[52]

TCS, the largest of the firms, is the hardest case to read. In April 2026 its chief executive, K. Krithivasan, told the Press Trust of India: 'We have made 25,000 offers for freshers in FY27. Clarity on demand will lead to more hiring.'[53] He explained the caution in terms of training: a fresher, he said, needs about nine months of it before working on projects, while an experienced hire contributes at once.[54] *Business Standard* framed the 25,000 as the year's target against a usual figure of about 40,000.[55] *Business Today* reported it as the offers made so far, with more to depend on demand, and said the company had reportedly hired more than 44,000 freshers in the previous year.[56] The second reading is closer to Krithivasan's words. By the end of September 2026, according to the company's figures as reported, about 14,000 freshers had joined in the first quarter of the year and 10,000 in the second.[57] Its total headcount stood at 598,056, up 4,258 in the quarter.[58] 'We are hiring for demand, and we definitely see good demand for these technologies, so we will continue to hire,' its chief human resources officer, Sudeep Kunnumal, told analysts on 8 October 2026.[59]

These numbers complicate any simple story in which the factory has stopped. In 2026 most of the largest firm's freshers were joining, and the industry as a whole still added people. The waits that Gayatri and thousands of others endured began in 2022, in the slowdown that followed the pandemic hiring boom, before AI coding agents, programs that write and test code with little human direction, were in wide use.[60] So the first answer to the obvious question, whether artificial intelligence caused all this, is that the joining date became uncertain before the machines arrived. How much AI has added since is a question for the last chapter of this history, where the evidence is weighed. It is also the question that makes the history worth telling.

## The question

The question this book asks is how India came to build software engineering at all, as an education and as a profession, and what AI does to what was built. Gayatri's offer letter was the product of a long chain of decisions. Some were made in planning offices and some in boardrooms. Others were made in classrooms that taught a particular kind of engineer, on export contracts that priced Indian programmers by the hour, and in visa offices abroad. Each chapter follows one link in that chain, and each asks who was taught what, by whom, and what work they did afterwards, for what pay and in which cities. The training campus where her career ended is one of the last links. It exists because colleges and companies spent decades arguing over who should teach the engineer what the job required.

The chain starts long before there was an industry, in two research institutes where computers were scientific instruments and nobody yet thought of programming as a job. In 1955, Rajaraman's history of Indian computing records, only a few dozen scientists and engineers in India knew about computers; by 2010 more than 2.4 million people were employed in computer-related jobs.[61] In Calcutta, the Indian Statistical Institute, founded in 1931 by the statistician Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis, wanted machines to do the arithmetic of national planning.[62] In August 1955 a small British computer, the HEC-2M, started working there, installed by two of the institute's own engineers from their notes because it arrived without manuals; a Soviet URAL followed in 1958.[63] 'Around a dozen people learnt to program the computer,' the computer scientist V. Rajaraman wrote of the first machine.[64] In Bombay, at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, a team of about six young physicists, none of whom had ever used a computer, set out in the same year to build one.[65] India's engineer factory began with those few dozen people, and with the question of whether the country should build its machines or buy them.
