# Chapter 17. The product question (1986-2024)

In 1992 Rajesh Hukku, then head of Citicorp Overseas Software, the bank's in-house programming unit in India, spun the unit off from the bank as a company of its own. Fifteen years later, in an interview with Ravi Aron of the Wharton School published in March 2007, he remembered one of the Citibank chiefs asking him, at the start, a blunt question: 'Why should I start the one-thousand-and-first software company in India?'[1] His answer, as he recalled it, was that the new firm would be the first to be wholly focused on understanding its customers' business, so that no banker could say of its staff, 'These guys are great techies, but they don't understand my business.'[2]

In Hukku's account, Citi had decided that writing software for sale was not its business. It invested $400,000 in the venture anyway, and the unit became Citicorp Information Technology Industries Ltd. (CITIL), later renamed i-flex solutions.[3] Hukku described the bargain in two halves. 'We like you guys,' he recalled the bank saying, but it would not put in 'management mind-share' because selling software was not its core business. There was a second understanding: 'Please don't come back asking for business from Citibank.'[4] In hindsight, he said, it was the best combination, 'because you get the money and you don't get controlled'.[5]

The question the Citibank chief put to Hukku is the question of this chapter, turned round. By 1992 India already had hundreds of software companies, and almost all of them sold the same thing: the time of Indian engineers, billed to foreign clients by the hour, the month or the project. A software product is something different. It is a program that a firm owns and sells many times over, so that the cost of writing it is paid once and the revenue keeps coming. The country that became the world's largest supplier of software labour built remarkably few products of its own. Why that happened, and what changed when it did, is the subject here.

The question matters for this book beyond national pride. A firm that owns a product keeps the gains when its engineers become more productive. A firm that sells hours must negotiate with its clients over who keeps them. That difference decides how the tools of artificial intelligence, which make a programmer's hour more productive, land on the two kinds of firm. It is the subject of Chapter 19. The story of India's products begins earlier than Hukku's spin-off, in a family business in the 1980s, and runs through a foreign bank's captive, a product nested inside the largest services company, a quarrel inside the industry's lobby, a warehouse in Chennai and a state-built payments network.

## An accounting package for small business

The longest-lived Indian software product grew from the home market. According to the history page on its own website, Tally Solutions began in 1986 as 'Peutronics Financial Accountant', founded by Bharat Goenka, 'inspired by his father, Shyam Sundar Goenka's vision to simplify business operations'.[6] The company's profile of Bharat Goenka says he established the firm, then known as Peutronics, 'under the guidance of his father', and that it grew 'from an accounting package for SMEs', small and medium enterprises, into business software for firms of all sizes.[7]

The company's timeline is short and is the only account of its early years in the sources read for this chapter. In 1988, it says, it 'Launched Tally 3.0, pioneering code-less accounting'. In 1991 'Tally Solutions Private Limited was officially established'.[8] The company register used for this book records an incorporation on 8 November 1991 and a change of name to Tally Solutions in 1999, which may mean that the 1991 company took the Tally name later. The Registrar of Companies record would settle it.[9]

What the timeline shows clearly is how closely the product followed the Indian state's tax law rather than any foreign client's needs. Tally 6.3, in 1999, added support for several currencies and more advanced inventory management. Tally 7.2, in 2005, 'introduced VAT compliance, aiding businesses with tax regulations'. In 2017 the company launched software compliant with the new Goods and Services Tax (GST), and in 2020 a new generation of the product, TallyPrime.[10] Its buyers were small and medium Indian businesses, and it reached them not through onsite consultants but through a network of partners that, the company says, provide sales, support and services.[11]

The company's claims about its present size are large, and they are claims. Its website says it has helped 'millions across 100+ countries' for nearly four decades, that it is trusted by more than 2.5 million businesses, and that it is 'supported by 28,000+ partners'.[12] It also runs a training arm. Through TallyEducation, the company says, it has upskilled more than 800,000 learners with certifications and practical training in its software.[13] No independent figures for Tally's revenue or staff were found for this chapter.

Tally's model was the opposite of the export firms' in almost every respect. It sold a packaged product, not people. Its customers were Indian, not American. It earned in rupees from small businesses rather than in dollars from large ones. Its survival over four decades is evidence that a domestic market for software existed through the years when the export industry was growing much faster. Its modest public profile beside Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) or Infosys is a sign of how small that market was. Goenka himself would become a public critic of how the industry's institutions favoured services, as a later section shows.

## Selling to Africa first

i-flex took a different road, out of India and into the world, but not to the countries everyone else was going to. Hukku had studied electrical and electronics engineering at the Birla Institute of Technology and Science (BITS) at Pilani in Rajasthan, and had worked for TCS, the export firm F.C. Kohli built, before joining Citicorp's in-house unit.[14] In the 2007 interview he gave his college much of the credit for the habit of improvising. The campus was out in the desert, he said, with good laboratories and good professors and a village outside the gate, a bus stop, three tea shops and a small cinema. With no television, the students built their own radio station and a closed-circuit television system.[15]

The new company began poor. 'On day one,' Hukku said, 'we were selling professional services, we were doing consulting, and we were also trying to survive by spending very little money.'[16] It could not afford to chase clients in Britain or America. 'We decided early on that we were not going to go to the U.K. or the U.S. because we couldn't even afford the airfare. We said, "Let's go to Africa. That's all we can handle right now."'[17] The firm took the rights to an older Citicorp banking program called MicroBanker, improved it and sold it to African banks. By 1995, by Hukku's account, MicroBanker was number one in the league tables, sold to 117 banks, most of them in Africa.[18] He remembered visiting Nairobi in those years and seeing a large billboard advertising for staff: 'Wanted. IT people, MicroBanker experience essential.' So many banks there ran the program, he said, that there were not enough people who knew it.[19]

The services work and the old product paid for a new one. In Hukku's words, 'The money we were making on the services, consulting, MicroBanker, etc., was used to fund the creation of our dream product', which was FLEXCUBE, launched in 1997, an integrated banking package covering consumer, corporate, investment and internet banking and asset management.[20] This is the most important detail in his account for the argument of this chapter. India's best-known early global product was not built in spite of services. It was paid for by them.

Hukku's way of teaching his engineers the business they were automating was itself unusual. 'Right from the beginning, we said 10% to 15% of the staff have to be bankers, because how else will I learn the domain?'[21] The bankers did not come from Wall Street. 'We did not have any American or European bankers when we started,' he said. 'We took all the bankers from Indian banks, from Bank of India, from Reserve Bank of India, from smaller financial institutions.'[22] Many were not computer science graduates but were, in his phrase, 'computer savvy', and the company offered them the role of teacher: 'You come teach us what you know about banking.'[23] By 2007, he claimed, at least a quarter of the firm's staff could walk onto a trading floor and talk to a trader without being dismissed as 'just a techie'.[24] In an industry whose training, as Chapter 12 showed, turned graduates into programmers for foreign clients' systems, i-flex was turning programmers into people who understood banking.

Two of the company's choices ran against the industry as a whole. One concerned markets. Hukku saw that the services outsourcing business depended on America, 'and we said that's a very big limitation for the whole industry to be dependent on one country'.[25] By 2007 i-flex claimed customers in more than 125 countries, and Hukku put its market share in several African countries at between a quarter and nearly half of the banks.[26] The other choice concerned the Y2K boom, the rush to fix two-digit dates in old programs before the year 2000, which Chapter 11 showed bringing Infosys about a fifth of its revenue at its height. 'We're perhaps the only IT company from India who made $0 from Y2K,' Hukku said. 'We made a conscious decision to stick to the value-addition paradigm and the original principles.'[27] This is a founder's account, given years later. It is not confirmed by the company's accounts in the sources read here, and it should be read as his description of a choice rather than a verified figure.

FLEXCUBE climbed quickly. Hukku cited an annual sales league table compiled by International Banking Systems, a British research firm. There were, he said, 60 or 70 such core banking products, mostly American and European. FLEXCUBE was 16th in 1997, ninth in its second year, second in 1999 and first 'since 2002'.[28] By early 2007 the firm had sold it to more than 300 banks in 120 countries.[29] i-flex went public in 2002 on the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange, and in the December quarter of the year to March 2007 its revenue was Rs 5.5 billion ($126 million at the rate Wharton used).[30]

The mix of products and services carried the firm through the crash that followed the dot-com boom. In the interview Aron put it to Hukku that i-flex's shares had doubled while almost every other technology stock fell, and that in 2003 *Time* magazine had picked it as one of fifteen survivors of the crash. Hukku's explanation was the business model. 'We were a balanced product and services company with a globally hedged customer base,' he said. While the services firms slowed after Y2K and the dot-com boom, customers in Korea, Japan and elsewhere were still buying i-flex's packages.[31] The firm kept adding products. About three years before the interview, Hukku said, it had begun investing in software to help banks comply with new rules such as the Basel II capital standards, and he claimed that three of the five largest American banks had chosen its product, Reveleus, for that purpose.[32]

The ending was a sale. In 2005, according to the Wharton account, Oracle bought Citicorp's 42 per cent stake for $592 million.[33] Oracle's own announcement of 2 August 2005 put the stake at 41 per cent, held by Citigroup Venture Capital International, without stating the price.[34] Either way, Hukku said, 'No one at the time, including me, ever dreamed that the $400,000 they put in would become almost $600 million in 13 years.'[35]

Hukku was clear that i-flex had never been a pure product company. 'We are a product company. We're also a services company,' he said, listing technology deployment, consulting and specialist business process outsourcing.[36] The pattern fits Chapter 15. As with Cognizant, a foreign company's captive centre in India became an independent firm. The difference was that this one sold a product, and its largest exit was to an American buyer.

## A product inside the factory

Infosys, the archetype of the services firm, built a banking product of its own. Its annual report to American investors for the year to March 2002 said that, besides its IT services, it marketed and licensed 'our own centralized bank automation software application, known as FINACLE(TM)'.[37] It described Finacle as 'a fully web-enabled, integrated core banking solution that addresses the retail, corporate and trade finance activities of a bank', sold alongside an internet banking program called BankAway.[38] How Finacle grew from an earlier product is told in a business school case that was not obtained for this chapter.

The numbers show where the product sat in the firm. In the year to March 2009 Infosys told investors that it had earned 3.9 per cent of its total revenue from licensing software products, against 3.6 per cent the year before. 'The markets for our primary suite of software products which we call Finacle TM are competitive,' it added, and the costs of developing new products 'may not be recouped'.[39] Three years later the share was a little higher but still small: 4.6 per cent in the year to March 2012, 4.9 per cent in the year to March 2011 and 4.2 per cent in the year to March 2010.[40]

The product was also sold with services wrapped round it. 'We also provide a core banking software solution, Finacle TM, for the banking industry and provide customization and implementation services around this solution,' the company said in 2009.[41] By March 2012, Infosys said, Finacle was used by 160 banks in 78 countries, powering operations across 48,500 branches.[42] In a reorganisation in 2011, when the company merged seven business units into four organised by client industry, it noted that 'India and Finacle TM business units continue to be organized as before'.[43]

One detail of the 2009 report shows how a services firm thought about its product. The director responsible for Finacle, T.V. Mohandas Pai, who had been chief financial officer from 1994 to 2006, was listed as head of 'Administration, Education and Research, Finacle, Human Resources Development' and the Infosys Leadership Institute.[44] The banking product shared a portfolio with the training machine. Finacle, like FLEXCUBE, was never a pure product business. It was a product nested inside a services company, contributing less than a twentieth of revenue.

## Thirty firms walk out

By 2013 the product companies had decided that the industry's lobby did not speak for them. The National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, had represented the industry for about 25 years, as the industry grew, by Wharton's figures, from $100 million in 1992 to $108 billion in 2012.[45] In March 2013 Knowledge at Wharton reported that a group of 30 software product companies had recently come together to form the Indian Software Product Industry Round Table, known as iSPIRT, a think-tank for the products segment.[46]

Bharat Goenka of Tally was a founder member. He told the business daily *Business Standard*: 'At Nasscom, the product companies do not have the same weightage as the services companies. The policy interventions Nasscom is trying to make are more in favor of the services industry.'[47] Another founder member, Sharad Sharma, said: 'We believe that with this think-tank, we will get a louder voice in policy making and other initiatives.'[48]

Nasscom played the split down. Its chairman, N. Chandrasekaran, who was also chief executive of TCS, told Wharton's India publication: 'When you are on a growth paradigm, you cannot do everything that you want to do. I don't see any conflict.'[49] The association had in fact set up an expert committee the previous July, chaired by N.R. Narayana Murthy, co-founder of Infosys, to chart a roadmap for the industry. Releasing its report, Murthy said the industry should aim to reach $300 billion by 2020, and that 'we need to look at new areas of growth like Internet and mobile content and commerce, software products and the domestic market'.[50]

The committee's arithmetic shows the scale of priorities. By 2020, it suggested, the internet and mobile could account for $100 billion, the domestic market $75 billion, and software products 'around US$10 billion'.[51] Products were on the list, at about one-thirtieth of the target. Murthy also said it was imperative to rebrand India as 'a proactive solutions provider, instead of a reactive solutions developer'.[52]

What iSPIRT did next, including the advocacy for public digital infrastructure with which it later became associated, is described in sources not read for this chapter. The break itself is documented. In 2013 some of the country's leading product founders said, in public, that the industry's main institution was built around selling services, and the institution's own plan gave products a small place in its future.

## The warehouse in Chennai

The next generation of Indian product companies did not sell boxes of software to shopkeepers or core banking systems to banks. They sold subscriptions over the internet, software as a service (SaaS), mostly to small businesses abroad. The best documented is Freshworks, because it listed its shares in the United States and had to explain itself to American investors.

Its founder and chief executive, Girish Mathrubootham, began his letter in the 2021 registration statement with its origins: 'We started in a 700 square foot warehouse in Chennai in 2010. Our simple idea was to create a "fresh" helpdesk in response to a poor customer service experience I had.'[53] The dreams, he wrote, came in increments. In 2011 the big dream was to reach $1 million of revenue. In 2012 the company began planning a second product, Freshservice, before its first, Freshdesk, had reached that mark.[54] He listed the doubts he had heard along the way. Among them were 'You can't find talent in Chennai; you can't win unless you move to Bangalore' and 'You really can't build a global SaaS company from India.'[55]

The company's model needed none of the apparatus of the services industry. 'Freshworks was global from "day one" with our first few customers coming from four different continents,' Mathrubootham wrote. 'Our initial sales came through search-driven online acquisition, powered by robust product-led growth.'[56] Customers found the software by searching the web, tried it and paid for it, without a salesman on a plane. There were no onsite engineers, no visas, no time sheets and no client billing by the hour. By mid-2021, the company said, businesses from more than 120 countries used its products, and it had about 52,500 paying customers.[57]

It also lost money as it grew. Its revenue was $172.4 million in 2019 and $249.7 million in 2020, a rise of 45 per cent, and its net losses in those years were $31.1 million and $57.3 million.[58] The work was done in India. As of July 2021, Freshworks said, it had about 4,300 employees, and about 3,800 of them were in India, 'where most of engineering, product design, sales and marketing, customer support, and general and administrative personnel are located'. Its headquarters, where most of its executives sat, were in San Mateo, California.[59] Among its risk factors it told investors that 'a majority of our software engineering resources' were in India.[60]

The rivalry among India's product firms reached an American court. The registration statement disclosed that on 17 March 2020 Zoho Corporation, an Indian software company, had sued Freshworks in the Northern District of California, alleging violations of federal and California trade-secret law and of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. Freshworks said it believed it had 'meritorious defenses'.[61] These were allegations, and the outcome of the case is not in the sources read for this chapter. Zoho's own history, as told by its founder Sridhar Vembu, could not be obtained.

On 22 September 2021 the final prospectus fixed the price: 'The initial public offering price is $36.00 per share.' The company offered 28,500,000 shares of Class A common stock for listing on the Nasdaq Global Select Market.[62] At that price the shares on offer came to about $1.03 billion before any extra shares the underwriters might buy.[63]

The listing settled something that the Chennai warehouse had left open. The most celebrated Indian software-as-a-service company was, legally and financially, an American one: incorporated in Delaware, headquartered in San Mateo and listed in New York, with its engineers in India.[64] The value it created was priced in dollars on an American exchange. Like i-flex's sale to Oracle, the reward for building a product in India was realised abroad.

## Money arrives, and leaves

In Hukku's account, the shortage that had mattered most in 1992 was money. 'Even in 1992, there was not enough capital,' he said in 2007. By then, he believed, the position had reversed: if three people with Indian-sounding names came in with an IT business plan, there would be at least three financiers ready to back them. 'So capital is not a problem today.'[65]

Fourteen years later capital arrived on a scale far beyond anything he described, and much of it went to consumer internet businesses rather than to software products in his sense. How large it was depends on whose count is used. According to figures from Tracxn, a data firm, reported by *The Week* in January 2025, Indian start-ups raised $25.7 billion in 2022, a fall of 31 per cent on the year before, which implies about $37 billion in 2021.[66] Other trackers put the 2021 total higher, at about $42 billion or more.[67] Each defines a 'start-up' differently, and their series cannot be joined. On Tracxn's count, funding then fell by close to 58 per cent in 2023, to $10.8 billion, and rose by about 2 per cent in 2024, to $11.1 billion. Six new 'unicorns', private firms valued at $1 billion or more, were created in 2024, against two in 2023.[68]

The emblem of the bust was Byju's, an education technology company. Its fall is documented in this chapter only through press reports of court proceedings. According to those reports, in mid-2024 the National Company Law Tribunal admitted an insolvency petition from the Board of Control for Cricket in India over unpaid dues of about Rs 158.9 crore (Rs 1.59 billion, about $19 million at 2024's average rate). An appellate tribunal later closed the proceedings after a settlement, and on 23 October 2024 the Supreme Court set that order aside.[69] The primary court records were not read for this chapter.

Byju's matters to this book less for its legal fate than as a marker. The boom moved Indian technology investment towards consumer services, many of them selling to Indians and employing engineers in large numbers before cutting them. Their engineers' accounts of those years, of joining and of being let go, are not in the sources gathered for this chapter, and the gap is a real one.

## Sidebar: Why did India's biggest platforms come from the state?

By the measure of daily use, the most successful Indian software of the 2010s was not sold by a private firm at all. Chapter 16 told the story of Aadhaar, the biometric identity number. The other case is the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), a system run by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) that lets a person pay another directly from a bank account using a phone.

The Reserve Bank of India's statistics show how fast it grew. In the year to March 2017, the year in which UPI was introduced, it carried about 18 million transactions, worth about Rs 6,961 crore (Rs 69.6 billion).[70] Accounts differ on whether the launch came in April or August 2016.[71] Seven years later, in the year to March 2024, it carried 131.13 billion transactions, worth about Rs 199.95 lakh crore (Rs 199.95 trillion, roughly $2.4 trillion at that year's average rate).[72] All India Radio, reporting NPCI's figures, said that in December 2024 alone UPI processed about 16.73 billion transactions worth around Rs 23.25 lakh crore (Rs 23.25 trillion), with the number of transactions about 39 per cent higher than a year earlier.[73]

No private Indian software product in this chapter came near that scale of use. Tally counts its users in millions of businesses, by its own claim; Freshworks counted its paying customers in tens of thousands.[74] UPI counts its transactions in billions each month.

The sources gathered for this chapter document the scale. They do not explain it, and the question in this sidebar's title cannot yet be answered from them. Several explanations suggest themselves, none of them tested here: that a payments network is public infrastructure, which a private firm would have charged for or kept closed to rivals; that only the state could bring the banks in; and that it drew on engineers the services industry had trained, some of whom moved from private firms into public projects, as Nilekani did (Chapter 16). The role of the product founders' round table, iSPIRT, in arguing for such public platforms is reported in sources not read here, and is left out for that reason.

What the comparison does show is a different answer to the product question. Where private Indian firms built products, they either sold them to small Indian businesses, as Tally did, or sold them abroad and realised their value abroad, as i-flex and Freshworks did. UPI was built for Indians, used at home, and run as infrastructure rather than sold as a product. The comparison the outline of this book first proposed, with China's domestic platforms, would set this against a country whose private firms built their platforms behind a protected home market. That comparison needs sources this chapter does not have.

## Products and people

The 'services mindset' is the most common explanation in India for why the country built so few products. It is the participants' own. Hukku said Indian software companies had followed the model F.C. Kohli pioneered at TCS in the 1970s, 'of exporting the Indian brain, or doing software services projects offshore'. Kohli had worked very hard to make it succeed, Hukku said. 'The not-so-great news was that hundreds of companies that came after copied him. Basically it is a human tendency to take the route which is already a proven success.'[75] When Aron asked whether i-flex was 'Made in Spite of India', he replied: 'It's not in spite of India, but it's in spite of whatever was successful when we came into existence.'[76] Goenka's complaint about Nasscom, and Nasscom's own roadmap, said the same thing about the industry's institutions.[77]

These are explanations from people with a stake in them, and they have not been tested. Several structural explanations fit the same facts. One is capital, which Hukku himself named: there was not enough of it in 1992, and venture funding at scale came only in the following decades.[78] A second is the market. Tally's history suggests that small Indian businesses would pay for software when the state's tax rules required it, but no measure of Indian spending on software over the period was found for this chapter.[79] Building a product for India was no guarantee of success either. The Simputer, a low-cost multilingual hand-held computer designed in 2000 by a group from the Indian Institute of Science and Encore Software in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), received worldwide attention but was, in the words of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, 'not a commercial success'.[80] A third is talent. As Chapter 12 showed, the services firms offered graduates secure, well-paid jobs with training attached, and a young product firm had to compete with that. Talent also meant knowledge of the customer's trade. i-flex's answer was a school of its own: from the start, by Hukku's account, it hired bankers from Indian banks and the Reserve Bank of India and asked them to teach programmers banking.[81] A fourth is the exit. The largest rewards for building products in India were realised through a foreign buyer, as with Oracle and i-flex, or a foreign listing, as with Freshworks.[82] A 'mindset' may be the participants' name for these conditions rather than a separate cause. Deciding between the explanations would need comparative evidence on capital, on domestic software spending and on where engineers chose to work, and this chapter does not have it. The question stays open.

What can be measured is the difference in scale, and it cuts both ways. At the end of 2024 Freshworks had about 4,400 employees, and approximately 3,700 of them, 83 per cent, lived in India.[83] That was slightly fewer people in India than the 3,800 it had reported at the time of its listing, and 53 per cent of its 2024 revenue came from customers outside North America. The company said more than 72,000 companies used its products.[84] Its revenue that year was $720.4 million, up from $596.4 million in 2023 and $498.0 million in 2022.[85] In the same year Cognizant, the American services firm born from a captive centre in India, had about 336,800 employees, 241,500 of them in India.[86] TCS reported 607,979 employees in its fact sheet for the quarter to March 2025.[87] Infosys had about 150,000 as early as March 2012 (Chapter 12).[88] The figures come from different years and are given only to show orders of magnitude. Freshworks earned its revenue with a workforce less than a fiftieth the size of Cognizant's in India.

The debate in India treated products as the higher path, and on revenue per engineer they were. But the engineer factory described in Chapter 12 was turning out graduates in the hundreds of thousands each year. No set of product companies of the size documented here could have employed them. The services model was not only a habit of mind. It was the model in which the industry's growth turned into mass employment.

Freshworks's annual report for 2024 shows what happened when the product side met the new technology. On 5 November 2024, the company said, it had committed to a plan to realign its workforce 'that resulted in a 13% reduction in force', at a cost of about $9.7 million.[89] The same report described its generative AI offerings. 'Freddy AI Agent offers always-on, autonomous, personalized resolutions to customer and employee queries,' the company told investors, while Freddy AI Copilot provided 'always-on contextual assistance' for support, marketing and sales staff, 'designed to boost productivity'.[90] Among its risks it listed the chance that competitors 'may be able to develop new AI offerings that disrupt our customers' needs and negatively impact demand for our products'.[91]

Read beside Chapter 14, the description is pointed. An Indian-built product company was selling software designed to answer customers' and employees' questions without a person, which is much of what India's call centres and IT help desks had been paid by the hour to do. The same filing that put 83 per cent of its staff in India recorded its own exposure to disruption by AI. Products had not escaped the AI question. They met it from the other side, as sellers of the automation that the services firms would have to absorb.

Hukku had made a prediction in 2007: 'I can guarantee you that five years from now, there will be many famous product companies growing out of India.'[92] By the early 2020s there were some, and a flood of venture money that peaked in 2021. Yet in those same pandemic years the old model did not shrink. The firms that sold engineers' hours went on hiring, in numbers that no product company could match, and it was their boom, and what it did to the people hired in it, that shaped the lives of most young Indian engineers. That is the subject of the next chapter.
