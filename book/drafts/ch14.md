# Chapter 14. Backlash and scale (2002-2011)

In February 2004 N. Gregory Mankiw, a Harvard economist then serving as chairman of the White House Council of Economic Advisers, made a remark about trade that the Princeton economist Alan Blinder would later call a 'textbook' statement. Offshoring, the movement of jobs but not of the people who do them from rich countries to poor ones, was in his words 'the latest manifestation of the gains from trade that economists have talked about at least since Adam Smith'. He added: 'More things are tradable than were tradable in the past, and that's a good thing.'[1] The remark caused what Blinder called a national uproar. 'Democratic and Republican politicians alike excoriated Mankiw', Blinder wrote, while economists lined up to defend him.[2]

The politicians were already acting. On 20 February 2004 the trade paper *Government Computer News* (GCN) reported that a provision in the omnibus budget bill President George W. Bush had signed the month before barred companies that won federal work through competitions under the government's Circular A-76 from shifting that work to other countries.[3] The measure was named the Thomas-Voinovich Amendment after its two Republican sponsors, Senator Craig Thomas of Wyoming and Senator George Voinovich of Ohio. GCN described it as 'part of a swell of legislation' against businesses and governments buying software and IT services from countries such as India and China.[4] For one year, work won by a vendor could not be 'performed by the contractor at a location outside the United States'.[5]

The states went further on paper. The National Conference of State Legislatures had counted 29 bills in 19 state legislatures that would require contractors to hire only American citizens or legal residents, regulate agencies' use of offshore call centres, or otherwise restrict foreign purchases.[6] Justin Marks, the conference's research analyst, said that state legislatures had had to fill a $200 billion budget gap over the previous three years and faced a choice between saving money by outsourcing and keeping jobs at home. Over the years, he said, legislators 'have seen manufacturing jobs go overseas and now they see white-collar jobs going abroad'.[7] The television presenter Lou Dobbs published a book that year with the title *Exporting America*.[8]

Not everyone thought the noise would matter. John Kost, a managing vice-president at the research firm Gartner, told GCN that government agencies had been buying offshore software for years. 'People are naive if they think it is not already happening in large quantities,' he said. 'Once the economy improves and politicians move to a different topic, I doubt it will be a potent political issue.'[9] He doubted that the managers of government programmes, who knew how much offshore work went into the software they bought, would support rules 'requiring projects to omit offshore labor, thereby driving up the cost of projects'.[10]

Kost was right that the rules would not stop the work, and wrong about the argument. The federal restriction applied to a narrow class of contracts, and for one year.[11] The industry it was aimed at kept growing. By the estimate of Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, Indian information technology and business process outsourcing firms employed nearly two million people directly in the year to March 2008, roughly ten times the 200,000 software professionals it had counted, on a narrower definition, a decade earlier.[12] But the argument about which work could be sent abroad did not fade. It became sharper, and one version of it, written in 2006, still sets the terms for the last part of this book.

This chapter covers the decade between the dot-com bust and the end of India's software park tax holiday. It was the decade in which the factory of Chapters 11 and 12 reached full size, in which a second industry of back offices and call centres grew beside it, and in which the financial crisis of 2008 tested the model. It is also the decade for which the record of ordinary workers' lives is thinnest in the sources this book has been able to use. Where the voices come from a partisan or anonymous account, the text says so.

## The wire test

Alan Blinder was no protectionist. He had served on the Council of Economic Advisers in 1993-94 and as vice-chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1994 to 1996.[13] In an essay in the March/April 2006 issue of *Foreign Affairs* he agreed with Mankiw's economics and disagreed with his sense of scale.[14]

He began modestly. There were no reliable national data, he wrote, but fragmentary studies suggested that 'well under a million service-sector jobs in the United States have been lost to offshoring to date'. In the churn of the American labour market that was 'less than two weeks' worth of normal gross job losses'.[15] The future was another matter. Improvements in technology and communications, he argued, 'virtually guarantee that the future will bring much more offshoring' of what he called 'impersonal services', which he defined as 'services that can be delivered electronically over long distances with little or no degradation in quality'.[16] He called the change 'a third Industrial Revolution'.[17]

His central claim was that the line between safe and exposed work would not follow the line between skilled and unskilled work. 'Janitors and crane operators are probably immune to foreign competition; accountants and computer programmers are not.'[18] Typing, a low-skill job, and security analysis, a high-skill one, were both 'already being delivered electronically from India', though on a small scale so far.[19] The telephone operator who arranged a conference call, or the clerk who took an airline booking by phone, might already be there. 'He or she may be in India already.'[20] His rough estimate of the American service jobs 'susceptible to offshoring in the electronic future' was 'two to three times the total number of current manufacturing jobs (which is about 14 million)', that is, something like 28 to 42 million jobs.[21] He thought the better-known estimates were too low. Forrester Research had caused a stir by predicting that 3.3 million American service jobs would move offshore by 2015, and a 2003 Berkeley study and a McKinsey study had each put 11% of American jobs at risk. Those studies, Blinder wrote, counted only 'the currently visible tip of the offshoring iceberg'.[22]

The usual answer to such fears in rich countries was more education. Blinder thought that answer was mistaken, or at least incomplete, because the dividing line he described was not a line of education. 'The geeks may not inherit the earth after all', he wrote, 'at least not the highly paid geeks in the rich countries.'[23] He told Americans to worry less about China, whose challenge came mainly in manufacturing, and more about India, which was 'learning to exploit its already strong comparative advantage in English'.[24]

One passage in the essay matters more to this book than the rest. 'The dividing line between personal and impersonal services will move over time. As information technology improves, more and more personal services will become impersonal services.'[25] Blinder's test was whether a task could be sent down a wire without losing quality. The Indian industry had grown by answering yes for a lengthening list of tasks: first code, then accounts, then the voice on the end of a telephone line. The work that could be specified, documented and delivered electronically to Bangalore or Gurgaon was the work Blinder thought most exposed. He was writing about the movement of work between countries. His test does not care where the work goes, and the final chapters of this book apply it to a different destination.

## Reconciliations and call centres

Software was half of the industry's story in these years. The other half was business process outsourcing (BPO): the transfer of a company's back-office work, such as accounts, claims, collections and customer calls, to another site or another firm. Its best-known Indian pioneer was Raman Roy, who helped build the Indian operations of American Express and General Electric before founding his own company. He told his story to *Knowledge at Wharton*, the University of Pennsylvania's business journal, in June 2003, and it is his own account.[26]

At American Express's Indian division, Roy said, the cost per transaction turned out to be 'lower almost by 40% to 50%' than in the company's more advanced markets. Others in the company did not believe it. 'These guys in India obviously don't know how to make their reports; how could these numbers be true?'[27] Roy put the advantage down to the people. An associate doing bank reconciliations in the United States might have just completed high school and earn '$15,000 to $18,000 or perhaps $20,000'. At those salaries, he said, 'I was able to field professionals who were at least college graduates and who sometimes were CPAs', meaning certified public accountants.[28] This was the arbitrage of the software story applied to the back office: Indian graduates doing work that in rich countries was done by people with less schooling, for less money.

The savings were not only in wages. By paying suppliers' invoices faster, the Delhi unit earned American Express discounts for prompt settlement, and Roy once set those discounts against his unit's salary bill in a presentation to the company. 'You've got us for free', he told it, 'because the incremental discounts that we got alone were more than the salary bill in India.'[29]

The obstacles were physical. When the team asked the Indian telecommunications company VSNL for 100% up-time, 'they looked at us as though we were from some other world because 90% up-time was considered outstanding'.[30] Roy also described a small piece of invention. His Delhi unit reconciled about 12,000 accounts in the spreadsheet program Lotus 1-2-3, where one slip of the hand could throw an account out of balance. Head office planned a standard platform that would take fifteen months to reach India, so Roy had an interim tool built with a local firm run by his former head of technology, Sunil Gujral. When the American package proved slower than expected on test data, Roy ran the test files through the Indian tool. 'It took only 25% of the time to reconcile the file.' Within three months, he said, it had become American Express's global reconciliation platform.[31] Once a process had been moved and written down, the people running it began to improve it.

Roy joined GE in 1996. Its first Indian project was work GE had already sent to Mexico, and Roy had to quote a lower price than Mexico's; his finance chief told him, 'we will probably lose money on every transaction.'[32] His unit became what he called 'the Five Sigma beta site for GE Capital', a testing ground for the company's quality programme.[33] When he proposed handling live customer calls from India, a very senior executive 'pulled out his pen and crossed out the investment', saying that 'call centers would never happen out of India'. Roy borrowed equipment from vendors for three months and found a client to fund part of a trial. 'It was nationalistic pride that drove me to see that a call center came into being,' he said. By 2003 he believed that more than half of GE Capital's Indian revenue came from its call centres.[34] He went on to found Spectramind, which the Indian software company Wipro bought in 2002.[35]

The dates of this history are not settled. The *Knowledge at Wharton* introduction places Roy's work at American Express in the mid-1980s.[36] V. Rajaraman's history of Indian computing says that American Express was the first organisation to start BPO work in India, in 1994, followed by GE Capital International Services (GECIS) in 1997.[37] Other accounts date GECIS to October 1996, and credit its founding both to Roy and to Pramod Bhasin, who headed it.[38] The accounts can be partly reconciled, since Roy's mid-1980s findings concern the cost of the existing Indian card operation, not a dedicated export centre, but who founded GECIS and when remains open.

What is not in dispute is the kind of worker the model sought. In the same interview Roy described Indian BPO staff as 'well qualified, highly educated people' who were 'not a transient workforce'. For them, he said, 'BPO is a full-time career', and they expected employers to think seriously about their careers.[39] Rajaraman, writing of the decade as a whole, put the requirement more plainly. The back office meant accounts receivable, payroll, account reconciliation and similar work, and starting a BPO centre needed little more than overseas clients and 'trainable human resources with good knowledge of English (which was available in reasonable numbers)'.[40] That was a manager's view of his workforce in 2003. The workers' own view is harder to find.

## The night shift

The scholarly studies of Indian call centres in these years, which quote agents at length, could not be obtained for this book. The one first-hand account in hand comes from an anonymous activist, recruited through a German job centre, who spent three months in 2005-06 as a foreign call-centre agent in Gurgaon, a fast-growing satellite town south of Delhi, and published a report on the libcom.org website in January 2007.[41] It is a political tract, its names cannot be checked, and its figures are the author's. It is used here because nothing better is available, and everything drawn from it is labelled.

The author worked for a research outsourcing firm whose Gurgaon office employed about 800 people. The work was outbound calling to companies in Europe: about 100 calls in a ten-hour shift.[42] By the author's estimate there were probably about 150,000 call-centre workers in Gurgaon alone, and one building occupied by Genpact, the former GE Capital operation, held about 12,000.[43] The rules were set by the clients' world. Officially, the author wrote, 'the only language allowed for internal communication in the call centre is English'.[44] The agents, mostly in their twenties and about half of them men by the author's count, had nearly all worked in other call centres nearby.[45]

The report includes short interviews, recorded during breaks and given without names. A 22-year-old woman described her first job interview with a Gurgaon call centre in April 2004, while she was still living in Bhopal; she went to it with her mother. In the next year and a half, by her account, she worked in fourteen call centres and raised her monthly pay from Rs 8,000 to Rs 20,000 (about $180 to $440 at the 2006 average exchange rate) by changing jobs. She had worked six days a week from 2.30 in the morning to 12.30 in the afternoon, calling the United States, Canada and Britain, sometimes on automatic diallers that placed 400 to 500 calls a shift.[46] 'In call centres you mainly learn about working time and discipline,' she said, 'you are physically un-free, but mentally free.'[47]

A 21-year-old woman who had worked on GE Capital's Australian shift, with a cab collecting her at 2.30 in the morning, said she had answered about 100 calls a day and had five minutes to go to the toilet. She had to use an English name. 'I thought it would be fun, but it was more like a prison.'[48] A 22-year-old man from Calcutta, who described his background as Adivasi (a member of India's indigenous communities), took a technical-support job for Hewlett-Packard in a centre run by Daksh, later taken over by IBM. His training lasted two months and was mainly about using the tools. 'I brought all my software books from the course, because I thought that I would work at IBM now, but I do not need them.'[49]

The same man's basic wage was Rs 10,500 a month. He was expected to sell software and computers to the callers whose problems he had solved; by his account he sold $1,000 to $2,000 worth in a month and received Rs 1,000 to Rs 1,500 in incentives for it.[50] A 24-year-old man who had worked on Citibank's American credit-card process for an outsourcing contractor said the contractor 'made sure that they got the people with the best accent' for that work. Agents on it wore ties, started at Rs 8,500 a month and, he said, could be dismissed for leaving a desk for a minute without logging out.[51]

The author put the basic wage for new agents at about Rs 8,000 a month and the average, with incentives, at Rs 12,000 to Rs 14,000 for a week of about 50 hours on night shifts, which is roughly $265 to $310 at the 2006 average rate.[52] The cleaners and canteen staff in the same building, many of them former small farmers and carpenters from Calcutta and Bihar, were paid about Rs 2,000 a month for 60 to 70 hours a week.[53] The author's impression, which was no more than that, was that upper-caste surnames were more frequent among the agents, though caste itself was never mentioned.[54] 'Not a single call centre employee I talked to had experience with manual labour,' the report says.[55] The workers were also clear about why the jobs had come: most of those the activists spoke to said that the main reason was wages of about a fifth of those in the United States or Britain, and some said that after five years in call centres you were finished.[56]

Schooling was part of the bargain. The author describes the agents as students or former students, several of whom had learned a second foreign language because it paid better, at courses only the upper-middle class could afford.[57] The woman from Bhopal had learned French when she decided that 'only speaking English is not enough of a qualification, because so many people speak English'. She estimated that about 40% of call-centre workers were studying by correspondence, and said that many gave up after a while.[58]

The night shifts depended on cars. The report describes cab drivers 'tired after seventeen hours shifts' and a colleague killed in a car crash on her way home from work.[59] The transport of women working at night became a public issue in these years, but the legal record of the case that brought it to national attention has not been read for this book, and it is not described here.

## Two million

The industry's own figures for 2008 come from a Nasscom presentation and must be read as the claims of an interested party. Nasscom described itself as having more than 1,200 members, including 250 global companies, which together accounted for over 90% of exports and a majority of 'the 2 million professionals employed in the sector'.[60] It estimated direct employment at nearly two million for FY2007-08, and added an estimate of seven to eight million indirect jobs, a multiplier of its own.[61] Its chart of industry revenue rose from about $21.6bn in FY2003-04 to an estimated $64bn in FY2007-08, of which exports were 62-66%.[62] It put the BPO business at an estimated $10.3bn in FY2007-08. Its chart of the BPO business lists customer interaction and support first and appears to make it the largest category, though the figures in the copy read for this book are hard to match to their labels.[63] Net additions to employment, by its figures, were 234,000 in FY2004-05, 328,000 in FY2005-06 and an estimated 375,000 in FY2007-08.[64] Rajaraman gives 2.4 million directly employed in IT in 2010.[65] His account of the industry at that date stresses maturity. The large companies had moved from low-level testing to complete applications and took fixed-price contracts; 166 Indian IT companies had reached level 5, the top level, of the Software Engineering Institute's Capability Maturity Model (Chapter 11); and more than 400 of the Fortune 500 companies were clients.[66] Ten Indian IT companies were listed on foreign stock exchanges, with more than 400 delivery centres outside India.[67]

The official record is the Reserve Bank of India's. Its balance-of-payments series for software services receipts rose from $9.6bn in FY2002-03 to $40.3bn in FY2007-08 and $53.1bn in FY2010-11.[68] The bank's separate annual survey of software and IT services exporters, which covered 760 companies in 2010-11, put exports that year at about $47.6bn, up from $38.7bn the year before.[69] The two series measure different things and should not be added or compared directly. Nor are they wholly independent of the lobby: the bank noted in its survey for 2007-08 that it compiled the balance-of-payments software figures using Nasscom's data as a controlling total.[70] Both show an industry several times larger at the end of the decade than at the start.

The work was heavily concentrated. Nasscom's map of delivery locations in 2008 gave Bangalore 36%, the New Delhi, Noida and Gurgaon region 17%, Chennai 15%, Mumbai and Pune 15%, Hyderabad 14% and all other places 5%.[71] Its caption said that the industry was 'well spread across multiple locations' and that smaller 'Tier 2/3 cities' were emerging.[72] By its own figures, only 5% of the work lay outside these centres (its percentages, as printed, add up to slightly more than 100). The United States took 61% of exports in FY2006-07, Britain 18% and continental Europe 12%.[73]

The presentation is more revealing about strain than about scale. Its list of the questions clients asked began with attrition and wage inflation: 'is India's talent pool running dry?'[74] Under 'Pressure Points' it listed 'High lateral hiring leading to attrition' and 'Tier 3 colleges yields low', and its first point was that talent was trainable but the industry needed 'to make them employable'.[75] Its proposed remedies were an education programme in miniature: an entry-level assessment for BPO and IT recruits, 'finishing schools' to prepare graduates for work, a faculty development programme to make teachers more suitable, and government plans for 20 new Indian Institutes of Information Technology.[76] Nasscom's cost comparison for 2007 put India at about $28,000 against $100,000 for the United States, and projected that India would keep most of that advantage to 2015.[77]

The last part of the presentation dealt with a different worry. 'With increasing levels of outsourcing,' it said, 'employees were viewed as a significant weak link'. Recruitment and initial training cost about six to eight months of an employee's wages. Nasscom estimated that about 30% of CVs contained false claims, and it noted that India had 'No law addressing privacy or employee rights'.[78] Its answer was the National Skills Registry, a database of employees' verified education and work histories, with photographs and fingerprints, a Nasscom initiative meant, in its words, to 'instill confidence in client about employees'.[79] The registry was a sign of how far the industry's workforce had become something its foreign customers wanted to inspect.

## The factory at full size

The largest firms show what this growth meant in practice. Infosys, whose filings with the American Securities and Exchange Commission give the most consistent figures, employed about 25,700 people at the end of March 2004.[80] Four years later it employed about 91,200, and by March 2012 about 150,000.[81] Its BPO subsidiary, Progeon, had about 1,900 employees in March 2004 and recruited about 2,100 more, net of departures, in the following year.[82] Its revenue rose from $1,062.6m in FY2003-04, 98.6% of it from exports, to $4,176m in FY2007-08 and $4,663m in FY2008-09.[83]

Hiring on this scale was a sorting exercise. In FY2004-05 Infosys reported screening about 1,348,800 applications and making about 14,500 offers of employment. In FY2007-08 it received about 885,000 applications and made about 46,300 offers, a figure the filing does not divide between new graduates and experienced recruits.[84] The company did much of its own teaching. In 2005 its new training facility at Mysore could house 4,500 trainees at a time and train about 12,000 a year.[85] By the FY2008-09 filing it could house 13,500 and train about 40,000 a year, and Infosys employed 476 full-time faculty, 186 of them with doctorates or master's degrees.[86] Chapter 12 follows what the trainees were taught. Here the point is the scale: by the end of the decade one company was running a residential college for tens of thousands of engineers a year, because, in Nasscom's words, the colleges did not make their graduates employable.

The other strain was people leaving. Infosys estimated attrition across the Indian technology services industry at about 20% a year in the middle of the decade, and its own at 10.5% in FY2003-04 and 9.7% in FY2004-05.[87] Its rate then rose to 13.7% in FY2006-07 and 13.4% in FY2007-08.[88] The company noted that attrition in business process management was 'generally significantly higher' than in technology services.[89] For a firm that spent months training each recruit, every departure was a loss, and the lateral hiring that Nasscom complained of was one firm's recruit becoming another's.

## The quarter to March 2009

The financial crisis of 2008 reached the Indian firms through their largest customers. About a third of Infosys's revenue, 33.9% in FY2008-09, came from the financial services industry.[90] Its filing for that year noted that leading American investment banks had gone bankrupt or been forced to sell themselves, and warned that clients might cut, postpone or consolidate their technology spending.[91]

The firm's response can be read in its numbers. Offers of employment fell from about 46,300 in FY2007-08 to about 16,900 in FY2008-09, while applications fell from 885,000 to 488,700.[92] The second instrument was pay. 'Until 2009, when we instituted a freeze in salary increases,' the company said, it had reviewed compensation every year.[93] The third was the bench, the pool of trained staff not assigned to paying work. For the quarter ended 31 March 2009 the share of Infosys's technology professionals, including trainees, who were billed to clients fell to about 66.9%, against 70.2% a year earlier.[94] At the low point, about a third of the technical workforce, trainees included, was being paid without being billed. The filings show no mass layoffs: headcount still rose, from about 91,200 in March 2008 to about 104,900 in March 2009 and 113,800 in March 2010.[95]

The cost of the crisis fell in particular places. It fell on graduates who did not receive offers in the year to March 2009, or whose offers came later; this book has not found press accounts of those deferred joiners, and their experience is a gap in the record. It fell on employees whose pay stood still. The FY2009-10 filing reported average increases that year of 8% for offshore staff and 2% for those working at client sites abroad, against 11-13% and 4-5% in FY2008-09.[96] The filing dates the increase 'with effect from October 2010', which cannot be right for a rise paid within the year to March 2010. The company's quarterly report for the three months to December 2009 shows that the annual rise, normally made in April, had been postponed to October 2009.[97] Utilisation including trainees averaged 67.5% for FY2009-10, lower than the year before.[98] The training programme lengthened. The FY2008-09 filing gave the integrated training for most new student hires as about 14 weeks in one passage and about 19 in another; the FY2009-10 filing gave 20 to 29 weeks, and a new education centre at Mysore that could train about 14,000 people at a time.[99] The filing does not say why the course grew longer while billable work was scarce.

Clients also pushed costs onto their suppliers. The FY2008-09 filing reported 'increased demand from our clients that all risks associated with foreign exchange fluctuations be borne by us'.[100] The rupee, which had averaged about Rs 40.3 to the dollar in FY2007-08, averaged about Rs 46.0 in FY2008-09.[101] The filings described 'a growing trend among consumers of IT services towards consolidation of technology service providers in order to improve efficiency and reduce costs', and long contracts with benchmarking provisions, under which a client could commission a study comparing the supplier's prices with those of its rivals and seek lower fees.[102]

The pattern had appeared before. In 2004, after the dot-com bust, Infosys had said that 'declines in demand, pricing pressures for our services, appreciation of the rupee against the dollar and increased wage pressures in India' had kept it from 'historical levels of profitability'.[103] On the evidence of its filings, Infosys met the second downturn by slowing hiring, carrying a larger bench, holding down pay and absorbing pressure on prices, while its headcount kept rising. By FY2010-11 utilisation including trainees was back to 72.1%, and in FY2011-12 the company made about 41,460 offers.[104] Attrition climbed back with demand, to 17.0% in FY2010-11.[105]

The crisis also showed how much of the model still depended on people abroad. At the end of March 2009 most of Infosys's technology professionals in the United States held either H-1B visas, about 8,200 people, or intra-company L-1 visas, about 1,400.[106] A year later the figures were about 8,900 and 1,800.[107] About 20,200 of its 104,900 employees worked outside India.[108] Those visa holders were the narrow top of the onsite-offshore pyramid described in Chapter 11, and the part of it most exposed to American politics (Chapter 13).

## From parks to zones

The state's support for the industry changed form in these years. The tax holiday of the 1990s had been tied to software technology parks (Chapters 8 and 9): export profits of units in the parks were exempt from income tax. The Finance Act of 2005 introduced a separate holiday for units in designated special economic zones (SEZs) that began providing services on or after 1 April 2005. Such units could deduct all their export profits from taxable income for five years and half of them for a further five, with more relief available on conditions.[109] The Special Economic Zones Act was passed the same year.[110] Rajaraman counts 257 software and services companies set up in SEZs by 2007, and writes that many BPO centres were placed in the zones because profits earned there were tax exempt.[111]

The park holiday was extended and then allowed to end. Infosys reported that the Finance Act of 2009 extended the holiday for park units until 31 March 2011, and that for its remaining park units it would expire by the end of that fiscal year.[112] By March 2009 its centres in Chandigarh, Chennai, Mangalore and Pune operated in SEZs; by March 2012 the list had grown to Chandigarh, Chennai, Hyderabad, Mangalore, Mysore, Pune, Trivandrum and Jaipur, and 'many of our proposed development centers are likely to operate in SEZs'.[113] The FY2011-12 filing said that the expiry of tax holidays had already hurt its profit margin.[114]

The change favoured firms large enough to build or lease whole zones, and it moved the industry from office towers into walled campuses. It did not remove the old frustrations. Roy, who had set up centres for three companies, said in 2003 that the government promised a 'single-window clearance', but 'there are 25 single windows you need to go to'. For one new centre in Pune he counted 119 places where he had signed.[115]

By the end of the period the offshore model had largely won. The Reserve Bank's survey for 2010-11 found that about four-fifths of software and IT services export revenue, 79.3%, was earned from work done in India, against 74.6% in 2007-08; the United States took about 63.6%.[116] The same survey put ITES and BPO at about 26.3% of these exports in 2010-11, down from 31.0% the year before.[117] In the 1980s most Indian software export revenue had been earned by engineers working at clients' sites. Now most of it was earned by engineers and agents in Indian cities, at desks a cab ride from home.

## The other employers

The decade left a puzzle that the industry's own figures did not answer. The story told in Nasscom's presentation, and in this chapter so far, is mainly a story of Indian firms: Infosys, Wipro and their rivals building campuses, hiring by the tens of thousands and riding out the crisis. Yet many of the people in this chapter worked for foreign companies. Roy built his first centres inside American Express and GE. The Gurgaon call centres in the activist's report worked for Hewlett-Packard, Citibank, American Express and Deutsche Bank, sometimes in centres those companies owned and sometimes through Indian or foreign contractors across the road.[118] Nasscom's own chart of the industry divided it between Indian providers, the captive centres of global companies, and global service providers.[119]

Blinder's test applied to all of them. Work that could be delivered down a wire could be done in Gurgaon by an Indian firm, by a foreign firm's own Indian office, or by whatever technology came next; on how far the process would go, Blinder admitted, 'No one knows'.[120] The Indian firms were the most visible employers of the period. They were not the only ones. Since 1985, when Texas Instruments began work in India, foreign companies had been building their own engineering centres in the country.[121] Their pay, their work and their career ladders, the next chapter argues, differed from those of the services firms. That chapter follows this second profession.
