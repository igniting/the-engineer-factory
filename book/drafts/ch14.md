# Chapter 14. Backlash and scale (2002-2011)

In February 2004 N. Gregory Mankiw, a Harvard economist then serving as chairman of the White House Council of Economic Advisers, made a remark about trade that the Princeton economist Alan Blinder would later call a 'textbook' statement. Offshoring, the movement of jobs but not of the people who do them from rich countries to poor ones, was in his words 'the latest manifestation of the gains from trade that economists have talked about at least since Adam Smith'. He added: 'More things are tradable than were tradable in the past, and that's a good thing.'[1] The remark caused what Blinder called a national uproar. 'Democratic and Republican politicians alike excoriated Mankiw', Blinder wrote, while economists lined up to defend him.[2]

The politicians were already acting. On 20 February 2004 the trade paper *Government Computer News* (GCN) reported that a provision in the omnibus budget bill President George W. Bush had signed the month before barred companies that won federal work through competitions under the government's Circular A-76 from shifting that work to other countries.[3] The measure was named the Thomas-Voinovich Amendment after its two Republican sponsors, Senator Craig Thomas of Wyoming and Senator George Voinovich of Ohio. GCN described it as 'part of a swell of legislation' against businesses and governments buying software and IT services from countries such as India and China.[4] For one year, work won by a vendor could not be 'performed by the contractor at a location outside the United States'.[5]

The states went further on paper. The National Conference of State Legislatures had counted 29 bills in 19 state legislatures that would require contractors to hire only American citizens or legal residents, regulate agencies' use of offshore call centres, or otherwise restrict foreign purchases.[6] Justin Marks, the conference's research analyst, said that state legislatures had had to fill a $200 billion budget gap over the previous three years and faced a choice between saving money by outsourcing and keeping jobs at home. Over the years, he said, legislators 'have seen manufacturing jobs go overseas and now they see white-collar jobs going abroad'.[7] The television presenter Lou Dobbs published a book that year with the title *Exporting America*.[8]

Not everyone thought the noise would matter. John Kost, a managing vice-president at the research firm Gartner, told GCN that government agencies had been buying offshore software for years. 'People are naive if they think it is not already happening in large quantities,' he said. 'Once the economy improves and politicians move to a different topic, I doubt it will be a potent political issue.'[9]

In India, the programmers whose work the bills were meant to keep at home were not persuaded. That same month *Wired* published a long report by the journalist Daniel Pink from Hexaware Technologies, a software exporter on the edge of Mumbai that he ranked eighteenth among India's software and services exporters. One of its project managers, Aparna Jairam, an engineering graduate of the University of Pune, wrote embedded software for handheld devices, and her main client was a utility company in the western United States. Her salary, Pink reported, was about $11,000 a year, and he set it beside 'your $70,000-a-year job', the job of the American reader he had in mind.[10] A colleague, Kavita Samudra, rejected the premise of the backlash. 'Back in the US, it's all about cheap, cheap, cheap. It's not only about India being cheap. It's quality services.'[11] The answer did not dispose of the charge. Whatever the quality, the client was buying an engineer's time, and in Mumbai that time cost a fraction of the American price. That is the bargain the rest of this book follows, and the backlash was an argument about its price.

Kost was right that the rules would not stop the work, and wrong about the argument. The federal restriction applied to a narrow class of contracts, and for one year.[12] The industry it was aimed at kept growing, and the argument about which work could be sent abroad did not fade. It became sharper, and one version of it, written in 2006, still sets the terms for the last part of this book.

The decade between the dot-com bust and the end of India's software park tax holiday was the one in which the factory reached full size, a second industry of back offices and call centres grew beside it, and the financial crisis of 2008 tested the model.

## The wire test

Alan Blinder was no protectionist. He had served on the Council of Economic Advisers in 1993-94 and as vice-chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1994 to 1996.[13] In an essay in the March/April 2006 issue of *Foreign Affairs* he agreed with Mankiw's economics and disagreed with his sense of scale.[14]

He began modestly. There were no reliable national data, he wrote, but fragmentary studies suggested that 'well under a million service-sector jobs in the United States have been lost to offshoring to date', less than two weeks of normal gross job losses in the churn of the American labour market.[15] The future was another matter. Improvements in technology and communications, he argued, would bring much more offshoring of what he called 'impersonal services', which he defined as 'services that can be delivered electronically over long distances with little or no degradation in quality'.[16] He called the change 'a third Industrial Revolution'.[17]

His central claim was that the line between safe and exposed work would not follow the line between skilled and unskilled work. 'Janitors and crane operators are probably immune to foreign competition; accountants and computer programmers are not.'[18] Typing, a low-skill job, and security analysis, a high-skill one, were both already being delivered electronically from India, though on a small scale so far.[19] The telephone operator who arranged a conference call, or the clerk who took an airline booking by phone, might already be there. 'He or she may be in India already.'[20] His rough estimate of the American service jobs that could be offshored in the electronic future was two to three times the 14 million then in manufacturing, something like 28 to 42 million jobs. He thought the published estimates too low; one of them, he wrote, counted only 'the currently visible tip of the offshoring iceberg'.[21]

The usual answer to such fears in rich countries was more education. Blinder thought that answer incomplete, because the dividing line he described was not a line of education. 'The geeks may not inherit the earth after all', he wrote, 'at least not the highly paid geeks in the rich countries.'[22] He told Americans to worry less about China, whose challenge came mainly in manufacturing, and more about India, with its already strong advantage in English.[23]

One passage in the essay matters more to this book than the rest. 'The dividing line between personal and impersonal services will move over time. As information technology improves, more and more personal services will become impersonal services.'[24] Blinder's test, put shortly, was this: if a task can be sent down a wire without losing quality, it is exposed, however skilled the person who does it. The Indian industry had grown by answering yes for a lengthening list of tasks: first code, then accounts, then the voice on the end of a telephone line. Blinder was writing about the movement of work between countries. His test does not care where the work goes, and the last chapters of this book return to it.

## Reconciliations and call centres

Software was half of the industry's story in these years. The other half was business process outsourcing (BPO): the transfer of a company's back-office work, such as accounts, claims, collections and customer calls, to another site or another firm. Its best-known Indian pioneer was Raman Roy, who helped build the Indian operations of American Express and General Electric before founding his own company. He told his story to *Knowledge at Wharton*, the University of Pennsylvania's business journal, in June 2003.[25]

At American Express's Indian division, by Roy's account, the cost per transaction turned out to be 'lower almost by 40% to 50%' than in the company's more advanced markets. Others in the company did not believe it. 'These guys in India obviously don't know how to make their reports; how could these numbers be true?'[26] Roy put the advantage down to the people. An associate doing bank reconciliations in the United States might have just completed high school and earn '$15,000 to $18,000 or perhaps $20,000'. At those salaries, he said, 'I was able to field professionals who were at least college graduates and who sometimes were CPAs', meaning certified public accountants.[27] This was the arbitrage of the software story applied to the back office: Indian graduates doing work that in rich countries was done by people with less schooling, for less money.

The savings were not only in wages. Paying suppliers faster earned American Express discounts for prompt settlement, and Roy once set those against his unit's salary bill. 'You've got us for free', he told it, 'because the incremental discounts that we got alone were more than the salary bill in India.'[28]

The obstacles were physical. When the team asked the Indian telecommunications company VSNL for 100 per cent up-time, 'they looked at us as though we were from some other world because 90% up-time was considered outstanding'.[29] Roy also described a small piece of invention. His Delhi unit reconciled about 12,000 accounts in the spreadsheet program Lotus 1-2-3, where one slip of the hand could throw an account out of balance. Rather than wait fifteen months for head office's standard platform, Roy had an interim tool built by a local firm run by his former head of technology, Sunil Gujral. When the American package proved slower than expected on test data, the Indian tool reconciled the same files: 'It took only 25% of the time to reconcile the file.' Within three months, he said, it had become American Express's global reconciliation platform.[30] Once a process had been moved and written down, the people running it began to improve it.

Roy joined GE in 1996. Its first Indian project was work GE had already sent to Mexico, and Roy had to quote a lower price than Mexico's; his finance chief told him, 'we will probably lose money on every transaction.'[31] His unit became what he called 'the Five Sigma beta site for GE Capital', a testing ground for the company's quality programme.[32] When he proposed handling live customer calls from India, a very senior executive 'pulled out his pen and crossed out the investment', saying that 'call centers would never happen out of India'. Roy borrowed equipment from vendors for three months and found a client to fund part of a trial. 'It was nationalistic pride that drove me to see that a call center came into being,' he said. By 2003 he believed that more than half of GE Capital's Indian revenue came from its call centres.[33] He went on to found Spectramind, which the Indian software company Wipro bought in 2002.[34]

Whatever the dates, the kind of worker the model sought is clear. In the same interview Roy described Indian BPO staff as 'well qualified, highly educated people' who were 'not a transient workforce'. For them, he said, 'BPO is a full-time career', and they expected employers to think seriously about their careers.[35] V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, put the requirement more plainly. The back office meant accounts receivable, payroll, account reconciliation and similar work, and starting a BPO centre needed little more than overseas clients and 'trainable human resources with good knowledge of English (which was available in reasonable numbers)'.[36] That was the employers' view of the workforce. The workers' own view is harder to find.

## The night shift

The fullest first-hand account of a call-centre floor in these years comes from an anonymous activist, recruited through a German job centre, who spent three months in 2005-06 as a foreign call-centre agent in Gurgaon (now Gurugram), a fast-growing satellite town south of Delhi, and published a report on the libcom.org website in January 2007.[37] It is a political tract, and its figures are the author's. The scholarly studies that follow set it beside other evidence.

The author worked for a research outsourcing firm whose Gurgaon office employed about 800 people, making about 100 outbound calls to companies in Europe in a ten-hour shift.[38] Gurgaon alone, the report estimated, probably had about 150,000 call-centre workers, and one building occupied by Genpact, the former GE Capital operation, held about 12,000.[39] The rules were set by the clients' world: officially, 'the only language allowed for internal communication in the call centre is English'.[40] The agents, mostly in their twenties and about half of them men, had nearly all worked in other call centres nearby.[41]

The report includes short interviews, recorded during breaks and given without names. A 22-year-old woman described her first job interview with a Gurgaon call centre in April 2004, while she was still living in Bhopal; she went to it with her mother. In the next year and a half, by her account, she worked in fourteen call centres and raised her monthly pay from Rs 8,000 to Rs 20,000 (about $180 to $440 at the 2006 average exchange rate) by changing jobs. She had worked six days a week from 2.30 in the morning to 12.30 in the afternoon, calling the United States, Canada and Britain, sometimes on automatic diallers that placed 400 to 500 calls a shift.[42] 'In call centres you mainly learn about working time and discipline,' she said, 'you are physically un-free, but mentally free.'[43]

A 21-year-old woman who had worked on GE Capital's Australian shift, with a cab collecting her at 2.30 in the morning, said she had answered about 100 calls a day and had five minutes to go to the toilet. She had to use an English name. 'I thought it would be fun, but it was more like a prison.'[44] A 22-year-old man from Calcutta (Kolkata since 2001), who described his background as Adivasi (a member of India's indigenous communities), took a technical-support job for Hewlett-Packard in a centre run by Daksh, later taken over by IBM. His training lasted two months and was mainly about using the tools. 'I brought all my software books from the course, because I thought that I would work at IBM now, but I do not need them.'[45]

The same man's basic wage was Rs 10,500 a month. He was expected to sell software and computers to the callers whose problems he had solved; by his account he sold $1,000 to $2,000 worth in a month and received Rs 1,000 to Rs 1,500 in incentives for it.[46] A 24-year-old man who had worked on Citibank's American credit-card process for an outsourcing contractor said the contractor 'made sure that they got the people with the best accent' for that work. Agents on it wore ties, started at Rs 8,500 a month and, he said, could be dismissed for leaving a desk for a minute without logging out.[47]

The report put the basic wage for new agents at about Rs 8,000 a month and the average, with incentives, at Rs 12,000 to Rs 14,000 for a week of about 50 hours on night shifts, which is roughly $265 to $310 at the 2006 average rate.[48] The cleaners and canteen staff in the same building, many of them former small farmers and carpenters from Calcutta and Bihar, were paid about Rs 2,000 a month for 60 to 70 hours a week.[49] The author's impression was that upper-caste surnames were more frequent among the agents, though caste itself was never mentioned.[50] 'Not a single call centre employee I talked to had experience with manual labour,' the report says.[51] The workers were also clear about why the jobs had come: most of those the activists spoke to said that the main reason was wages of about a fifth of those in the United States or Britain, and some said that after five years in call centres you were finished.[52]

Schooling was part of the bargain. The agents were students or former students, several of whom had learned a second foreign language because it paid better, at courses only the upper-middle class could afford.[53] The woman from Bhopal had learned French when she decided that 'only speaking English is not enough of a qualification, because so many people speak English'. She estimated that about 40 per cent of call-centre workers were studying by correspondence, and said that many gave up after a while.[54]

Sociologists recorded the employer's side of that bargain. Carol Upadhya and A.R. Vasavi of the National Institute of Advanced Studies (NIAS) in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), whose fieldwork ran from 2003 to 2006 and included structured interviews with 34 BPO workers, were told by one HR officer that a candidate with a 'very good family background with high percentage of marks in their degree' would not be taken, being likely to be 'looking at call centres as a stopgap arrangement'. One of their respondents had left a postgraduate degree off his c.v. to get a call-centre job.[55] Workers on British projects at one company had to understand 'fourteen different British dialects'.[56] One respondent told them: 'Initially I felt so ashamed that I worked in a call centre that I used to tell people that I'm working for an internet service provider without mentioning any details.'[57]

The pay looked different from inside. Ernesto Noronha and Premilla D'Cruz of the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad interviewed 18 telemarketing agents in Bangalore and Mumbai for a study published in 2007, and quote them without names. One set the pay against the alternatives: 'So repeating 10 lines a day, I will get paid 10000-12000 rupees - amazing, believe me, it is amazing.' Someone working from morning to evening 'in a garment or textile shop', the agent went on, 'wouldn't have been paid even more than 3000 rupees'.[58] Another described the targets that came with the pay: 'everyone knows that for one non-performer, there are five guys outside waiting to replace him.'[59] A third, with an engineering background, had 'got into telemarketing because typical engineering jobs did not pay'.[60]

Not every agent served foreign callers. A domestic industry, much of it answering calls for mobile and other telephone companies, grew beside the export one. Phil Taylor, D'Cruz, Noronha and Dora Scholarios, who surveyed 618 of its employees in 2008, cite a 2008 estimate by the investment bank Avendus Capital of average starting salaries of $2,000 a year there, against $4,500 for agents serving international customers.[61] A team leader at a Chennai outsourcer told them: 'I cannot show that I am under pressure but I have to give this pressure to my agents'.[62]

Some agents organised. UNITES, a union for BPO employees, was formed in September 2005 with backing from the global union federation UNI, and was, in the words of Taylor and his colleagues, 'the first explicit attempt to give BPO employees an independent voice'.[63] Despite attempts in several states, it managed to register only in Karnataka.[64] In 2007 the same researchers surveyed 879 of its members in six cities, a sample of union members rather than of agents at large.[65] A voice agent at a Chennai captive centre, one owned by the client company, told them that 'a technician is supposed to take at least 50-60 calls', that breaks were stopped or postponed, and that 'Once they find that the person has shouted at the customer, he is thrown out of the job.'[66] D'Cruz and Noronha, summarising eight years of fieldwork, found employers blocking the 'bank salary accounts of those absenting themselves' and refusing relieving letters to those who quit, and members who feared 'having one's name blacklisted on NASSCOM's database'.[67]

The night shifts depended on cars. The report describes cab drivers 'tired after seventeen hours shifts' and a colleague killed in a car crash on her way home from work.[68] Transport became a public issue after December 2005, when a woman who worked the night shift for Hewlett-Packard's Bangalore operation was killed, in the words of Taylor and his colleagues, 'by a man purporting to be her driver'.[69] He was a cab driver, and he told her that the car on her usual route was not available and that she would have to travel in his.[70] A fast-track court in Bangalore convicted him in 2010, and the Karnataka High Court upheld the conviction and his life sentence in 2016. In 2023 the Supreme Court, hearing his appeal against sentence, ordered that he serve at least thirty years before he could be released.[71] Its judgment treated the case as one about the industry: 'The issue is of safety and security of women working with such companies.'[72] UNITES won its first tranche of recruits after campaigning for better regulation of transport.[69] One of Upadhya and Vasavi's informants described another risk: 'When a girl gets a drop at some odd hour in the night, neighbours switch on the lights and gaze at her with shock and make comments'.[73] The reviewer Bob Russell writes that Reena Patel's 2010 study of women in the industry found that company transport could 'extend the length of the working day by several hours', and that the mobile phone was used 'to keep track of women who work the night shift'.[74]

## Two million

The industry's own figures for 2008 come from a Nasscom presentation, and they are the claims of an interested party. The number it put first was two million, the 'professionals employed in the sector' directly in FY2007-08, roughly ten times the 200,000 software professionals it had counted, on a narrower definition, for the year to March 1998.[75] The official record of exports, the Reserve Bank of India's balance-of-payments series for software services receipts, rose from $9.6bn in FY2002-03 to $53.1bn in FY2010-11.[76] Rajaraman gives 2.4 million directly employed in IT in 2010,[77] and by the industry's count, which he relays, 166 Indian IT companies had reached level 5, the top level, of the Software Engineering Institute's Capability Maturity Model, and more than 400 of the Fortune 500 companies were clients.[78]

The work was heavily concentrated. Nasscom's map of delivery locations in 2008 gave Bangalore 36 per cent, the New Delhi, Noida and Gurgaon region 17 per cent, Chennai 15 per cent, Mumbai and Pune 15 per cent, Hyderabad 14 per cent and all other places 5 per cent, though its caption said that the industry was 'well spread across multiple locations'.[79] The United States took 61 per cent of exports in FY2006-07, Britain 18 per cent and continental Europe 12 per cent.[80]

The presentation is more revealing about strain than about scale. Its list of the questions clients asked began with attrition and wage inflation: 'is India's talent pool running dry?'[81] Under 'Pressure Points' it listed 'High lateral hiring leading to attrition', meaning the recruiting of experienced staff from rival firms, and 'Tier 3 colleges yields low', and its first point was that talent was trainable but the industry needed 'to make them employable'.[82] Its proposed remedies were an education programme in miniature: an entry-level assessment for BPO and IT recruits, 'finishing schools' to prepare graduates for work, a faculty development programme to make teachers more suitable, and government plans for 20 new Indian Institutes of Information Technology.[83] Nasscom's cost comparison for 2007 put India at about $28,000 against $100,000 for the United States, and projected that India would keep most of that advantage to 2015.[84] That gap was the bargain in a single line, and every pressure point on the list, from wage inflation to lateral hiring, was a threat to it.

The last part of the presentation dealt with a different worry. 'With increasing levels of outsourcing,' it said, 'employees were viewed as a significant weak link'. Nasscom estimated that about 30 per cent of CVs contained false claims, and it noted that India had 'No law addressing privacy or employee rights'.[85] Its answer was the National Skills Registry, a database of employees' verified education and work histories, with photographs and fingerprints, meant, in its words, to 'instill confidence in client about employees'.[86] The industry's workforce had become something its foreign customers wanted to inspect.

## The factory at full size

The largest firms show what this growth meant in practice. Infosys, whose filings with the American Securities and Exchange Commission give the most consistent figures, employed about 25,700 people at the end of March 2004.[87] Its revenue rose from $1,062.6m in FY2003-04, 98.6 per cent of it from exports, to $4,176m in FY2007-08 and $4,663m in FY2008-09.[88] By the FY2008-09 filing its training campus at Mysore (Mysuru since 2014), which Chapter 12 follows, could house 13,500 trainees and train about 40,000 a year, with 476 full-time faculty.[89] One company was running a residential college for tens of thousands of engineers a year, because, as Nasscom put it, the industry needed 'to make them employable'.[82]

## The quarter to March 2009

The financial crisis of 2008 reached the Indian firms through their largest customers, and the clearest record of it is the quarter that closed Infosys's financial year on 31 March 2009. About a third of the company's revenue, 33.9 per cent in FY2008-09, came from the financial services industry.[90] Its filing for that year described 'leading investment banks going bankrupt or being forced to sell themselves in distressed circumstances', and warned that clients might respond with 'contract terminations, deferrals of projects or delays in purchases'.[91]

In that quarter about 66.9 per cent of Infosys's technology professionals, trainees included, were billed to clients, against 70.2 per cent a year earlier.[92] Put the other way, about a third of the technical workforce was on the bench, the pool of trained staff not assigned to paying work, and was being paid without being billed. Pay stood still. 'Until 2009, when we instituted a freeze in salary increases,' the company said, it had reviewed compensation every year.[93] The gate narrowed: Infosys made about 16,900 offers in the year, against about 46,300 the year before.[94] Fewer people left, and attrition fell to 11.1 per cent.[95] Yet the filings show no mass layoffs. Infosys added about 12,400 employees, net of departures, and its headcount rose from about 91,200 in March 2008 to about 104,900 in March 2009 and 113,800 in March 2010.[96]

This was the bargain under strain. A firm that sold engineers' time had hired ahead of demand, and when demand paused it carried the unsold time on its own books rather than shed the people the next upturn would need. Part of the cost fell on the graduates who in a normal year would have been hired, and part on employees whose pay stood still. The FY2009-10 filing reported average rises that year of 8 per cent for offshore staff and 2 per cent for those working at client sites abroad, against 11-13 per cent and 4-5 per cent in FY2008-09, and the annual rise, normally made in April, was postponed to October 2009.[97]

Clients also pushed costs onto their suppliers. In response to smaller client budgets, Infosys said, it was offering more of its work at fixed prices and on fixed timetables, and such projects rose to 35.4 per cent of services revenue in FY2008-09 from 31.0 per cent the year before.[98] The same filing reported 'increased demand from our clients that all risks associated with foreign exchange fluctuations be borne by us'.[99] The filings also described clients consolidating their suppliers to cut costs, and long contracts with benchmarking provisions, under which a client could commission a study comparing the supplier's prices with those of its rivals and seek lower fees.[100]

The pattern had appeared before. In 2004, after the dot-com bust, Infosys had said that 'declines in demand, pricing pressures for our services, appreciation of the rupee against the dollar and increased wage pressures in India' had kept it from 'historical levels of profitability'.[101] On the evidence of its filings, Infosys met the second downturn by slowing hiring, carrying a larger bench, holding down pay and absorbing pressure on prices, while its headcount kept rising. By FY2010-11 utilisation including trainees was back to 72.1 per cent, and in FY2011-12 the company made about 41,460 offers.[102]

The crisis also showed how much of the model still depended on people abroad. At the end of March 2009 most of Infosys's technology professionals in the United States held either H-1B visas, about 8,200 people, or intra-company L-1 visas, about 1,400.[103] About 20,200 of its 104,900 employees worked outside India.[104] Those visa holders were the narrow top of the onsite-offshore pyramid, and the part of it most exposed to American politics (Chapter 13).

## From parks to zones

The state's support for the industry changed form in these years. The tax holiday of the 1990s had been tied to software technology parks: export profits of units in the parks were exempt from income tax. The Finance Act of 2005 introduced a separate holiday for units in designated special economic zones (SEZs) that began providing services on or after 1 April 2005. Such units could deduct all their export profits from taxable income for five years and half of them for a further five, with more relief available on conditions.[105] The Special Economic Zones Act was passed the same year.[106] Rajaraman counts 257 software and services companies set up in SEZs by 2007, and writes that many BPO centres were placed in the zones because profits earned there were tax exempt.[107]

The park holiday was extended and then allowed to end. Infosys reported that the Finance Act of 2009 extended the holiday for park units until 31 March 2011, and that for its remaining park units it would expire by the end of that fiscal year.[108] Four of its centres operated in SEZs by March 2009 and eight by March 2012, and 'many of our proposed development centers are likely to operate in SEZs'.[109] The FY2011-12 filing said that the expiry of tax holidays had already hurt its profit margin.[110]

The change favoured firms large enough to build or lease whole zones, and it moved the industry from office towers into walled campuses. It did not remove the old frustrations. Roy, who had set up centres for three companies, said in 2003 that the government promised a 'single-window clearance', but 'there are 25 single windows you need to go to'. For one new centre in Pune he counted 119 places where he had signed.[111]

By the end of the period the offshore model had largely won. The Reserve Bank's survey for 2010-11 found that about four-fifths of software and IT services export revenue, 79.3 per cent, was earned from work done in India, against 74.6 per cent in 2007-08; the United States took about 63.6 per cent.[112] The same survey put IT-enabled services (ITES) and BPO at about 26.3 per cent of these exports in 2010-11, down from 31.0 per cent the year before.[113] In the 1980s most Indian software export revenue had been earned by engineers working at clients' sites (Chapter 9). Now most of it was earned by engineers and agents in Indian cities, at desks a cab ride from home.

## The other employers

Many of the people in this chapter worked for foreign companies. Roy built his first centres inside American Express and GE, and the Gurgaon call centres in the activist's report worked for Hewlett-Packard, Citibank, American Express and Deutsche Bank, sometimes in centres those companies owned and sometimes through contractors.[114] Nasscom's own chart divided the industry between Indian providers, the captive centres of global companies and global service providers.[115] Foreign companies had also been building engineering centres of their own in India since the mid-1980s, and the next chapter follows that second profession.[116]

Blinder's test applied to all of them, whoever owned the desk. It asked only whether the work could be sent down a wire, and in these years the list of work that could be sent kept growing. Blinder did not pretend to know where it would stop. 'No one knows how far this process will go.'[117]
