# Epilogue. India in 2035

In October 2025 NITI Aayog, the Indian government's policy think-tank, published a 28-page report called *Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy*. It was prepared by NITI's Frontier Tech Hub with the partnership of Nasscom, the software industry's lobby group, and the consultancy BCG.[1] Two forewords open it. The second is signed by Debjani Ghosh, a Distinguished Fellow at NITI, and it puts the choice in one sentence: 'By 2031, India's technology sector stands at a crossroads: we could lose 1.5 million jobs or create up to 4 million new opportunities.'[2]

The executive summary turns those words into two endpoints. In what it calls a business-as-usual case, 'the headcount in the tech services sector could go down from 7.5 to 8 million in 2023 to 6 million by 2031'. If India acted on the report's advice, the total 'could swell to 10 million'.[3] The two numbers sit a few lines apart on the same page. Between them lies a difference of four million working lives, and the report's case is that policy decides which way the country goes.

The numbers need three cautions before they can be used. The first is the base. On one page the report calls the 7.5 to 8 million the employment of the tech services industry, but on another it explains that the figure is a 'total tech workforce' that includes 'tech jobs in other sectors', outside the software industry itself.[4] It is not the industry headcount that Nasscom publishes. Nasscom estimated the industry's workforce at 5.8 million for the year to March 2025, and the two figures are not measured on the same basis.[5] The second is consistency. On one page the report says that 'by 2030, we can add 2 million more new jobs'; in the foreword the upside is up to 4 million by 2031.[6] The third is interest. The report was written to argue for an 'India AI Talent Mission', a new national programme of AI teaching and retraining, and its two paths are framed to show the cost of not adopting it.[7] That does not make the numbers wrong. It makes them advocacy, published under a government's name and co-written by the industry that would benefit from the spending.

The report also has characters. To show what AI does to ordinary jobs, it describes a junior quality-assurance engineer in Pune named Rekha, whose work is writing and running software tests, and a first-line support agent in Chennai named Aman, who sorts help-desk tickets.[8] They are illustrations invented by the report's authors, not reported people; the report gives no surnames and no sign that they are real. A document about millions of jobs presents its workers as examples it made up.

This book looked for a forecast of Indian software employment in 2035 and did not find one. The furthest official projection is NITI's, to 2031. The only mention of 2035 in that report is a proposed vision statement for the talent mission: 'To position India as the global epicentre of AI talent by 2035'.[9] That is a goal, not an estimate. The 2035 in this epilogue's title is therefore the book's own horizon, about ten years from the time of writing. What follows are four scenarios. They are this book's scenarios, not anyone's prediction. Each takes one force that earlier chapters documented and asks what India would look like if that force dominated for a decade. Each comes with signposts, things a reader could check in 2030, and with the evidence that cuts against it.

## The machine at the start

The scenarios start from what the book has shown. For about thirty years, the Indian software industry's revenue and its headcount grew together, because the core product was an engineer's time. Revenue was, roughly, the number of people billed multiplied by the rate for each hour or month. The colleges, coaching centres and company training campuses described in earlier chapters were built to feed that equation, and they fed it at enormous scale.

By 2026 the equation had loosened. Nasscom reported $315 billion in revenue for the year to March 2026 and 5.95 million direct employees, with 135,000 net additions. Its president Rajesh Nambiar spoke, in the Press Trust of India's paraphrase, of a 'non-linearity' between job growth of 2.3% and revenue growth of 6.1%.[10] Those are the lobby group's own estimates. Counted company by company, the five largest firms ended the same year with 6,981 fewer people than they started with, according to a *Business Standard* compilation.[11] Infosys made 87,286 offers of employment in its 2026 fiscal year, against 222,500 at the hiring peak of fiscal 2022.[12]

The supply side had been changing too, and earlier than the AI story suggests. According to an analysis of the regulator's data by the education charity Avanti Fellows, the approved undergraduate intake of engineering colleges under the All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE) peaked at about 1.7 million seats in 2014-15 and fell to about 1.25 million by 2021-22, with roughly a third of those seats left empty.[13] The same analysis found AICTE's own data on graduates who were placed in jobs unusable.[14] The country that built an engineer factory cannot yet measure, from official figures, how many of its graduates find engineering work.

Two people stand at the ends of this book's present. One is a trainee whom a *News Minute* reporter, Balakrishna Ganeshan, called Gayatri, a pseudonym his report marks as a changed name. She graduated in 2022, cleared Infosys's interview for a system-engineer post that year, waited nearly two years to be brought in, joined in October 2024 and was let go from the Mysuru training campus in February 2025 after failing assessments. 'They wasted two and a half years of my career,' she told him.[15] Infosys said its trainees get three attempts at the tests and that the process had existed for more than two decades.[16] The other is Rishabh Mishra, who joined the Indian Institute of Information Technology, Design and Manufacturing in Jabalpur in 2022. *Rest of World* reported in December 2025 that fewer than a quarter of his 400 classmates had secured job offers, with the course due to end in May 2026. 'It is really bad out there,' he said.[17] The four scenarios are, among other things, four answers to what their degrees will be worth.

## The thinning pyramid

The first scenario supposes that clients stop paying for hours and keep the savings from AI for themselves.

An Indian services firm has long been described as a pyramid: a wide base of junior engineers, a narrower band of team leads, and a few senior managers at the top. The base was where the money was made, because juniors were billed at rates well above their pay. In this scenario the base is what shrinks. The firms survive, smaller at the bottom and older on average, and the engineer factory turns out graduates whom the firms no longer need in volume.

The evidence for it is in the firms' own filings. In its annual report to American regulators for fiscal 2024, Infosys said that, because of the attention drawn by AI's ability to generate code, clients had demanded higher commitments to productivity improvements.[18] By fiscal 2026 it wrote that 'clients themselves are selectively insourcing capabilities as AI improves productivity'.[19] Wipro's report for the same year forecast that 'Hiring will shift from volume to skills'.[20] In May 2026 *Forbes India* reported on what it called AI deflation. HCLTech's chief executive C. Vijayakumar told investors that 'A $100 million deal would be much less today', maybe $80 million for the same scope. The broker Motilal Oswal estimated that 12-15% of the sector's revenue faced what it called direct AI-driven displacement risk.[21] The NITI report itself describes the mechanism. In an IT services company, it says, AI 'typically translates to a shrinking of entry-level roles involving QA and support'.[22]

For education the consequence is stark. Colleges would keep producing graduates trained for the base of a pyramid that is narrowing. The prologue's waiting fresher would become the normal case, not the exception, and the joining date would stop being a date.

The signposts are measurable. Fresher offers as a share of total headcount at the five largest firms would fall year after year. Revenue per employee would rise while headcount stayed flat or fell. Company filings would show more fixed-price and outcome-based contracts, in which the client pays for a result rather than for time. Engineering intake would stay high while the share of graduates in engineering jobs fell, which is why the missing placement data matters.

The counter-evidence is just as concrete. Infosys's offers had recovered from 26,975 in fiscal 2024 to 87,286 in fiscal 2026.[23] Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) onboarded about 24,000 freshers in the first half of its 2027 fiscal year, and said it would hire on demand.[24] Nasscom still reported net hiring.[25] And the delayed joining dates that left Gayatri waiting began in 2022, two years before Infosys's annual filings first mentioned AI code generation.[26] By 2026 the pyramid had not visibly thinned. The scenario supposes that it will.

## The diamond

The second scenario supposes that AI creates new paid work, and that the price of each remaining engineer rises.

In this version the shape changes from a pyramid to a diamond: fewer people at the bottom, a wide middle, and a narrow top. The image comes from the staffing executive Kamal Karanth, who, in a Reuters Breakingviews column's paraphrase, described a diamond with a big middle layer replacing the old pyramid.[27] The people in the middle are paid for judgement, for knowledge of a client's business and for checking and directing what AI tools produce. Wipro's description of future hiring, 'emphasizing AI fluency, domain expertise, and productivity-based workforce models', reads like the job advertisement.[28]

The evidence comes partly from the companies and partly from the NITI report. Nasscom put the industry's AI revenue at $10-12 billion in the year to March 2026.[29] TCS's chief executive K. Krithivasan told investors in April 2026: 'Our expectation is AI will be net accretive.' He expected AI revenue to overcompensate for traditional revenue that would 'slowly taper down', though he added that the timelines could vary.[30] NITI, Nasscom and BCG went further. 'We estimate the IT Services industry to grow 3-9% annually,' the roadmap says, and it expects 'the price-per-FTE for the industry to grow 5-8% annually over the next few years'.[31] FTE means full-time equivalent, one person's working time. On that view each engineer becomes more valuable, and the upside path to 10 million tech jobs opens.

For education this scenario is kinder but harder. It needs fewer graduates who can follow a specification and more who can judge whether a system is right. AICTE declared 2025 its 'Year of Artificial Intelligence' and its chairman T.G. Sitharam said a committee of experts was revising the model curricula so that AI could be taught in all branches of engineering.[32] No revised curriculum had been found by the time of writing. Earlier chapters showed that AICTE approves what colleges should teach but cannot make them teach it well.

The signposts would be revenue per employee and average pay rising together, lateral hiring of experienced engineers outpacing campus hiring, and AI revenue growing faster than total revenue, reported on definitions that can be compared between firms. At present they cannot. Gaurav Vasu of the research firm UnearthInsight told *Business Standard* that AI revenue was still under 5-7% of the top five firms' revenue and that smaller firms did not report it; Nasscom's president said that not all companies report AI revenue.[33]

The counter-evidence is the same as the first scenario's evidence. NITI's forecast of rising price per worker dates from October 2025. Seven months later HCLTech's chief executive, quoted by *Forbes India*, was describing, as a rough estimate, deals worth a fifth less for the same work.[34] The two claims measure different things, price per person and value per deal, but they point in opposite directions, and nothing published by 2026 settles which is right.

## The client moves in

The third scenario supposes that multinationals stop buying Indian engineers' time from Indian vendors and employ the engineers themselves.

This has a long history in the book. Texas Instruments, by its own account, began operating a software design centre in Bangalore in 1985, and Chapter 15 followed the foreign-owned engineering centres that came after it.[35] The industry now calls them global capability centres (GCCs): offices that a foreign company owns and staffs in India to do its own technology, research or back-office work. In September 2024 Nasscom and the consultancy Zinnov counted about 1,700 companies with GCCs in India, employing 1.9 million people, with revenue of $64.6 billion in the year to March 2024. They projected $99-105 billion by 2030, and 2,100-2,200 companies employing 2.5 to 2.8 million people.[36] Namita Adavi of Zinnov said India's long history as an offshoring centre had 'created a flywheel effect in terms of the talent that is being churned out'.[37]

By May 2026, according to *Business Today*'s report of the two organisations' next study, the count had reached 2,117 centres (a different unit from the earlier count of companies) employing 2.36 million people, with revenue of about $98.4 billion in the year to March 2026.[38] On those figures the 2030 revenue projection was close to being met four years early, though employment was still below the projected range. Cognizant, a company headquartered in the United States, had about 351,600 employees at the end of 2025, of whom 256,900 were in India.[39] Infosys listed the risk plainly in its report for fiscal 2025: clients might build their own technology capability 'by setting up or expanding their own GCC in India'.[40] A year later it wrote that clients were selectively insourcing.[41] Karnataka's state policy for 2024-29 set a target of attracting 500 new GCCs to the state by 2029.[42]

In this scenario Indian engineers keep their jobs but change employers. The national champions that the book has followed since the 1970s, TCS, Infosys and Wipro, would matter less, and the Indian offices of foreign companies would matter more. It would bring back the question Chapter 5 asked about IBM's departure in 1978: who owns the capability that Indians build? For education it might change little. GCCs hire from the same colleges, though they may hire fewer people at higher pay.

The signposts would be GCC headcount growing while the large services firms' headcount stayed flat; services firms earning more from building and running GCCs for clients; office leasing in Bengaluru and Hyderabad led by GCCs; and states beyond Karnataka adopting their own policies under the national framework for states that the 2025-26 Union Budget promised to formulate.[43]

The counter-evidence is about the numbers themselves. Every GCC figure here comes from Nasscom, the lobby group, and Zinnov, a consultancy that sells advice to GCCs. Both have an interest in a growing count. Definitions have shifted before: in September 2024 Nasscom restated its GCC revenue and headcount after one of its five-yearly surveys, and a year after estimating industry revenue for the year to March 2025 at $282.6 billion it revised the figure to $297 billion, a revision for which the published report gives no reason.[44] No independent count of GCCs or their employees was found for this book.

## The builder state

The fourth scenario supposes that barriers abroad and ambitions at home turn the industry inward, with the Indian state as builder and buyer.

The route that carried Indian engineers to American client sites since the 1990s was the H-1B visa, the subject of Chapter 13. On 19 September 2025 a presidential proclamation required a payment of $100,000 for new H-1B petitions.[45] A second proclamation, in September 2026, extended the measure to 2027. It claimed that the largest IT staffing and outsourcing firms had cut their combined H-1B registrations from 24,946 to 2,055, 'a 92 percent decrease'.[46] That is the administration's figure, and it names no firms. By early October 2026 two federal courts had ruled against the fee or its implementing policies, so the route was contested rather than closed.[47] The decline also began before the fee. According to the National Foundation for American Policy, a pro-immigration research group, TCS's approved petitions for initial employment fell from 1,452 in the American fiscal year 2024 to 846 in 2025, while Amazon's rose to 4,644.[48]

At home, the state has a record of building at population scale. Chapters 16 and 17 told how it built Aadhaar, the biometric identity system, and UPI, the payments network, and asked why India's largest platforms came from the state rather than from private product firms. In March 2024 the government launched the IndiaAI Mission with an outlay of Rs 10,372 crore (Rs 103.7 billion, about $1.25 billion).[49] It selected the start-up Sarvam and others to build foundation models, the large general-purpose AI systems on which applications are built, and Sarvam released models called Sarvam-30B and Sarvam-105B in early 2026.[50] The NITI roadmap proposes an 'India Open-Source AI Commons' and a 'federated national compute and innovation grid'.[51] Nasscom reported that domestic revenue was set to grow 7.9% in the year to March 2026, faster than the industry as a whole.[52]

In this scenario more Indian engineers work on Indian problems for Indian users, through the state, its contractors and domestic product firms. It is the scenario in which the book's first question, why India built services rather than products, might begin to get a different answer. For education it would mean colleges training engineers for a home market that has rarely been their main destination.

The signposts would be government spending on AI and digital infrastructure sustained beyond one budget cycle; Indian-language models in use in state services; domestic revenue rising as a share of the industry's total; and a rising share of new graduates hired by domestic product firms and public bodies. GitHub, the code-hosting company, projects that India will have 57.5 million or more developers on its platform by 2030, ahead of the United States.[53] That counts accounts on one company's service, not jobs, but a rising number of Indians writing code for Indian users would show up there first.

The counter-evidence is serious. *Inc42* judged that Sarvam's models, while competitive, typically trail the leading foreign models in 'raw reasoning depth'.[54] The NITI report itself records that India's share of granted AI patents fell from 8-10% in 2010 to under 5% in 2023, according to the 2025 AI Index, and that India loses more AI talent to migration than it gains.[55] Building identity and payments systems was a feat of engineering and organisation. Building frontier AI needs far more capital, and the state's record in the first may not carry over to the second.

## Reading the signposts

The four scenarios are not exclusive. The pyramid can thin at the base while the middle thickens; GCCs can grow while the state builds; visa barriers can push work offshore to the very GCCs that take it from Indian vendors. The likeliest India of 2035 combines them in proportions that no source available to this book can predict, and this book does not try.

What it can say is where to look. Several of the signposts are numbers that India does not yet collect well. AICTE's placement data were found unusable.[56] Nasscom's headcount is an industry body's estimate, revised as definitions change. GCC counts come from parties that sell to GCCs. AI revenue has no common definition. The NITI roadmap's own base mixes the industry with every other employer of technical staff. A reader in 2030 who wants to know which future is arriving will need, first, better measures of the present: an independent count of software employment, a placement series for engineering graduates, and revenue per employee for the large firms, published on comparable terms.

The book's history also suggests a caution about forecasts. In 2024 Nasscom and Zinnov projected GCC revenue for 2030, and on their own later count it was nearly reached by 2026.[57] In October 2025 NITI expected the price of each engineer to rise; by May 2026 *Forbes India* was reporting AI deflation.[58] Projections in this industry have been overtaken or contradicted within a few years, and most of them were made by people with an interest in the answer.

## The joining date

The book began with a joining date that kept moving. For Gayatri the date came after nearly two years, and the job lasted four months. She told the reporter it had been her dream job.[59] For Rishabh Mishra and his classmates in Jabalpur, the reporting ends before any date. Some of them were thinking of further study instead. Mishra was doubtful: 'after one year, if you return to the job market, your degree is even more irrelevant'.[60]

His sentence is about education as much as about jobs. For half a century the engineering degree in India was a ticket to a particular kind of work: billable hours, learned first on a company training campus and then sold to a client by the month. The colleges, the entrance examinations, the coaching towns and the company campuses were built around that ticket. Each of the four scenarios keeps some of that machinery and discards some. In the thinning pyramid the degree buys less. In the diamond it buys more, but only for those taught to judge as well as to code. In the third scenario it buys a job with a different employer. In the fourth it buys a place in building something for India itself.

None of the evidence in this book says which. It says that the engineer factory was built on purpose, by the state and the companies and millions of families, in answer to the questions of its time. The question for the next ten years is what a joining date means when the factory's product is no longer hours.
