# Chapter 9. 1991 (1990-1997)

On 24 July 1991 Manmohan Singh, a professional economist who had been finance minister for about a month, rose in the Lok Sabha to present the budget for the year to March 1992 (FY1991-92). He began with a dedication to Rajiv Gandhi, the former prime minister, who had been assassinated during the election campaign that spring.[1] Then he described what the new government had inherited. 'The new Government, which assumed office barely a month ago, inherited an economy in deep crisis. The balance of payments situation is precarious.'[2] India had borrowed from the International Monetary Fund in July 1990 and again in January 1991, and its foreign-exchange reserves had still fallen sharply. 'We have been at the edge of a precipice since December 1990 and more so since April 1991,' he said. 'We have not experienced anything similar in the history of independent India.'[3]

On his second page he gave the number that made the crisis concrete. 'The current level of foreign exchange reserves, in the range of Rs.2500 crores, would suffice to finance imports for a mere fortnight.'[4] Rs 2,500 crore was Rs 25 billion, about $1.1 billion at that year's average exchange rate.[5]

On page 20 of a speech that ran to thirty-one pages, the minister came to software. It got one paragraph. 'Our software industry has made considerable progress in recent years. However, there is still a vast unexploited potential for growth. It is time we make all-out efforts to capture the overseas software market.' To that end he proposed to extend to software exports the income-tax concession under section 80HHC of the Income-tax Act, which gave a deduction for profits from exports. 'With this concession, the exports of this industry should register rapid growth.'[6] Eleven pages later he closed by quoting Victor Hugo, that 'no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come', and named the idea as India's emergence as a major economic power. 'Let the whole world hear it loud and clear. India is now wide awake. We shall prevail. We shall overcome.'[7]

The prediction came true. Software exports, by one count, rose from $128 million in the year to March 1991 to $1,759 million in the year to March 1998, an average of about 45 per cent a year.[8] That growth, and the date at which it began, has made 1991 the most famous year in the story of Indian software. Founders date their industry's take-off from it, and a common view holds that the industry is a product of the liberalisation that began that summer. On the documents, 1991 changed money: the exchange rate, the right to spend foreign currency, and the right to raise capital. It did much less to change the work itself, which was still mostly sold by the hour or the month, and much of it still done at the client's site abroad. It also changed, more quietly, who was recruited into the work and how.

## A fortnight of imports

Singh traced the crisis to deficits at home and abroad. The central government's fiscal deficit was more than 8 per cent of gross domestic product in 1990-91. External debt had reached about 23 per cent of GDP, and debt service took 'about 21 per cent of current account receipts in 1990-91'. These strains, he said, 'were stretched to a breaking point on account of the Gulf crisis last year'.[9] V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, puts it more bluntly. India 'was about to default on foreign loans', and it was bailed out by the IMF on conditions that included devaluing the rupee and opening the country to foreign investors.[10]

The response came in stages. The rupee was devalued on 1 and 3 July 1991. The budget speech described those 'exchange rate adjustments' and a liberalised system of import licences as 'the beginning of a transition from a regime of quantitative restrictions to a price based mechanism'.[11] Annual averages show the size of the change: about Rs 17.5 to the dollar in 1990 and about Rs 25.9 in 1992.[12]

On foreign capital the minister turned a long tradition around. 'After four decades of planning for industrialisation, we have now reached a stage of development where we should welcome, rather than fear, foreign investment. Our entrepreneurs are second to none. Our industry has come of age.' Foreign equity of up to 51 per cent in high-priority industries would get prompt approval.[13] He also promised full statutory powers to the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), transferring to it powers then held by the Controller of Capital Issues and the government, so that it could regulate the stock exchanges.[14]

The software paragraph sat inside this argument about foreign exchange. Two paragraphs after it, Singh extended the same logic to professionals generally. Their 'scientific, technical and professional skills, knowledge and experience' had, he said, 'an increasing capacity to earn foreign exchange for the country'.[15] In July 1991 software entered national economic policy as a way to earn dollars. It was not described as an industrial capability to be built for its own sake. That framing was continuous with the export obligations of the 1970s and 1980s, when firms were allowed to import computers only if they promised to export.

## What came before July

The reforms did not create the infrastructure on which the next decade's offshore work would run. The Software Technology Parks (STPs), government-run sites that offered exporters shared satellite earth stations and a single window for official permissions, had opened at Pune, Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) and Bhubaneswar in 1990, and the body that ran them, Software Technology Parks of India, says it was registered as an autonomous society on 5 June 1991, seven weeks before the budget.[16] The economists Gaurav Khanna and Nicolas Morales date the setting up of satellite links in the parks to 1992, which they say began 'negating the need for some kinds of on-site work'.[17] The parks were planned before the reforms and began to matter after them.

The government's export targets had a longer history of disappointment. Nagy Hanna, in a World Bank study published in 1994, recorded that it had set targets of $400 million by 1992 and $800 million by 1995.[18] On Rajaraman's figures exports were $225 million in 1992-93 and $734 million in 1995-96; the $800 million mark was passed only in 1996-97, when exports reached $1,100 million.[19] The targets were missed, and then the trade overtook them.

## Fifty visits to Delhi

The best-known account of what 1991 did for software comes from N.R. Narayana Murthy, who founded Infosys in 1981 with six colleagues.[20] In an oral history recorded for the Computer History Museum in Bangalore in January 2020, he said 'there is absolutely no doubt that for an industry to take off and demonstrate rapid growth you need the blessings of the government'. He dated his industry's growth from 1992 and credited the reforms of 1991 with three changes.[21]

The first was the end of licensing for computer imports. Before it, he said, 'it would take us fifty visits to Delhi and about three years of elapsed time before we got a license to import a computer of about 300,000 US dollars'.[22] The second was convertibility on the current account, the freedom to buy foreign currency for trade and travel. 'Before that, if I wanted to travel abroad even for a day, I had to apply to the Reserve Bank of India (the Central Bank), wait for about 15 days, get the permission and then travel.' He told of a fellow entrepreneur who had permission for two days in Paris and one in Frankfurt. A prospect moved, so he spent one day in Paris and two in Frankfurt, and reported this honestly on his return. 'He promptly got a show cause notice from the Reserve Bank!'[23]

The third change concerned the stock market. Until then an official in Delhi, the Controller of Capital Issues, decided whether a company could offer shares to the public and at what price. In Murthy's words, 'That government officer had no idea what the capital markets were'. The controller priced issues on past performance, so most were at par, the shares' face value. 'Therefore, most entrepreneurs were uncomfortable about going public because they gave away a lot of their equity and raised very little money.' When the office was abolished and companies could set their own prices with their bankers, Murthy said, 'that made life so much easier for us and the industry took off'.[24]

Rajaraman, drawing on Murthy, adds that convertibility let firms 'send software engineers to clients' sites at short notice'.[25] He also credits the devaluation, which, he writes, 'enabled Indian software companies to be competitive in selling their services', and a run of smaller concessions that followed it, from ten years' freedom from tax on export earnings to private satellite links to overseas customers; multinationals could now own their Indian companies outright.[26]

Murthy is an interested witness, a founder whose company went public under the new regime. But the three changes he names have something in common. None of them changed what Indian firms sold. They changed what it cost a firm to move people and money across borders and to raise capital at home. For a business that sold the time of programmers, many of whom worked at clients' sites abroad, the right to send an engineer to America at short notice, without a fortnight's wait for the Reserve Bank, was close to the heart of the business.

## Only 64 brilliant young engineers

Four months after the budget, on Tuesday 26 November 1991, Infosys took half a page in the Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) edition of the *Times of India*. Above a photograph of a crowd on a city street, with crosshairs on the face of one young man, the headline read: 'Only 64 brilliant young engineers are destined to conquer the world of software. Find out if you are one of them this Saturday.'[27]

The advertisement shows in unusual detail how the profession was assembled in these years. It asked for 'young male/female engineers in any discipline (or science graduates plus MCAs)', the last being holders of the Master of Computer Applications degree. The firm wanted people who had been 'among the top 10 rank holders in their discipline' with 'an aggregate score of 65%. Nothing less will do.' Then came the line that matters most to the argument of this book: 'Ideally we expect you to have up to one year of work experience in any field of engineering. A computer software background is not essential.'[28]

Infosys was buying general ability and proposing to make programmers of it. The advertisement described the conversion. Training, it said, would be 'on live and simulated projects using our in-house facilities', which it listed as two IBM AS/400 minicomputers, a VAX 3400 and several Unix machines running Oracle. Recruits would share the experience of two holders of doctorates from the Indian Institute of Management who were dedicated to training. 'Training at Infosys, as some of our past recruits have discovered, is like doing your post graduation and being paid for it.'[29] A World Bank study three years later would recommend exactly this kind of conversion. Infosys was already doing it, and selling the training as a reason to join.

The pay was stated plainly. 'You will join us as a Software Engineer Trainee and will be initially based at Bangalore. The gross annual pay package is Rs.45,000 plus.' After twelve months' probation the trainee would be confirmed as a software engineer, and 'The best can expect to earn a gross annual compensation of Rs.57,000 on completion of the one year training period.'[30] Rs 45,000 was about $2,000 at the 1991 average exchange rate, and Rs 57,000 about $2,500.[31] A separate box offered a higher starting salary to engineers with one or two years in the software industry; the figure is illegible in the surviving copy.[32]

The rest of the page sold a life. 'The job is transferable, so you can look forward to jet setting not only in India, but all over the world.' The recruit would join a team of 400 people drawn from all over India. The firm said it had offices in Bangalore, Delhi, Bombay, Atlanta, Boston and Paris, a joint venture with the American consultancy Kurt Salmon Associates, and clients that included General Electric, Reebok and Polo Ralph Lauren. Forty engineers in Bangalore, it said, were working on a large project for Reebok UK. Under a heading promising fast promotion without a change of employer, it claimed that people who had joined as software engineers had risen to be vice presidents within six years.[33] Of Bangalore it said: 'The garden city, as it is called, has a quaint ambience of its own.'[34]

The selection was compressed into a weekend. Candidates were to come to the Leela Kempinski hotel in Bombay at 9 a.m. on Saturday 30 November, with their bio-data and certificates. After an initial screening on qualifications came 'a round of brain teasers, IQ and aptitude tests'; a box of tips warned that the test had sections on arithmetical reasoning and analytical skills. Those who did well would be interviewed on the Sunday, and the successful were guaranteed an appointment letter on the Monday. The heading over that promise read: 'Your girlfriend is sure going to miss you next Monday'.[35] Despite 'male/female' in the call, the page seems to have imagined its reader as a young man. Senior professionals were invited to see Nandan Nilekani or K. Dinesh, two of the founders, at the same hotel.[36]

How many came is not documented. A 2017 article on a business website, which reproduced the advertisement, says Murthy recalled about 1,500 people turning up and a crush at the hotel, but it gives no source for the recollection, and it misreads the Rs 57,000 as Rs 52,000.[37]

## Seventy thousand applications

By the end of the decade the Saturday test at a Bombay hotel had become a national sorting machine. Infosys's registration statement for its American share listing, filed in February 1999, described it. The company focused its recruiting 'on the top 20% of students from engineering departments of Indian schools' and chose them through tests and interviews, selecting 'on the basis of their ability to learn as well as their academic achievement'. In the year to March 1998 it received about 70,870 applications, tested about 21,350, interviewed about 5,800, made about 1,790 offers and saw about 1,300 accepted. A faculty of 33, twenty of them with master's degrees or doctorates, ran fourteen-week training courses for new recruits.[38]

The numbers say something about the education that fed the industry. Fewer than one applicant in fifty became an Infosys employee. The test, not the degree, did the sorting, and the firm's own course, not the college, taught the work. The same filing gave the national picture as an argument for investors: according to the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, the number of software professionals had grown from about 56,000 in the year to March 1990 to about 200,000 in the year to March 1998, and India had more than 1,800 engineering colleges and technical institutes training about 68,000 graduates a year in information technology.[39] Other counts, Rajaraman's among them, differ by source and definition.[40] The firm itself grew from about 480 IT professionals in the year to March 1994 to about 2,200 in the year to March 1998.[41]

The selling point to clients was price. Rajaraman writes that 'The salary of software engineers in India was about 20% that of an equivalent western engineer.'[42] The Infosys filing, citing the consultancy Software Productivity Research, put the average Indian software wage at 'approximately 15% of the average U.S. rate'.[43] A sharper comparison, which Khanna and Morales quote from a 1992 study, is that in 1989 'an Indian computer professional earned about $5486, whereas Microsoft was offering $40,000 plus relocation benefits and a green card'.[44] The trainee's Rs 45,000 in 1991 was well below even the 1989 figure, though the two are not the same measure.

## Going public

Infosys had meant to sell shares to the public in 1991. Murthy said in 2020 that he had wanted to list within ten years of founding, as American companies did. The assassination of Rajiv Gandhi in May 1991 and the arrival of the reforms led the firm to wait. In 1992 the stock market was shaken by what he called 'a huge stock exchange scam', the securities scam associated with Harshad Mehta, and on 6 December 1992 a mosque, the Babri Masjid, was demolished. Each time, Murthy recalled, the firm was advised to wait.[45]

'So, we went public on 24th of February 1993. It was a very tough time. We barely managed it. Nobody had any idea what a software company was.' Infosys was, he said, the second software company to go public, after Mastek in December 1992. The offer ran from 24 to 27 February, but the shares were listed only in June. 'It opened pretty well at about 50 percent premium.'[46] The company's 1999 filing says that in 1993 it raised 'approximately $4.4 million in gross aggregate proceeds' from its initial public offering, and it names the Bangalore Stock Exchange.[47]

The listing mattered to Murthy for reasons beyond the money. He said that from the start the company would give its accounts to any journalist, and that going public was meant 'to convince the decision makers of our customer organizations in the U.S. that we were a transparent company'. From 1995 it voluntarily prepared accounts under American accounting rules.[48] A firm that sold its engineers' time to American corporations was also selling trust, and a public listing was a way to show it.

## The hotel rooms

In May 1995 Infosys's largest customer came to renegotiate. Murthy's account of the episode, in the same oral history, shows in detail how the trade was priced. General Electric (GE) had chosen four Indian firms, Infosys among them, after a presentation to Rajiv Gandhi's government in the 1980s. Infosys began working for GE in 1990. GE renegotiated its contracts every five years, and by 1995, Murthy said, it 'formed about 25 percent of our revenue'.[49]

'They sequestered the vendors in different rooms at a five-star hotel in the city. They would go from room to room and would negotiate to get better and better terms.' The talks ran over a Thursday and a Friday. 'All other competitors of ours were ready to sign on the dotted line.' Infosys would not cut its price. Murthy said he told GE that 'if we reduced our prices to them we would have to reduce prices to our other customers too', and that lower prices would leave less money for pay, tools, technology and productivity.[50] Infosys withdrew and handed the work over to competitors, and, he recalled, 'my colleagues were unhappy with me'.[51]

The company's filing tells the same story without naming the client. In 1995 Infosys 'chose to reduce significantly the services provided to its then-largest client rather than to accept the price reductions and increased Company resources sought by such client'.[52] That client's share of revenue fell from 21.5 per cent in the year to March 1996 to 6.7 per cent in the year to March 1997. Total revenue still rose by 48.8 per cent, to $39.6 million, in the year to March 1997.[53]

The episode shows who held the power in a trade sold by time. The buyer set the terms in a hotel, room by room, and played its suppliers against one another. A supplier could refuse only if it had other buyers, and Infosys, on its own account, did. Murthy's further claim, that Infosys long charged more than its Indian rivals, is taken up in Chapter 11.

What was being priced was mostly time. In the year to March 1996, 76 per cent of Infosys's revenue came from time-and-materials contracts, under which the client pays for hours or months worked, and 24 per cent from fixed-price contracts. In the year to March 1997 the split was 63 per cent and 37 per cent.[54] In its 1999 filing the company said it was 'seeking to increase the proportion of projects that are undertaken on a fixed-price, fixed-time frame rather than a time-and-materials basis'. Its reason was that such projects offered 'the Company the potential benefit of enhanced margins for projects that are performed efficiently'.[55] Under time-and-materials, a firm that finished faster billed less. Under a fixed price, it kept the saving. The filing added that 'The Company's policy is to decline or discontinue projects that do not offer the potential to meet the Company's profit margin targets.'[56] Selling time also meant that revenue could grow only as fast as the number of engineers billed, which is why the recruiting machine had to keep running. That is the bargain the rest of this book follows. The question of who keeps the gains when work is done faster, the client or the supplier, returns at the end of the book, when the tools doing the work faster are machines.

## Onsite, offshore and the visa

The reforms did not bring the work home quickly. On Nasscom data reported by Khanna and Morales, 'The fraction of on-site work went from 90% in 1988 to 66% in 1995 and 56% in 2000', measured as a share of software revenue.[57] Richard Heeks, a researcher at the University of Manchester who studied the trade through the 1990s, found that 'by 1997/98, it was still true that more than half of all software services export earnings came from onsite work'.[58] Other series, which count exports or development effort rather than revenue, differ by a few points but agree on the direction and the pace.[59]

Two forces pushed it towards India. One was the satellite links. The other was American visa policy, whose shift of 1993 Chapter 13 describes. The H-1B visa, created in 1990 for workers in 'specialty occupations', required the employer to pay the greater of the wage paid to its other employees in the job or the prevailing wage for the occupation. Its cap was set at 65,000 a year. 'In the early years, the cap was never reached', but by the mid-1990s it 'started binding'.[60] Heeks dates the main change to 'the arrival of the Clinton administration, partly in response to the desire of local labour unions to halt or reduce the use of foreign workers in the US'. Indian workers 'were channelled into taking temporary work visas (rather than student or training or even tourist visas as in the past)', and the changes 'pulled a number of Indian companies out of their complacency about onsite work'.[61]

The leading firms built a method around the shift. Rajaraman calls it the Global Delivery Model and, citing Murthy, says Indian software companies invented it. In his description, the part of a project that needed face-to-face work with the customer, typically 20 to 30 per cent of the time, was done at the client's premises. Coding and testing, 70 to 80 per cent of the time, was done in India, in secure areas set aside for a single client and joined to it by a dedicated satellite link.[62] The time difference became an asset: India's working day fell in the American night, so bugs reported at the end of an American shift could be fixed by morning.[63] Murthy said in 2020 that he 'formalized' the model in the late 1980s and early 1990s, aiming at 'only about 20 percent of our effort at customer site'.[64]

Infosys's 1999 filing described the mechanics as the firm practised them: a small team at the client's site and a much larger one in India, with the client's systems linked by satellite to as many as four Indian centres. 'By pursuing this model, the Company completes approximately 80% of its project work at its offshore software development facilities in India.'[65] At any time about 15 per cent of its professionals were working at client sites abroad. In December 1998, 231 of its people in the United States held H-1B visas and 111 held L-1 visas, which were open only to employees who had worked for the company for at least a year.[66]

That 80 per cent was the leading firm's account of its own work at the end of the decade. The industry as a whole still earned more than half its export revenue onsite.[67] The model described where the best-organised firms were going, not where the trade already was.

To persuade distant clients that people they never saw would do the work properly, the leading firms also collected certificates of process, and by 1999 Infosys described itself as 'Certified under ISO 9001, TickIT and at Level 4 of the Capability Maturity Model', a five-level grading of software development; Chapter 11 follows how such certificates were sold.[68]

## Not only the reforms

Two series of export figures cover the decade, one compiled by Subhash Bhatnagar and one by Heeks from his own research and the trade magazine *Dataquest*. They differ by up to about a tenth in some years, but both show growth of between about a quarter and two-thirds a year, faster from 1993 than in 1990-93; the data appendix sets them side by side.[69]

Not all of the growth came from policy. Rajaraman lists the decade's 'fortuitous circumstances' alongside policy: the millennium bug, the conversion of European banking software to the euro, and 'a technically savvy influential Indian diaspora in the USA'.[70] The millennium bug, or Y2K, already brought Infosys 23.3 per cent of its revenue in the year to March 1998.[71]

On the diaspora he writes that 'The "brain drain" of the 60s and the 70s became "opportunity gain" in the 90s'. Indians in senior posts at American companies gave Indian firms 'small contracts at the beginning which grew as the companies delivered good quality software applications'.[72] He offers no data for the claim.

Foreign firms came too, beginning with a Tata joint venture with IBM, which had left India in 1978, that Rajaraman dates to 1992.[73] On his dating, American Express, Oracle and GE Capital International Services followed between 1994 and 1997, and in Bangalore engineers at Texas Instruments designed a signal-processing chip, Ankur, announced in 1997 and 'entirely designed in India'.[74] Chapter 15 follows these foreign-owned centres.

The boom was concentrated. Heeks estimated that by 1998 there were 400 to 500 active software export firms, most of them small firms with a single contract. The top five firms earned more than 40 per cent of all exports and the top twenty about 70 per cent.[75] It was concentrated in place as well. Of 558 software companies whose headquarters *Dataquest* recorded in 1998, 152 were in Bangalore, 122 in Bombay, 93 in Madras and 86 in Delhi.[76] By the close of the period, Rajaraman writes, 'the voice of NASSCOM was strong and it got the ear of the government'.[77]

## What 1991 changed

There are three ways to read the years after the crisis, and the evidence supports parts of each.

The first is the founders' reading. Murthy credits the reforms with the industry's take-off. The export series gives him only partial support: growth was already fast in the late 1980s, slowed in the first years of the decade, and quickened again from 1993.[78] The reforms that mattered most to software were the ones Murthy named, about money and movement: devaluation, convertibility, the end of licensing for imports, and the freedom to price a share issue.

The second is a reading of continuity. The leading firms, the business of sending programmers to clients' sites, the duty-free import regime for exporters and the Software Technology Parks all predated July 1991. The budget's own contribution to software was a tax concession, and it justified that concession as a way to earn foreign exchange.[79] The onsite series and the Infosys contract split show that where the work was done, and how it was sold, changed only slowly. Heeks's verdict in 1998 was that much of the growth 'may have come from its industry's dogged following of the low-risk, low-barrier first path', the export of labour.[80]

The third reading looks to America. Khanna and Morales argue, using an economic model, that the prospect of American jobs through the H-1B visa drew Indian students into engineering, and that those who could not get visas because of the cap stayed home and staffed the Indian industry.[81] On this reading, American demand and American immigration law shaped the Indian profession at least as much as Indian policy did. It is an argument from a model, calibrated on data.

What none of the readings disputes is the change in who did the work. In 1991 a firm of 400 people advertised for sixty-four engineers of any discipline, promised to make them programmers, and paid them about $2,000 a year. By 1998 the same firm was testing more than twenty thousand applicants a year and hiring fewer than one applicant in fifty. Its recruits were trained in a company faculty, worked to documented processes, and spent most of their time in India while their clients slept. The advertisement of 1991 had told them where they would start. 'Say cheers to Bangalore', it said.[82] The next chapter follows them there.
