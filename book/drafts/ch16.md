# Chapter 16. Fraud and foundation (2008-2018)

On 7 January 2009 the board of Satyam Computer Services, a software company with its headquarters in Hyderabad, received a letter from its chairman and founder, B. Ramalinga Raju. It began: 'It is with deep regret, and tremendous burden that I am carrying on my conscience, that I would like to bring the following facts to your notice.' The first fact was that the company's balance sheet at 30 September 2008 carried 'Inflated (non-existent) cash and bank balances of Rs.5,040 crore (as against Rs. 5361 crore reflected in the books).'[1] Rs 5,040 crore is Rs 50.4 billion, or about $1.16 billion at the 2008 average exchange rate. On Raju's own account, nearly all the cash the company said it held did not exist.[2]

Copies went to the chairman of the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), the market regulator, and to the stock exchanges. Satyam filed the letter the same day with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), because its shares also traded in New York.[3] By the end of that day, according to the order of the Company Law Board two days later, the share price had fallen from Rs 188 to Rs 38.40.[4]

Satyam was not a marginal firm. Its own filings called it the fourth-largest Indian IT services company by export revenue.[5] Its engineers were recruited, trained and deployed in much the same way as those of Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), as the next section shows. The fraud therefore put a question to the whole industry this book describes. If the books of one of its four largest firms could be false for years, what exactly had clients, investors and graduates been buying? The answer that emerged over the next three months was that the work was real and the accounts were not.

The same month, in an unrelated decision, the government of India created an agency to give every resident a unique identity number. Within six months it had hired an Infosys co-founder to run it.[6] Satyam showed what happened when a software company's governance failed. Aadhaar, the identity system, showed what happened when the state borrowed the industry's people and methods for a project of its own, and when the Supreme Court then had to decide what that system could be made to do to Indian citizens. This chapter follows both stories from 2008 to 2018.

## The company on paper

The last annual report Satyam filed before the letter was its Form 20-F for the year to March 2008, lodged with the SEC that summer. It described a business growing very fast. 'As of March 31, 2008, we had 50,570 employees, whom we refer to as associates, worldwide as compared to 39,018 associates as of March 31, 2007.'[7] Revenue for the year was reported as $2,138.1 million, and the company said its revenue had grown at a compound rate of 39.1 per cent a year over three years.[8] It had 654 active customers, 'including 185 Fortune Global 500 or Fortune U.S. 500 companies'.[9]

The 20-F is also a record of how such a company made engineers. 'We recruit graduates from the engineering departments of India's leading universities, engineering and technical colleges and management institutes,' it said. It counted 'over 1,500 such institutions' in India, and described a selection process 'involving a series of tests and interviews'.[10] Each new technical recruit, the filing said, went through 'an intensive 12 week initial training program' and then at least 40 hours of training a year.[11] Satyam ran its own Satyam Learning Center with 'qualified full-time faculty', and a School of Leadership whose trainers, it said, included professors from the Harvard Business School.[12] Managers from fields other than software were given 'extensive training, usually over a six-month period, in software engineering and project management skills'.[13] The company reported that 47,405 of its associates were technical staff in IT services, and that 9,941 of them were working onsite, at clients' premises abroad.[14] Attrition in IT services, the share of staff who left in a year, had been 13.1 per cent in the year to March 2008. 'None of our associates are represented by a union,' the filing said.[15]

That was the profession Satyam offered: a campus test, three months of company schooling, a project, perhaps a posting abroad. Nothing in it was unusual for the industry in 2008. Chapter 12 described the same funnel at Infosys on a larger scale.

The financial pages were where the fiction lay, though no outside reader could see it. The 20-F reported 'cash and cash equivalents of $290.5 million' at 31 March 2008. It explained that cash used in investing in the year to March 2007 had risen 'primarily due to investment in bank deposits amounting to $745.6 million'.[16] Raju's letter, written five months after the filing, said that cash and bank balances of Rs 5,361 crore in the books included Rs 5,040 crore that did not exist.[17] A company growing at nearly 40 per cent a year, adding more than 11,000 people to its payroll in a year did not look strange from outside. That was the difficulty.

## Riding a tiger

The letter, as filed with the SEC, runs to about two pages. Press reproductions differ from it in small ways, so this chapter quotes the SEC copy.[18] After the cash, Raju listed 'An accrued interest of Rs. 376 crore which is non-existent', 'An understated liability of Rs. 1,230 crore on account of funds arranged by me', and debtors (money owed by customers) overstated by Rs 490 crore.[19] For the quarter to September 2008, he wrote, the company had reported revenue of Rs 2,700 crore and an operating margin of Rs 649 crore, or 24 per cent of revenue. The actual figures were revenue of Rs 2,112 crore and an operating margin of Rs 61 crore, 3 per cent of revenue. In that quarter alone, he said, the false cash and bank balances had grown by Rs 588 crore.[20]

He explained how it had happened. 'What started as a marginal gap between actual operating profit and the one reflected in the books of accounts continued to grow over the years. It has attained unmanageable proportions as the size of company operations grew significantly.' The company, he wrote, had to carry extra staff and assets to justify the higher level of operations it was reporting, which raised its costs further. Every attempt to close the gap had failed. Because the promoters (the founding family) held only a small share of the equity, he feared that poor results would bring a takeover that would expose the gap.[21] Then he wrote: 'It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten.'[22]

The letter named the event that had brought the crisis into the open. According to the government's petition, as the Company Law Board summarised it, Satyam's board had recently approved a proposal to buy stakes, at huge cost, in two companies controlled by Raju's near relations; the Board itself recorded that four independent directors had resigned once the decision became public.[23] Raju called the 'aborted Maytas acquisition deal' 'the last attempt to fill the fictitious assets with real ones'.[24] A Satyam consultant later spoke of 'the shareholder revolt' that followed it.[25]

Much of the letter is Raju's defence of himself, and it should be read as that. He claimed that neither he nor the managing director, his brother B. Rama Raju, had sold shares in eight years except a small number for charity. He said that Rs 1,230 crore had been 'arranged to Satyam' over two years, outside the books, 'by resorting to pledging all the promoter shares'. He wrote that 'Every attempt was made to keep the wheel moving and to ensure prompt payment of salaries to the associates.' He claimed that 'neither me, nor the Managing Director took even one rupee/dollar from the company', and that none of the board members, past or present, had known. He listed seventeen senior executives who, he said, were also unaware.[26] These are the claims of the man who had confessed. SEBI later held him, his brother and three officials liable for fraud and insider trading and ordered them to give up illegal gains, and an appeals tribunal upheld those findings, as the section on punishment below describes.[27]

He closed with what he had built. 'I have seen it grow from few people to 53,000 people, with 185 Fortune 500 companies as customers and operations in 66 countries.' And: 'I sincerely apologize to all Satyamites and stakeholders'.[28] He recommended that the bank Merrill Lynch 'can be entrusted with the task of quickly exploring some Merger opportunities'.[29]

## Two days in January

Merrill Lynch had already gone. The same SEC filing carried a letter from Kevan Watts, president of DSP Merrill Lynch, to the chairman of SEBI, dated 7 January. It said the bank had ended its advisory engagement with Satyam the day before. 'In the course of such engagement, we came to understand that there were material accounting irregularities, which prompted our aforesaid decision.'[30] An outside adviser had seen enough to walk away a day before the chairman confessed.

Satyam's own press release that day quoted Ram Mynampati, a whole-time director acting as interim chief executive: 'We are obviously shocked by the contents of the letter.' The company said its priorities included protecting 'the careers and security of its approximately 53,000 associates'.[31] SEBI appointed an investigating authority the same day to examine dealings in Satyam's shares, including possible fraud and insider trading.[32]

The state then moved quickly. On 8 January the Registrar of Companies for Andhra Pradesh petitioned the Special Judge for Economic Offences in Hyderabad for permission to search Satyam's offices and seize its books, papers and computer records. The judge's order records that the petition was filed 'at 04.30 PM at my residence'. The Registrar, the order says, apprehended that the company appeared to have shown 'artificial and inflated profits for the last several years'. The judge found a prima facie case and allowed the search.[33]

On 9 January the Union of India petitioned the Company Law Board in New Delhi to remove the directors. The petition, as the Board summarised it, described a listed company with about 300,000 shareholders and more than 53,000 employees, the fourth-largest IT company in India, which had 'received a number of awards for best corporate governance'.[34] The Board noted that the promoter group now held less than 4 per cent of the shares and financial institutions about 60 per cent. Its chairman, S. Balasubramanian, wrote that Raju's written admission established 'beyond any shadow of doubt' that there had been 'financial impropriety and jugglery of financial statements'. 'It appears that a serious fraud has been perpetrated on the society as a whole.'[35] He suspended the board with immediate effect and authorised the central government to appoint up to ten 'persons of eminence' as directors.[36]

Satyam's spokesperson welcomed the 'landmark decision', which the company said would restore 'the confidence of 53,000 employees and their families, 300,000 shareholders and 650 customers across the globe'.[37] Two years later the SEC summarised it more bluntly: 'the India government seized control of the company by dissolving its board of directors and appointing new government-nominated directors'.[38] The Board's order gave its reasons in terms of the employees, customers and shareholders, and of the damage to 'the confidence in the concept of corporate governance practiced in India'.[39]

## The people who did the work

For the engineers, the first question was whether they would be paid. *Business Today*, a business magazine, sent reporters to Satyam's staff in the weeks after the letter and published their accounts in its issue of 8 February 2009.[40] A week after Raju's arrest, the magazine reported, a European client had decided to move a project, with the team working on it, to another Indian vendor. Satyam staff in Europe told the magazine that '95 per cent of the team had agreed to join the competing vendor'.[41]

The rest of the industry did not rush to hire them. T. Sreedhar, managing director of TMI Network, a recruitment firm in Hyderabad, said his office was overrun by Satyam employees. They were, he said, 'stuck between the devil and the deep sea. They cannot quit the job because there is no option being made available by the large companies (who have chosen not to hire them)'. If they stayed, he added, they could not be sure of being paid that month.[42] The magazine reported that Nasscom (the National Association of Software and Service Companies), the industry's lobby group, and Infosys had issued statements against recruiting Satyam's people, 'not because they are tainted but to ensure that the company doesn't suffer because of an exodus'.[43] Rishi Das, chief executive of another recruiter, CareerNet Consulting, estimated that about 35,000 Satyam employees, 70 per cent of the workforce, were already looking for jobs with rivals. That was a recruiter's estimate, not a count.[44]

Some staff were tied in. Insiders told the magazine that about 7,000 employees had been required to give 'Rs 2 lakh bonds' (Rs 200,000) as a deposit under two-year contracts, which they would forfeit if they left.[45] Employees also described a mail from B. Rama Raju dated 24 October 2008 warning of sacking or demotion where 80 per cent of targets were not met and cutting variable pay; the magazine reported the mail from employees' accounts, and it has not been seen.[46] The newest recruits were the most exposed. In 2007, the magazine said, Satyam had issued about 6,000 offer letters on campuses across the country. 'Those offers are not worth the paper they are written on.'[47]

The magazine printed five short first-person accounts, each 'as told to' one of its reporters. N. Kavitha, 22, had studied engineering at the Muthukumaran Institute of Technology near Mangadu, a suburb of Chennai, and had been selected by Satyam in a campus placement in 2007-08. 'I was told that I would get a joining letter by September 2008. This did not happen. The company HR said that I could definitely expect to join in January 2009.' Now, she said, she had lost hope of getting it. But she would still join if it came. 'In this recession, a fresher needs to grab any opportunity. I am not worried too much about Satyam's reputation in the market; I want the work experience first.'[48] Her position was the one Chapter 11 described at Anna University in 2002, and close to the one Chapter 14 traced in the collapse of campus offers in 2009: a campus offer was a promise, not a job, and the graduate carried the risk when the company's plans changed.

S. Mastanamma, 24, had worked at Satyam's Chennai centre for two years after graduating from Newton's College of Engineering in Guntur, Andhra Pradesh. 'I am very positive about the company's future,' she said. She praised the project-level leadership, the employee portal and the training, and said human resources had opened a round-the-clock helpline for staff. 'I am not worried about lay-offs. The existing clients have to be serviced and the new board at Satyam is saying that people are needed to continue to service the clients.'[49] (She put the number of Fortune 500 clients at 160, not Raju's 185.)[50]

A consultant in Europe, two years with the firm, who was not named, said the client had arranged to move the whole project team to another vendor. 'We, as employees, are very clear we want to save our livelihoods.' As an employer, the consultant said, Satyam had paid well. 'Raju was always elusive, I have never seen or heard from him in the two years I have been with the firm', except for one mail defending the Maytas deal.[51] A retail consultant five months into the job, also unnamed, said: 'There is a bit of uncertainty in every job.'[52] Kulwinder Singh, 36, a marketing head based in Hyderabad, was not rank and file, but his account touched the question Raju's letter had raised about salaries. 'I cannot remember a single month in my last three-and-half years of service when the salary has been delayed or missed.'[53]

In a bracketed note of its own in its issue of 22 February 2009, the magazine reported that employees had started to have their pay credited to their accounts.[54] When the board held a press conference in April, the company said that on 20 March 2009, by its internal data, it had about 42,000 employees on a stand-alone basis (the parent company without its subsidiaries).[55] That is not directly comparable with the 53,000 in Raju's letter, which was a group figure from before the departures began, and the filings do not say how many had left.

## Rs 58 a share

The government's nominees took over a company whose clients were still being served and whose cash was gone. The new directors included Kiran Karnik, who became chairman, and Deepak Parekh.[56] With Goldman Sachs and Avendus Capital as financial advisers, the board ran an auction under the supervision of Justice S.P. Bharucha. Bidders were judged first on technical criteria, including their record in corporate governance and in managing distressed companies, and then on price.[57] On 13 April 2009 the financial bids were opened. They were 'Rs. 58 (Tech Mahindra), Rs. 45.90 (Larsen & Toubro Limited) and Rs. 20 (consortium of WL Ross funds) per share'.[58] Parekh and another director, S.B. Mainak, abstained because of possible conflicts of interest with other bidders.[59]

Venturbay Consultants, a company controlled by Tech Mahindra, agreed to buy 31 per cent of the enlarged share capital at Rs 58 a share, putting Rs 1,756 crore into Satyam, which the company converted at about $351 million at a rate of Rs 50 to the dollar. It would then have to offer to buy at least a further 20 per cent from other shareholders.[60] Karnik said the result 'signals a new stage for the Company in its progress towards stabilization and growth'. He hoped it would reassure customers, 'who continue to be happy with Satyam's excellent service delivery'.[61] That was a claim by the chairman of a company being sold, but the bids themselves were evidence of a kind. The process, the company noted, 'has reached this significant stage within three months of the new Board's first meeting'.[62] By October 2009 the company's correspondence with the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI), India's federal police agency, went out under the name Mahindra Satyam.[63]

The speed of the sale says something about what an Indian services firm was. Its accounts had been falsified for years and its founder had been arrested. Yet within three months two large Indian companies offered real money for control of it. What they were buying was the people, the client relationships and the delivery machinery. The board also told the press that Satyam held about 450 acres of land and that two Hyderabad campuses had been valued at an average of Rs 1,700 crore.[64] But it was the engineers who kept the clients, and the clients who made the company worth buying. The weakness had been in a different place: a founder-led company whose founders had come to hold a small share of the equity, and the checks that should have caught them.

## The auditors

Satyam's auditors were Indian affiliates of PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC), and the 20-F carried the consent of Price Waterhouse as its independent registered public accounting firm.[65] In April 2011 the SEC set out what it found they had done. Five Indian affiliates, which it called PW India, had 'repeatedly' conducted deficient audits of Satyam and enabled 'a massive accounting fraud to go undetected for several years'.[66] They agreed to settle and pay 'a $6 million penalty, the largest ever by a foreign-based accounting firm in an SEC enforcement action'. They also agreed not to take new American clients for six months, to train their staff, to revise their audit procedures and to appoint an independent monitor.[67] In a parallel case two of the affiliates agreed to pay a further $1.5 million to the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, the American body that regulates auditors of listed companies.[68]

The core finding was simple. When an auditor wants to know whether a company really holds the cash it reports, it writes to the banks and asks them to confirm the balances directly to the auditor. According to the SEC's order, PW India staff 'failed to conduct procedures to confirm Satyam's cash and cash equivalent balances or its accounts receivables'. They 'routinely relinquished control of the delivery and receipt of cash confirmations entirely to their audit clients and rarely, if ever, questioned the integrity of the confirmation responses they received from the client by following up with the banks'.[69] The SEC found that this was not limited to Satyam but was 'indicative of a much larger quality control failure throughout PW India'.[70] Robert Khuzami, the SEC's director of enforcement, said: 'PW India violated its most fundamental duty as a public watchdog'.[71] Cheryl Scarboro, who ran its unit for the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, said: 'PW India failed to conduct even the most fundamental audit procedures.'[72]

These were findings in a settled case, not the verdict of a trial. The SEC also recorded that, after the fraud came to light, PW India had replaced nearly all of its senior management responsible for audit and removed the Satyam audit team from client work. It noted that Indian authorities had filed criminal charges against several former Satyam officials and two lead engagement partners from PW India.[73] The same day, Satyam itself, now under new management, agreed to settle the SEC's fraud charges, pay a $10 million penalty and carry out internal reforms.[74]

## Findings and punishment

The Indian regulators moved more slowly. SEBI passed a final order on 15 July 2014 against Ramalinga Raju, Rama Raju and three officials: V. Srinivas, the former chief financial officer; G. Ramakrishna, a vice-president of finance; and V.S. Prabhakara Gupta, the head of internal audit. It held them liable for fraud under the securities laws and for insider trading, 'in the context of their role in the falsified financial statements' of the company, and barred them from the securities market for fourteen years.[75]

They appealed. On 12 May 2017 the Securities Appellate Tribunal upheld SEBI's findings on the merits but sent back the question of how much each should repay and for how long each should be barred.[76] The tribunal's own summary of SEBI's investigation is the clearest statement in the record of how the fraud worked, and it is a finding, not an allegation. 'Investigation carried out by SEBI revealed that Ramalinga Raju as Chairman and Rama Raju as, MD of Satyam were instrumental in creating fictitious invoices, fictitious receipts etc. on the basis of which fictitious monthly bank statements were prepared'. The three officials, it found, had allowed the books to be prepared from those documents 'inspite of noticing' them.[77]

In October 2018 SEBI issued a fresh order on the three officials. It recorded that the tribunal had relied on the admissions of Srinivas and Ramakrishna and on statements by others, and had traced their knowledge of the fraud back to about 2001. Among the matters discussed in their meetings with the Raju brothers, it listed 'the need to show inflated results to attract customers/employees'.[78] That phrase connects the fraud to the labour market of the industry. In a business that sold the hours of engineers, reported growth was, on this account, part of what attracted clients and recruits.

The officials' own submissions, which SEBI summarised without accepting, gave their side. Srinivas said he had spent more than two years in jail. He said there had been two parts to the fraud, bogus invoices and inflated bank balances, and that he had known nothing of the deposits. In a company run by its promoters, he said, it had been hard to oppose or question their decisions.[79] SEBI found Gupta's role less serious. As head of internal audit he had raised three instances of unreconciled invoices but, the order says, 'had to abide by the instructions of Rama Raju, the Managing Director, to close the same'.[80] SEBI barred Srinivas and Ramakrishna for seven years and Gupta for four, counting time already served since 2014. It ordered them to repay about Rs 15.7 crore, Rs 11.5 crore and Rs 0.48 crore respectively, with simple interest at 12 per cent a year from 7 January 2009. Because appeals were pending, the order was to take effect only from a date the Supreme Court would direct.[81]

The 2018 order also refers in passing to an order of the CBI Special Court of 9 April 2015.[82] According to a *Business Today* report that day, the court found Raju, his brother and eight others guilty and sentenced all ten to seven years' rigorous imprisonment, and they chose to appeal. The judgment itself has not been read for this book, and the book does not follow the appeals.[83]

Satyam is often called India's first great corporate-governance failure. That claim has not been tested here against earlier scandals in the Indian markets, and it is not made. What the record supports is narrower. On Raju's own figures it was a fraud of more than Rs 5,000 crore at what its filings called the fourth-largest Indian IT services company. It led to what the SEC called the largest penalty ever paid by a foreign-based accounting firm in an SEC enforcement action.[84] And within two days of the letter it led to an order suspending the board of a private, listed company and authorising the government to appoint its own directors.[85]

## A number for every resident

On 28 January 2009, three weeks after Raju's letter, the Planning Commission issued a notification creating the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI).[86] The project had a longer history, which the Supreme Court later set out from government records. In March 2006 the Department of Information Technology had approved a project called 'Unique Identification for BPL Families', for households below the poverty line, to be carried out by the National Informatics Centre, the government's own computing agency.[87] An Empowered Group of Ministers was set up on 4 December 2006 to bring that project together with the National Population Register. Through 2007 a committee worked out the details. At its meeting on 15 June 2007, the judgment records, 'The numbering format of 11 digits was approved', and the committee agreed that the authority should be created by an executive order under the Planning Commission.[88] The notification of January 2009 made it an attached office of the Commission, and the Finance Minister approved Rs 147.31 crore for the first phase of enrolment.[89]

Then the state hired from the industry. By a notification of 2 July 2009, 'Mr. Nandan Nilekani was appointed as the Chairman of UIDAI for an initial tenure of five years in the rank and status of a Cabinet Minister. He assumed charge on July 24, 2009.'[90] Nilekani was a co-founder of Infosys and had been a director since 1981; from 1981 to 1987 he had managed its marketing and development in the United States, the years of the body-shopping trade described in Chapter 6.[91] A Prime Minister's Council on UIDAI was set up on 30 July 2009. At its first meeting, on 12 August, the chairman presented the strategy for the project, including a proposal for a law to give it legal standing. A Cabinet Committee headed by the Prime Minister followed in October.[92]

The name came to carry its own argument. When the Supreme Court decided the case in 2018, Justice A.K. Sikri began the majority judgment with a line from the project's own message: 'It is better to be unique than the best.' Aadhaar, he wrote, 'means, in English, "foundation" or "base"'.[93]

## Building it

What UIDAI set out to collect is recorded in Justice D.Y. Chandrachud's opinion in the same case, drawing on the agency's strategy paper. Besides name, date of birth, gender, address and the names of parents or spouse, every resident would give 'All ten fingerprints, photograph and both iris scans'.[94] A Biometrics Standards Committee reported in December 2009. For its work, the opinion says, more than 250,000 fingerprint images had been gathered from 25,000 people in districts of Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Orissa (Odisha since 2011), nearly all in rural areas, using different devices and different agencies. Chandrachud observed that the report did not say on what basis those fingerprints had been collected, and that this raised concerns about privacy.[95]

The scale grew quickly. The National Identification Authority of India Bill, meant to give the agency a statutory basis, was introduced in the Rajya Sabha, the upper house of Parliament, on 3 December 2010. On 13 February 2011 the one millionth Aadhaar card was delivered.[96] 'On 29 September 2011, the Aadhaar project completed one year. An announcement was made of the generation of ten crore enrolments and of more than 3.75 crore Aadhaar numbers', that is, 100 million enrolments and 37.5 million numbers.[97] For seven years the project rested on the Planning Commission's notification of 2009, which, Chandrachud pointed out, made no reference to biometrics at all.[98] The Aadhaar Act came only in 2016. It was introduced in the Lok Sabha, the lower house, as Bill No. 47 of 2016 and passed as a Money Bill, a category of legislation tied to taxation and public spending under Article 110 of the Constitution, in which the Rajya Sabha's part is limited. The petitioners argued that it was not a Money Bill at all.[99] By the time the Act was notified, according to the Press Information Bureau as cited by Chandrachud, UIDAI had generated about 100 crore (one billion) Aadhaar numbers.[100]

By then Aadhaar held records of about a billion people, and the agency's first chairman was a man who had spent his career building an export software company. The sources in hand for this chapter do not say who else came from the private industry to UIDAI, what the engineers who built the system were paid, or how the enrolment operators who took fingerprints in villages were trained and employed. Nilekani's own account, written with Viral Shah, has not been read for this book.[101] What the record does show is the shape of the transfer. The industry had learned to run very large projects to specification for foreign clients. In 2009 the state asked one of its founders to run a very large project for Indian residents.

## The right to privacy

The challenge came early. In 2012, while Aadhaar still rested only on an executive notification, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy, a retired judge, and Pravesh Khanna filed Writ Petition (Civil) No. 494 of 2012 in the Supreme Court. They argued that the scheme violated the right to privacy of 'innumerable citizens' under Article 21 of the Constitution, which protects life and personal liberty.[102] Two older decisions of the Court, *M P Sharma* and *Kharak Singh*, had been read as holding that the Constitution did not protect a right to privacy, and the question was referred to a bench of nine judges.[103]

On 24 August 2017 the nine judges answered it unanimously. Chandrachud wrote the lead judgment for Chief Justice J.S. Khehar, Justice R.K. Agrawal, Justice S. Abdul Nazeer and himself; Justices J. Chelameswar, S.A. Bobde, Abhay Manohar Sapre, Rohinton Fali Nariman and Sanjay Kishan Kaul each wrote separately.[104] The order of the Court, signed by all nine, overruled *M P Sharma* and, to the same extent, *Kharak Singh*, and declared: 'The right to privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and as a part of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution.'[105]

The judgment did not decide whether Aadhaar was lawful. It set the test that Aadhaar, and any later system that collected and processed Indians' data, would have to pass.

## Twenty-sixth of September, 2018

The Aadhaar case itself was decided on 26 September 2018 by five judges. Sikri wrote for Chief Justice Dipak Misra, Justice A.M. Khanwilkar and himself. Justice Ashok Bhushan wrote a separate concurring judgment. Chandrachud dissented.[106]

During the hearings the agency had explained its own engineering to the judges. Dr Ajay Bhushan Pandey, UIDAI's chief executive, gave a PowerPoint presentation in court.[107] The Authority claimed that within a few seconds of collection, biometric data were sent in encrypted form to its central repository and passed 'out of the reach of the enrolling agency'; that authentication was 'purpose blind', returning only a yes or a no to the agency asking; and that the biometric system 'provides high accuracy of over 99.86%'.[108] In answer to written questions from the petitioners' lawyers, UIDAI gave national figures. Of about 616 million Aadhaar numbers that had been used for fingerprint authentication, about 37 million, or 6.00 per cent, were recorded as failed; for iris authentication the figure was 8.54 per cent. It added that 'authentication failures do not mean exclusion or denial from subsidies, benefits or services', because agencies were obliged by law to provide other ways of establishing identity.[109] These were the claims of the system's builder, made in its own defence.

The majority upheld the Act and its central purpose, the use of Aadhaar to deliver subsidies and benefits paid for from the Consolidated Fund of India. It held that the Act had been validly passed as a Money Bill.[110]

The judgment's first limits concerned children and education. Children could be enrolled only with their parents' consent, and could opt out at adulthood. School admission could not require Aadhaar, because education for children aged six to fourteen was a fundamental right and not a 'benefit'.[111] And the examination and education bodies could not demand it either: 'CBSE, NEET, JEE, UGC etc. cannot make the requirement of Aadhaar mandatory as they are outside the purview of Section 7 and are not backed by any law.'[112] JEE is the Joint Entrance Examination for the engineering colleges, the gate through which much of the profession described in this book passed. The Court had drawn a line between the identity system and the machinery of selection for engineering school.

Beyond education, it cut the system back in ways that mattered to anyone who would write software against it. It struck down the regulation that allowed authentication records to be archived for five years: 'Retention of data beyond the period of six months is impermissible.'[113] It struck down section 33(2), which allowed disclosure of information in the interest of national security on the order of a joint secretary, leaving Parliament free to enact a provision with a judicial officer involved.[114]

It also struck down the part of section 57 that had allowed 'any body corporate or person' to use Aadhaar to establish identity under 'any contract'. A contract was not a law, the judges reasoned. The effect of the provision, they wrote, would be 'to enable commercial exploitation of an individual biometric and demographic information by the private entities'.[115] The rule requiring every bank account to be linked to Aadhaar 'does not meet the test of proportionality and is also violative of right to privacy of a person which extends to banking details'.[116] The telecommunications department's circular of 23 March 2017 requiring mobile numbers to be linked to Aadhaar was 'held to be illegal and unconstitutional as it is not backed by any law and is hereby quashed'.[117]

Chandrachud would have struck the Act down. He held that a bill containing provisions outside the subjects listed in Article 110 'is not a Money Bill', and that the programme violated 'essential norms pertaining to informational privacy, self-determination and data protection'.[118] Much of his opinion is about people the system failed. He collected reports of authentication failures in which, he wrote, eligible people were denied rations, pensions and other benefits for no fault of their own. 'For persons, who are engaged in manual labour, and persons who are disabled or aged, fingerprints actually cannot be captured by biometric devices.'[119] 'Should the scholarship of a girl child or a mid- day meal for the young be made to depend on the uncertainties of biometric matches?' He concluded: 'Dignity and rights of individuals cannot be based on algorithms or probabilities. Constitutional guarantees cannot be subject to the vicissitudes of technology.'[120]

The majority took a different view of the same evidence. It recorded the government's statement on affidavit that no eligible person would be deprived of a benefit because a fingerprint or iris failed to match, and that the project, being ongoing, might have 'some glitches' that would be corrected until 'it becomes foolproof over a period of time'. It treated such arguments as matters of the system's working rather than its constitutional validity.[121] It directed that no child be denied a benefit for lack of an Aadhaar number if identity could be shown by other documents.[122] Both sides accepted that the system made mistakes. They differed over whether a constitutional system could rest on a machine that was right most of the time.

## Two lessons

The two halves of this chapter look unconnected: a private fraud in Hyderabad, a public database in New Delhi. They are connected by what the Indian software industry had become by 2009. It was large enough that its largest failure needed the state to rescue it, and capable enough that the state turned to it for a project of national scale.

Satyam showed where the industry's value lay. The books were false, but the work was not; clients stayed, a rival bid Rs 58 a share within three months, and engineers like Mastanamma expected to go on servicing the accounts.[123] What failed was oversight: promoters who controlled a company in which they held little equity, an internal auditor who, SEBI found, had to close his findings on the managing director's instructions, and outside auditors who let the client handle the bank confirmations.[124] Raju's letter claimed that every attempt had been made to pay salaries promptly. SEBI's 2018 summary of the tribunal's findings listed 'the need to show inflated results to attract customers/employees' among the matters the officials had discussed with the Raju brothers. That suggests at least one thing the fiction was for.[125]

Aadhaar showed something different. For thirty years Indian programmers had built systems for foreign companies, under foreign contracts and foreign law. Here was a system built in India, by Indians, for the Indian state, applied to every Indian. The Supreme Court's judgments of 2017 and 2018 were the first time Indian institutions had to decide, in detail, what Indian software could be made to do to Indian citizens: how long it could keep their data, which companies could use it, and whether a failed fingerprint match could cost someone their rations.

The two stories also touched this book's other subject, the making of engineers. Satyam recruited from India's colleges, which its filing counted at 'over 1,500', and put each new technical recruit through twelve weeks of its own training.[126] The Court, ruling on the state's own system, kept Aadhaar out of the JEE, the examination through which students entered engineering college.[127] The firms trained what the colleges supplied, and the Court left the gate to those colleges outside the identity system.

The question the next chapter takes up follows from both. An industry that could run a client's systems so reliably that a fraud at the top did not stop them, and that could lend a founder to build a national identity platform, had still produced very few software products of its own. Aadhaar was built on the state's account. Chapter 17 asks why Indian firms so rarely built on their own.
