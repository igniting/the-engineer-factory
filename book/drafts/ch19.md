# Chapter 19. The machines learn to code (2022-2026)

On Wednesday 6 August 2025 two managers from Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) sat down at the Labour Office in Bengaluru (Bangalore until 2014) across from three officials of a trade union. TCS sent Boban Varghese Thomas, a general manager in human resources, and Mahesh G.K., an assistant manager. The Karnataka State IT/ITeS Employees Union (KITU) sent its general secretary Suhas Adiga, its president V.J.K. Nair and its secretary Sooraj Nidiyanga. Presiding as conciliation officer was G. Manjunath, Additional Labour Commissioner of Karnataka.[1] The meeting had been called because KITU had filed an industrial dispute on 29 July, two days after TCS announced internally that it would shrink. The union accused the company of illegal mass retrenchment.[2] The meeting ended without agreement, and the next round was set for 8 September.[3] What happened after that has not been found in any source read for this book.

The setting was ordinary for a labour dispute in India and unusual for this industry. Chapter 18 followed the first visible unions of software workers, KITU and the Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), through the pandemic boom and its end. Now they were in a government office, contesting a cut announced by the country's largest software company. The question in the room was narrow. KITU said TCS was pushing people to resign so as to avoid the government approval that the Industrial Disputes Act requires before a firm with more than 100 employees retrenches workers.[4] The question outside the room was much larger. TCS had described its restructuring as part of a plan to 'deploy artificial intelligence at scale'.[5] Its chief executive said the cut was not caused by AI.[6] Much of the commentary assumed that it was.

This chapter covers the years in which machines learned to write usable code, from 2022, when the pandemic hiring boom ended, to the autumn of 2026, when this book was being finished. For forty years the Indian software industry had sold the time of people who write code. Chapter 6 showed how the firms learned to price that time by the hour and the head. Chapter 12 showed how the colleges grew to supply the heads. When code became cheap to produce, both halves of the arrangement were exposed at once. The evidence for what happened is recent everywhere, thin in places and contested on the point that matters most, which is cause. Most of it postdates the training of the AI model that helped to research this book. It rests on documents read in October 2026, not on that model's memory. The chapter tries to say what the documents show and to stop where they stop.

## One sentence, three years

The most careful record of how the Indian service companies understood AI is not in their speeches. It is in their annual filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, where a misleading statement carries legal risk. Infosys files an annual report on Form 20-F. One risk factor in that report, about wages and productivity, changed in small ways between 2024 and 2026, and the changes trace the arrival of the problem.

In the report for the year to March 2024, filed in June 2024, Infosys wrote: 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements.'[7] In the report for the following year, filed on 1 July 2025, one phrase changed: clients 'have started demanding higher commitments around productivity improvements'.[8] In the report for the year to March 2026, filed on 15 June 2026, the cause was widened. The sentence now began 'Due to the attention created by evolving AI technologies', and the clients were still demanding.[9]

The sentences on either side explain what the demand meant. Before it, in both later reports, Infosys said that 'due to competitive market conditions and pricing pressures, we are committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts with our clients'. After it, the company warned that failing to deliver those improvements 'may impact our profitability'.[10] Read together, the three sentences describe a mechanism. Clients hear that machines can write code. They ask for contracts that assume fewer hours of human work. The vendor promises the savings in advance and carries the loss if they do not arrive. By 2026 the business press had a name for this. Infosys had set it out for its investors two years earlier.

The 2026 report added a passage that had not appeared before. In its review of operations, Infosys described what its clients were doing: 'large technology platforms continue to expand their service layers, while clients themselves are selectively insourcing capabilities as AI improves productivity and reduces dependency on external labor-based models'.[11] An 'external labor-based model' is a fair description of what Infosys had sold since its founding in 1981. Here the company named, in a regulatory filing, the threat this chapter is about.

Wipro's filings moved the same way. Its report for the year to March 2026 added a risk factor: 'AI-driven automation, efficiency gains and increased client self-service may reduce demand for certain services we provide and may adversely affect pricing, margins, service levels or the mix of services delivered.'[12] In its outlook for the industry, Wipro listed 'outcome-based pricing' among the strategies firms were likely to adopt. Outcome-based pricing means charging for a result delivered rather than for the hours spent on it. The same page predicted that 'Hiring will shift from volume to skills, emphasizing AI fluency, domain expertise, and productivity-based workforce models'.[13] The review of operations warned that 'elevated customer expectations on productivity due to AI advancements can potentially put pressure on our current revenue base'.[14]

Cognizant, which is registered in the United States but employs most of its staff in India (Chapter 15), said much the same in its annual report on Form 10-K for 2025, filed on 12 February 2026. 'As AI-based technologies or other forms of automation evolve,' it wrote, 'demand for some services that we currently perform for our clients may be reduced.'[15] A second risk factor admitted that the company might fail to recoup its spending on AI if clients did 'not accept new pricing models that reflect the value of our AI-enabled solutions'.[16]

All three companies also told investors that AI would help them, by creating new kinds of work, and the filings make that case at length. Risk factors are written for lawyers rather than customers, which is why they are useful here. They show that by 2026 three of the largest employers of Indian software engineers had told an American regulator that the machines threatened the volume of work they sold.

## The tools on the desk

The same filings record which tools the engineers were given. Infosys's report for the year to March 2025 said: 'We also utilize leading technology solutions like GitHub Copilot, where we are among the top GSIs in adoption.'[17] A GSI is a global systems integrator, and the ranking is the company's own claim. A year later the list was longer: 'We utilize leading AI solutions like Github Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code and Devin to name a few.'[18] All four are commercial products that use AI models to write or change code. Claude Code is made by Anthropic, which also makes the AI model that assisted with the research and drafting of this book. The reader should weigh this chapter with that in mind. Here the product is treated like the other three on the list, as a tool that a customer reported using. Infosys said it had 'infused AI throughout our software development lifecycle', and it offered its own platforms for what it called 'Agentic software engineering', meaning software agents that carry out programming tasks over several steps.[19]

Wipro gave a count. In the year to March 2025, 'over 20,000 associates' in its delivery teams were 'leveraging AI developer tools, boosting our overall productivity'.[20] A year later the same sentence said 'over 60,000'.[21] Wipro reported 'more than 240,000' employees at 31 March 2026, of whom about 38,000 were outside India, so on the company's own figures about a quarter of its workforce was using such tools.[22] The productivity claim is Wipro's. The filing does not say how it was measured.

Cognizant said it gave staff 'access to leading AI tools, including Microsoft Copilot, GitHub CoPilot, Google Gemini Code Assist and Windsurf'. It also described a training exercise: 'Associates were given access to leading vibe coding tools to sharpen their AI skills, producing more than 30,000 prototypes.'[23] 'Vibe coding' means describing a program in ordinary language and letting a model write it. The phrase thus entered an American annual report as a way of training a workforce of whom about 256,900, out of roughly 351,600 at the end of 2025, were in India.[24]

Training claims ran through every report, and none defined what training meant. Infosys said in 2025 that 'Over 2,75,000 of our people are trained on AI at different levels of proficiency', using Indian digit grouping.[25] In 2026 it said that 84% of its workforce was 'now AI-aware'.[26] Cognizant said it had 'upskilled more than 330,000 associates on GenAI' between July 2023 and the end of 2025.[27] TCS was reported to have trained more than 550,000 employees in basic AI and more than 100,000 in advanced skills.[28] Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, said that 2 million of the industry's 5.95 million direct employees had been 'upskilled in AI'.[29] These are claims by interested parties. They show what the companies wanted investors and clients to believe, which was that their workforces were being remade rather than replaced.

## A stall, not a collapse

The fear in 2025 and 2026 was that the machines would empty the offices. The headcount figures do not show that, at least not yet. They show a stall after the boom and bust that Chapter 18 describes.

Infosys had 317,240 employees at 31 March 2024, 323,578 a year later and 328,594 at 31 March 2026.[30] Its offers of employment, which include experienced hires as well as graduates, recovered from a trough of 26,975 in the year to March 2024 to 83,207 and then 87,286.[31] Applications rose to 4,457,748 in the year to March 2025 and 5,884,480 in the year to March 2026.[32] On the book's calculation that is about 54 applications for each offer in the first year and 67 in the second. More people were competing for each job than before.

Wipro reported 'more than' 232,000, 230,000 and 240,000 employees at 31 March 2024, 2025 and 2026.[33] At Cognizant, voluntary attrition in technology services, the share of staff who chose to leave in a year, fell to 13.9% in 2025 from 15.9% in 2024.[34] In 2022 it had been 31.7%.[35] People were not leaving, because there was nowhere obviously better to go.

TCS was the exception. Avik Das of *Business Standard*, compiling the companies' results in April 2026, reported that the five largest firms' combined headcount fell by 6,981 in the year to March 2026, after rising by 12,718 the year before. TCS's net headcount fell by 23,460 and Tech Mahindra's by 1,108, while Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech added people.[36] These are the companies' reported figures as compiled by the newspaper. TCS's own fact sheets could not be read for this book.

Nasscom's industry totals tell the same story at a larger scale. In February 2025 it estimated that the industry had added 126,000 people in the year to March 2025, taking its workforce to 5.8 million.[37] A year later it reported 135,000 net additions in the year to March 2026, taking direct employees to 5.95 million.[38] Revenue was estimated at $315 billion, up 6.1%.[39] Its president, Rajesh Nambiar, spoke of what the Press Trust of India paraphrased as 'a non-linearity' between job growth of 2.3% and revenue growth of 6.1%.[40] For thirty years the industry's revenue and its headcount had risen together, because revenue was, roughly, heads multiplied by a billing rate. A gap opening between the two lines is the change this chapter is about. Nasscom's figures are an industry body's estimates and are revised. Its estimate of revenue for the year to March 2025 was raised a year later from $282.6 billion to $297 billion, after Nasscom changed how it counted the revenue of foreign-owned centres.[41]

Those foreign-owned centres were where much of the growth now lay. Global capability centres (GCCs), the Indian offices that multinational companies own and staff themselves, had a long history, as Chapter 15 showed. In September 2024 Nasscom and the consultancy Zinnov counted about 1,700 companies with such centres, employing 1.9 million people, and projected revenue of $99-105 billion by 2030, up from $64.6 billion in the year to March 2024.[42] By May 2026 their next report put the total at 2,117 centres, 2.36 million employees and about $98.4 billion in revenue for the year to March 2026, close to the 2030 projection four years early.[43] These figures come from an industry body and from a consultancy that sells its services to GCCs, and they are not measured on the same basis as Nasscom's totals for the whole industry, so the two should not be added or subtracted. What they suggest is a shift in who employed India's engineers. The client was moving its work in house, and the house was often in Bengaluru or Hyderabad. That was the 'selectively insourcing' of which Infosys had warned, seen from the other side.

## July 2025

On Sunday 27 July 2025 TCS said it would reduce its workforce by 2% during the financial year, mainly at the middle and senior levels. Reuters put the cut at roughly 12,200 jobs out of more than 613,000.[44] The company said the move was part of a strategy to invest in new technologies, enter new markets, 'deploy artificial intelligence at scale, and realign its workforce model'.[45] TCS's own statement could not be read for this book, because the company's website was blocked to the research tools used.

The chief executive, K. Krithivasan, explained the decision to the business website Moneycontrol. Reuters quoted him: 'This is not because of AI giving some 20% productivity gains. We are not doing that. This is driven by where there is a skill mismatch, or, where we think that we have not been able to deploy someone.'[46] *Outlook Business*, relaying the same interview, reported him saying that 'Some people, especially at senior levels, find it difficult to transition to tech-heavy roles', and that 'This is not a question of demand. It is about us being future-ready.'[47] He described a move away from 'waterfall' project management, in which a project passes through fixed stages under layers of managers, towards agile methods that needed fewer such layers.[48]

That was the company's explanation, and it was disputed at once. Shritama Bose and Ujjaini Dutta of Reuters Breakingviews pointed out that TCS's headcount had risen 36% since 2020. Revenue per employee had risen 20% over the same period, but costs per employee had risen by a quarter.[49] Piyush Pandey, an analyst at Centrum Broking, estimated that the cut would trim employee costs by up to 4%.[50] Kamal Karanth of the staffing firm Xpheno told Breakingviews that the industry's old pyramid, with many low-cost employees at the bottom, was giving way to a diamond, with a big middle layer.[51] On this reading the cut was about cost after the pandemic over-hiring that Chapter 18 documents.

The unions offered a third reading. The IT & ITES Democratic Employees Association (IIDEA) said: 'What we are witnessing is not transformation but cost-cutting masquerading as future-readiness.'[52] It accused TCS of targeting experienced, better-paid staff.[53] On this view the company was replacing expensive people with cheaper ones.

So there are three explanations: the company's (skills that no longer matched the work, and people who could not be redeployed), the analysts' (cost after over-hiring) and the unions' (cheaper labour). They are not exclusive, and AI could sit behind any of them. A skill mismatch can arise because clients want AI work; costs can matter more when clients demand AI-driven discounts. But no document read for this book shows which roles were cut, or why, in enough detail to decide between them. Whether AI caused the TCS cuts remains, on present evidence, an open question.

## The bench

What the cut felt like is known from reporting in Bengaluru, almost entirely through employees who would not give their names. Anisha Reddy of *South First* reported on 2 August 2025 that more than 100 TCS employees had approached unions in the previous week.[54] KITU's secretary, Lenil Babu, told her: 'So far, we have filed complaints with the Additional Labour Commissioner of Karnataka on behalf of 15 employees who were already let go. But another 100 have approached us after being put on the bench.'[55]

The bench is where an engineer waits, still employed and paid, between projects. In the industry's long boom, time on the bench was a normal part of a career and a cost the firms absorbed. *South First* reported that a new TCS policy, in force from 12 June 2025, allowed an employee at most 35 days a year without a project and required at least 225 billable days.[56] The reporter saw an email sent to one employee: 'Your prolonged un-allocation without any productive assignment is a matter of concern to the company and the company views this matter very seriously.'[57] NITES said in a statement that the policy 'frames lack of allocation as a personal failure of the employee'.[58] TCS told *South First* that the restructuring was being planned with due care so that service to clients would not be affected.[59]

Korah Abraham of *The News Minute* reported on 7 August 2025 how the resignation meetings worked, again from anonymous accounts. A Bengaluru employee in middle management said: 'They offered three months' pay as an incentive to resign. But if someone refuses, they're told they'll be terminated without compensation, and won't receive a relieving letter.'[60] A relieving letter is the document an Indian employer gives a departing employee, and the next employer usually asks to see it. Two employees said they had been asked to switch off their phones and leave them on the table before meeting human resources staff.[61] A woman who had resigned said she would have fought the case if she could have afforded to: 'I come from a financially poor background.'[62] One employee alleged that more than 300 people had been made to resign from a single Bengaluru campus in a week.[63] That is one person's estimate, and the book cannot check it.

The two accounts of how people left are incompatible in tone, and neither can be settled here. TCS described a planned restructuring.[64] The unions and the anonymous employees described pressure to resign under threat.[65] Neither the outcome of the conciliation nor any finding by the Karnataka Labour Department was found. *The News Minute* also reported the union's complaint that some graduates who had been given TCS offer letters, with July 2025 joining dates, had heard nothing further.[66] That was the same pattern of delayed joining that Chapter 18 records at several firms from 2022.[67]

## The price of an hour

By 2026 the industry had a phrase for what Infosys had described in 2024. Payal Ganguly's report for *Forbes India* on 11 May 2026 called it 'AI-led deflation, characterised by a productivity-based pricing structure demanded by customers and the passing on of productivity benefits to enterprises'.[68] The report linked to the magazine's other coverage of Claude Cowork, another Anthropic product, as a cause of worry for Indian IT firms. The same disclosure applies: Anthropic makes the model that assisted with this book.[69]

Ganguly quoted HCLTech's chief executive, C. Vijayakumar, on his company's April 2026 earnings call. 'A $100 million deal would be much less today,' he said, 'maybe $80 million, just on a rough ballpark.' He added that winning the same revenue took 'at least 25-30 percent more effort'.[70] He also spoke of a deflationary effect of 3 to 5% on the company's guidance for the year to March 2027.[71] Krithivasan of TCS was more hopeful on his own call: 'Our expectation is AI will be net accretive.' He expected AI revenue to rise while some traditional revenue fell.[72] Salil Parekh, the chief executive of Infosys, said that his company was signing multi-year deals priced on productivity.[73]

The analysts were blunter. *Forbes India* cited a report by Motilal Oswal Financial Services, dated 4 May 2026, which estimated that 12-15% of the sector's revenue faced 'direct AI-driven displacement risk'. The report said: 'The cost of delivering an outcome has fallen sharply; these savings are currently being extracted almost entirely by enterprise buyers.'[74] A Kotak Securities report expected the industry's revenue growth to stay 'range-bound at 3 percent' because of AI deflation.[75] The broker reports themselves were not read.

The headline numbers fit this account without proving it. According to *Forbes India*, TCS's revenue fell 0.5% in US dollar terms in the year to March 2026, and Wipro's fell 0.32%.[76] At the same time the companies reported growing AI revenue: TCS an annualised figure above $2.3 billion by the end of that year, HCLTech $620 million of what it called 'Advanced AI' revenue for the year, and Infosys $275 million in a single quarter.[77] Nasscom made its first estimate of the industry's AI revenue, at $10-12 billion for the year.[78] Gaurav Vasu of the research firm UnearthInsight told *Business Standard* that AI revenue was still under 5-7% of the top five firms' revenue.[79] Each company defines AI revenue its own way, so these figures cannot be added up or compared.

This is where the chapter meets the argument of the whole book. Chapter 6 showed how the Indian firms learned to sell engineers' time by the hour, often at the client's site. Chapter 11 showed how the fixed-price contracts of the Y2K years first rewarded the firms that could do the same work with fewer hours. The evidence from 2026 suggests that the hourly model was being squeezed from both ends. Clients would not pay for hours that a machine could save, and the vendors had to promise the savings before they had made them.

## The class of 2026

The colleges asked a simpler question: would the entry-level job survive? The evidence is mixed, and it is easy to read too much into it.

In April 2026 *Business Standard* reported that TCS had given 'a fresher hiring number of 25,000' for the year to March 2027, and set this against the roughly 40,000 that the paper said TCS usually took from engineering colleges.[80] Other reporting puts that figure in a different light. Krithivasan had told the Press Trust of India earlier in April: 'We have made 25,000 offers for freshers in FY27. Clarity on demand will lead to more hiring.'[81] The 25,000 were offers already made by April, not a ceiling. The same report said TCS had 'reportedly' hired more than 44,000 freshers in the year to March 2026.[82] By October 2026, according to TCS's quarterly results as reported, about 14,000 freshers had joined in the April-June quarter and about 10,000 in the July-September quarter, and TCS's headcount had risen for three quarters in a row, to 598,056.[83] Its chief human resources officer, Sudeep Kunnumal, said: 'We are hiring for demand.'[84] The fair summary is that TCS's fresher hiring in 2026 was cautious and dependent on demand, not that it was cut to a fixed lower number.

The other firms were uneven. Infosys told *Business Standard* in April 2026 that it would add 20,000 freshers in the coming year.[85] That is a plan for graduate hires, and it measures something different from the 87,286 offers of all kinds in Infosys's annual report for the year before, so the two numbers should not be set against each other.[86] Wipro's chief human resources officer, Saurabh Govil, declined to give a figure: 'Hiring will be as per demand.'[87] HCLTech's chief people officer, Ramachandran Sundararajan, said: 'Given the current environment, it is difficult to provide a number.'[88] HCLTech had hired more than 11,000 graduates the year before.[89] Krithivasan added, in the newspaper's paraphrase, that freshers take 'up to nine months to come on the floor, while a lateral can deliver immediately'.[90] The complaint that graduates arrive unready is as old as the industry. Chapter 7 showed NIIT and Aptech building businesses on it. What changed was that some of the junior work a fresher used to learn on, such as fixing bugs and testing, could now be handed to a tool.

*Business Standard* itself blamed something else for the gloomy outlook: 'The impact of the conflict in West Asia is most pronounced in the hiring outlook for this fiscal.'[91] The dates matter here. Infosys's offers had already collapsed in the year to March 2024, and the delayed joining dates began in 2022.[92] Yet the company's first annual report to mention the 'code generation capabilities of AI technologies' was the one filed in June 2024; the reports for the two years before it do not use the phrase.[93] The first coding tool Infosys named in a filing, GitHub Copilot, appeared in the report filed in July 2025, and Wipro's first count of staff using such tools covered the year to March 2025.[94] On the companies' own records, the slowdown in graduate hiring began before AI coding tools were a reported part of their work. The timing alone cannot show that AI caused the slowdown in graduate hiring. AI is one of several causes on the evidence available, alongside the end of the pandemic boom, weak demand from clients and geopolitics.

What the slowdown felt like on a campus is documented by Yashraj Sharma of *Rest of World*, in a report published on 9 December 2025. Rishabh Mishra joined the Indian Institute of Information Technology, Design and Manufacturing in Jabalpur in 2022 with what the reporter called 'the most predictable dream in global tech': to 'study computer science, write code, and one day make it to Silicon Valley'.[95] His course was due to end in May 2026. Among his 400 classmates, according to the article, fewer than 25% had secured job offers; the article does not say where the figure came from.[96] 'It is really bad out there,' Mishra said.[97] Some of his classmates were thinking of further study before looking for work. He was doubtful: 'But after one year, if you return to the job market, your degree is even more irrelevant.'[98] Another student named in the report, Nishant Kaushik, who had studied computer science at a well-ranked college in eastern India, had decided to look for jobs in sales or marketing instead.[99] *Rest of World* framed its report around AI, and it cited a finding by the consultancy EY that Indian IT services firms had cut entry-level roles by 20-25% because of automation and AI.[100] The EY study was not read for this book. One college's placement rate in one year is a single data point, and Mishra's experience should not be taken as typical. It is the experience of a real student, in his own words, at the moment the argument about AI reached the campuses.

Those who did get jobs found that the starting salary had barely moved. Udit Hinduja of *ThePrint* reported in September 2025 on a TCS fresher in Ahmedabad who shared a room with three colleagues he had met in training, on a monthly salary of Rs 24,000 (about $270 at the year's average rate). 'I'm lucky that I had some savings to buy a bike, otherwise I would have to walk to work,' he said.[101] Neeti Sharma, chief executive of the staffing firm TeamLease Digital, told the same reporter that starting salaries for science and engineering graduates had been Rs 2-2.5 lakh a year in 2014 and were Rs 3-3.5 lakh (about $3,400-4,000) 'today', 'not even enough to cover inflation over the last decade'.[102] The fresher was not named, and the salary estimates are a staffing executive's.

## The regulator's answer

The All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE), the regulator that approves engineering colleges, responded with announcements. On 23 December 2024 it declared 2025 the 'Year of Artificial Intelligence', with initiatives to be carried out across more than 14,000 colleges with 40 million students.[103] They included a campaign called 'AI for All', AI laboratories, workshops for teachers and careers advice aimed at AI jobs.[104] On 6 June 2025 its chairman, T.G. Sitharam, told the Press Trust of India that AI would be brought into the model curricula of every branch of engineering, information technology and management. 'A committee of experts has been constituted to suggest necessary revisions in the model curriculums,' he said.[105] He noted that AICTE had introduced a B.Tech degree in AI and data science as early as 2017.[106]

No circular making AI part of every branch, and no revised model curriculum, was found in the research for this book. The regulator announced the change; whether colleges carried it out is not documented here. Chapter 12 found that AICTE could approve a curriculum but could not make an under-funded private college teach it well, and there is no evidence yet that AI will be different. The figure of 40 million students is the regulator's own.

## Washington's $100,000

The other door that had shaped the profession was the one to the United States. On 19 September 2025 President Donald Trump signed Proclamation 10973, 'Restriction on Entry of Certain Nonimmigrant Workers'.[107] Its opening said the H-1B programme, the American visa for skilled temporary workers that carried Indian engineers to client sites from the 1990s (Chapter 13), had been 'deliberately exploited to replace, rather than supplement, American workers'.[108] It singled out the industry this book is about: 'Information technology (IT) firms in particular have prominently manipulated the H-1B system.'[109] In support it cited a rise in the share of H-1B workers in IT from 32% in the US fiscal year 2003 to an average of more than 65% in the previous five years, and a study showing a '36 percent discount' for H-1B entry-level positions.[110] It also cited unemployment rates of 6.1% and 7.5% among recent graduates aged 22 to 27 in computer science and computer engineering.[111] Further on, it cited an unnamed 2017 study estimating that, without foreign workers, American computer scientists' wages would have been 2.6-5.1% higher in 2001 and their employment 6.1-10.8% higher.[112]

The operative clause restricted the entry of H-1B workers except those whose petitions were 'accompanied or supplemented by a payment of $100,000', from 12:01 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time on 21 September 2025, for twelve months.[113] It applied only to entries after that date, and it ordered new rules on wages.[114]

The traffic was already falling before the fee. The National Foundation for American Policy, a pro-immigration research group, counted 4,573 H-1B petitions approved for initial employment in the US fiscal year 2025 for the top seven Indian-based companies, 70% fewer than in 2015 and 37% fewer than in 2024.[115] TCS had 846 such approvals in 2025, against 1,452 the year before.[116]

On 18 September 2026 a second proclamation, 11069, extended the restriction to 21 September 2027.[117] Its preamble gave the administration's account of the first year. The $100,000 payment had been made for 'over 700 petitions'.[118] A new rule had weighted the H-1B lottery towards higher-paid workers for the 2027 season.[119] And 'the largest IT staffing and outsourcing firms' had cut 'their combined H-1B registrations from 24,946 to 2,055, a 92 percent decrease'.[120] The firms were not named, and the years compared are not stated in that sentence; the next sentence compares the 2025 and 2027 application seasons.[121] These are a party's figures, offered to justify the policy. If they are right, the route that carried Indian engineers to American client sites for three decades had nearly closed for the outsourcing firms within a year.

Then the courts intervened, and here precision matters, because the sources disagree. On 8 June 2026 Judge Leo T. Sorokin of the US District Court for the District of Massachusetts ruled in a suit brought by a coalition of states. According to a summary by the law firm Clark Hill, he held that the $100,000 fee was unlawful: in effect a tax that only Congress could impose, and a breach of the Administrative Procedure Act and of the separation of powers. Clark Hill described the decision as vacating the proclamation itself.[122] A later summary by DiRaimondo & Schroeder, another immigration law firm, described the same ruling as vacating 'the implementing policies', the agencies' rules for collecting the fee, and said the appeals court had refused to let the fee take effect while the government appealed.[123] On 30 September 2026, in *Global Nurse Force v. Trump*, Judge Haywood S. Gilliam Jr. of the Northern District of California vacated the agency policies implementing both proclamations, because they had been issued without the public notice and comment the law requires. That firm wrote: 'The Court did not strike down the President's orders themselves.'[124] The court orders were not read for this book. What both summaries agree on is that by early October 2026 two federal courts had blocked the collection of the fee, and the government was appealing. Separately, the Department of Homeland Security had proposed a fee of about $103,000 for certain H-1B petitions, through the formal rule-making process that the courts said had been skipped.[125] The matter was unsettled when this chapter was written.

## The state builds a model

The Indian state answered the AI question as it had answered identity and payments (Chapters 16 and 17), by building. The IndiaAI Mission was approved in March 2024 with an outlay of Rs 10,372 crore (Rs 103.7 billion, about $1.25 billion at the year's average rate), for computing capacity, data, models and research.[126] It selected twelve organisations and consortia to build foundation models, the large general-purpose models on which applications are built.[127] The first was Sarvam AI, founded in August 2023 by Vivek Raghavan and Pratyush Kumar, both graduates of the IITs.[128] On 26 April 2025, according to the trade journal *tele.net*, Sarvam was chosen to build 'an open source 120 billion parameter' model, out of 506 proposals received by the end of that month; three more start-ups, Soket AI, Gnani AI and Gan AI, were chosen later.[129] The minister for electronics and information technology, Ashwini Vaishnaw, said: 'We are confident that Sarvam's models will be competitive with global models.'[130]

At the India AI Impact Summit in February 2026 Sarvam unveiled two models, Sarvam-30B and Sarvam-105B.[131] The second is smaller than the 120 billion parameters announced in 2025, and the government's own release would be needed to know what was contracted. Debarghya Sil of *Inc42* judged the models competitive in their size class and strong on Indian languages, but behind the frontier models of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta in 'raw reasoning depth'.[132] Raghavan called the launches a 'significant first step'.[133] The state was not trying to replace the services industry with a model of its own. It was trying to make sure that the country that had supplied so much of the world's programming labour owned some of the machines that were starting to do it.

## More coders than ever

In October 2025 GitHub, the code-hosting service, published its annual *Octoverse* report. As reported by Joe Fay in *The Register*, it projected that India would add more than 35.6 million developers on the platform by 2030, reaching 57.5 million or more and overtaking the United States, projected at 54.7 million.[134] It also said that 80% of new users began using its AI coding assistant, Copilot, in their first week, which GitHub described as 'part of the default developer experience'.[135] These are a company's figures about its own platform. A GitHub account is not a job, and the projection is not a count of engineers.

Even so, the two numbers belong together. More Indians were writing code than at any point in the history this book has traced, and most of the newest of them were writing it with a machine at their elbow. The industry that had been built on selling their hours was telling its regulators that clients would buy fewer of them. The colleges were still producing graduates on the scale set in Chapter 12, and the regulator was promising to teach them AI. Washington had tried to price the old route to America out of reach, and the courts had so far stopped it. The state was building models of its own. None of these lines had reached an end by the autumn of 2026. Where they might lead by 2035 is not a matter of record, and the epilogue sets out several possible answers as scenarios, each tied to the evidence of the chapters before it.
