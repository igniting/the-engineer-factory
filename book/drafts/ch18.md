# Chapter 18. The pandemic boom (2020-2023)

On 25 March 2020 the Indian government put the country under lockdown.[1] Infosys, the second-largest of the Indian software services firms, had moved first.[2] In the annual report it filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission two months later, the company wrote: 'By March 20, 2020, we had enabled 93% of our employees to be able to work remotely.'[3] The company had 242,371 employees at the end of that month.[4] By the date of filing the figure was 98%, and the campuses in India were close to empty. 'Only essential and critical services for our clients and our own operations are being supported from our campuses in India,' the filing said.[5]

The same report listed what else had changed. The company had enabled remote working on secure laptops and desktops, increased the bandwidth of its data networks, published guidelines on confidentiality for staff working at home, and 'Implemented information security controls for remote working and activity monitoring.'[6] It would 'Continue to monitor the productivity of employees as they work remotely'.[7] Even the training school moved. Infosys's next annual report recorded that 'The early days of the COVID-19 pandemic saw us relocate trainees from Mysuru and continue their training without missing a beat', using an in-house learning platform called Lex.[8] Mysuru (Mysore) was where, as Chapter 12 described, the company trained its new graduates before they were placed on projects. Now the trainees were sent away and taught on screens.

For thirty years the Indian services firm had sold its clients a place as well as people: secure floors, dedicated links and guarded campuses (Chapters 11 and 12). In a few weeks of 2020 the place went away and the work carried on. This chapter follows what came next. Demand for the industry's services surged, and the firms hired on a scale they had never attempted. Engineers left their jobs at rates the companies called unprecedented, and some took second jobs on the side. Then demand turned, and campus recruits found their joining dates pushed back by months and years. The workers who complained began, for the first time in large numbers, to do so through unions. By the end of the period, one sentence in a company filing showed that something new had entered the terms on which India sold its engineers' time.

## The factory goes home

The move was not as smooth as the headline figure suggests. Many of the industry's contracts specified where the work would be done. Infosys's annual report for the year to March 2020 warned that its ability to deliver while employees worked from home was 'sometimes constrained by contractual terms with our clients'.[9] Some clients had refused permission, or had been slow to give it: 'Since few of our clients did not give us the required permissions, or there were delays in obtaining them, our service delivery was marginally disrupted'.[10] The company estimated that about a third of the pandemic's effect on the company in the last quarter of that fiscal year, which it called not significant, came from demand, partly because clients had not approved home working for the staff on their projects.[11]

Within a year those doubts had mostly gone. 'As of March 31, 2021, we had enabled 99.3% of our employees with a secure remote working environment and 96.5% of our employees were working remotely,' Infosys reported.[12] Staff on critical client projects and on work involving sensitive data still went into the offices.[13] The next year's report summed up the first weeks in a single figure: 'we were able to transition 99% of employees globally to a work from home arrangement.'[14]

The filings also recorded what home working cost the people doing it, in the language of risk. 'The productivity of our employees may be negatively impacted due to isolated remote working from home, quarantine requirements, negative social sentiment and personal anxiety,' Infosys wrote in 2020 and repeated in 2021.[15] The sources in hand do not record how individual engineers lived through those months: who worked from a parents' house in a small town, who shared a rented room in Bangalore (Bengaluru), how the hours changed. That evidence would have to come from surveys and personal accounts not yet gathered for this book.

What the filings do show is the factory's front door changing. Infosys's recruiters had tested and interviewed candidates in person. In the year to March 2021, the company said, 'All interviews across the Globe in fiscal 2021 were conducted virtually', and the process from application to offer was digitised.[16] The training that followed was online too. The company's Foundation Program for new graduates, run by its Global Education Center, had a curriculum of more than 46 variants of technology streams, behavioural competencies and niche skills, and Lex carried over 1,800 courses.[17] Infosys said it had also 'repurposed Lex for over a million college students in India via our InfyTQ app', a platform that, in the company's words a year later, 'brings the best of our Mysuru training to the hands of the learners across the country'.[18] Students who passed its assessments could earn an Infosys certification.[19] The firm's training school, which had once been a campus that graduates travelled to after they were hired, was now partly a phone app that students used before.

These are the company's own descriptions, and they say nothing about how well remote training worked for a twenty-two-year-old learning to program in a crowded home. They do establish the point that matters for the rest of the book. The work of the Indian services firm, and the teaching that prepared people for it, could be done almost anywhere. That opened two doors the industry had kept shut. An engineer working at home could work for two employers at once. And work that had been shown to need no particular place could later be examined for whether it needed a particular person.

## Five million applications

Demand came back faster than the firms had planned for, and the clearest record of it is in the recruiting figures.

Infosys publishes its recruitment funnel every year. In the year to March 2020 (FY2019-20) it received 2,141,373 applications, interviewed 150,130 people and made 83,319 offers of employment. In FY2020-21, the first pandemic year, the offers fell to 48,029.[20] Then came the surge. In FY2021-22 Infosys received 5,866,636 applications, interviewed 523,385 applicants and made 222,500 offers.[21] The offers figure counts experienced recruits as well as new graduates, so it is not a count of freshers. It is still the largest annual figure in any year for which this book has the company's numbers.[22] Offers rose about four and a half times in a single year.

Headcount followed. Infosys had 242,371 employees in March 2020, 259,619 in March 2021, 314,015 in March 2022 and 343,234 in March 2023.[23] Wipro, which reports rounded figures, said it had more than 200,000 employees in March 2021, 240,000 in 2022 and 250,000 in 2023.[24] Cognizant Technology Solutions, registered in the United States but with most of its staff in India (Chapter 15), went from about 289,500 employees at the end of 2020 to 355,300 at the end of 2022. Of the latter, 258,500 were in India.[25]

Most of the new hires came from the same colleges that had fed the factory for two decades (Chapter 12). A smaller stream came from elsewhere. Infosys said it had 'recruited over 7,280 employees locally in our markets' in FY2020-21, of whom 1,941 were fresh graduates, and that this hiring 'significantly de-risks our operations from regulatory changes related to immigration'.[26] The figures for the next two years were 14,805 local hires (3,650 fresh graduates) and 10,169 (2,216 fresh graduates).[27] The company ran versions of its foundation training in Mexico, Britain, Germany, Australia, Singapore and Japan, and a training centre in Indianapolis.[28] Beside the hundreds of thousands hired in India these numbers are small. They show the firms insuring themselves against the visa politics of Chapter 13 by training some of their recruits in the client's own country.

## A third of the workforce, every year

The boom was a labour-market event as much as a demand event. Engineers could leave, and they did.

Cognizant gives the clearest numbers, because, as an American company, it reports attrition (the share of staff who leave in a year) in its annual 10-K filing. Counting both resignations and dismissals, its attrition was 20.6% in 2020, 30.8% in 2021 and 31.7% in 2022. In the last quarter of 2021, on an annualised basis, it reached 34.6%.[29] 'In 2021, we experienced unprecedented attrition, which was considered industry-wide,' the company wrote. 'As a result, we hired over a hundred thousand new employees'.[30] It hired more than a hundred thousand again in 2022.[31] The departures were concentrated at the bottom of the pyramid. 'Our attrition is weighted towards our more junior employees,' Cognizant said, and in 2021 'voluntary attrition constituted the vast majority' of the total. In 2020, by contrast, only about half had been voluntary, because the company's own restructuring programme had raised the number of people it let go while the pandemic held down resignations.[32]

At those rates a large firm had to refill its pyramid almost from scratch every three years. Every departure meant another recruit to find, test, train and place on a project, and every replacement had to be paid more to stay. Cognizant's risk factors put the problem in the plainest terms of the business model: 'While we strive to adjust pricing to reduce the impact of compensation increases on our operating margin, we may not be successful in recovering these increases'.[33] Wipro's filing for FY2021-22 listed the same pressures on its margins: 'strong demand for digital and other niche skills are expected to result in wage inflation', 'Increased hiring and training costs due to higher attrition rates', and a 'Limited ability of the market to accept increase in prices for our offerings'.[34] This was the time-and-materials model under stress (Chapters 9 and 11). A firm that sells hours at an agreed rate makes money only while the hours cost it less than the rate. When engineers' pay rose faster than clients would pay, the margin was squeezed from both sides.

The equivalent attrition rates for Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) and Infosys appear in their quarterly fact sheets, which could not be obtained for this book.[35] Cognizant's are the only primary series in hand, and they come from a firm whose junior staff are mostly in India but whose definitions are its own.

The gains were not evenly shared. The demand for talent after the pandemic, *HR Katha* observed, had firms luring people with attractive packages and perks. Starting pay was another matter. A study by the business website Moneycontrol, relayed by the trade site *HR Katha*, put the median annual pay of freshers at large IT firms at about Rs 245,000 in 2012 and Rs 355,000 in 2022, a rise of 45% in ten years.[36] At each year's average exchange rate both figures come to about $4,500.[37] Adjusted for Indian consumer prices, the 2022 figure was worth nearly a fifth less than the 2012 one.[38] The same report noted that the median pay of chief executives at the large firms had risen by 835% over the same decade.[39] Whatever the boom did for engineers who changed jobs, on these figures it did not raise the real price of a new one.

Women's share of the workforce held steady through the churn, at least at the firms that report it. Cognizant said women made up 36% of its workforce at the end of 2020 and 38% at the end of 2021, the share it reported again for 2023.[40] Infosys said in its annual report for FY2023-24 that women made up 'nearly 40% of our workforce'.[41] Neither firm's filings, as read for this chapter, say whether women left or returned at different rates from men during the pandemic.

## Two employers, one laptop

Home working made a second job easy to hide. An engineer whose office was a bedroom, and whose manager saw her only on a video call, could in principle work for two companies at once. In 2022 the practice, known as moonlighting, became the subject of a public argument among the industry's leaders.

In August 2022 Rishad Premji, the chairman of Wipro, wrote on Twitter that there was a lot of 'chatter about people moonlighting in the tech industry', and that 'it is cheating- plain and simple.'[42] On 21 September he went further. Speaking at the convention of the All India Management Association, he said: 'The reality is that there are people today working for Wipro and working directly for one of our competitors and we have actually discovered 300 people in the last few months who are doing exactly that.'[43] Wipro had dismissed them. *Business Today* reported that Premji regarded the practice as a 'complete violation of integrity in its deepest form'.[44]

The industry did not speak with one voice. Infosys, the magazine reported, had told its staff the week before, in 'a strong and firm message', that moonlighting was not allowed. N. Ganapathy Subramaniam, the chief operating officer of TCS, had warned that employees chasing short-term gains in this way 'might lose out in the long term'.[45] T.V. Mohandas Pai, a former Infosys director, took the other side. 'No, moonlighting is not cheating,' he told the magazine. 'Employment is a contract between an employer who pays me for working for them for 'n' number of hours a day.' During those hours, he said, he was bound by the employer's conditions, including client confidentiality. 'Now what I do after that time is my freedom'.[46]

Infosys then wrote the problem into its annual report. From FY2022-23 its risk factors included a new line: 'Continued remote working exposes the company to the risk of some employees working for multiple employers simultaneously (moonlighting) without obtaining required prior approvals and this may result in potential conflict of interests, confidentiality breach, and reduced productivity.'[47]

The quarrel looks like one about loyalty. It was also about the unit in which the industry sold its work. The services firms billed their clients by the hour or the person-month. Pai's defence turned the same logic around: the employer bought a fixed number of hours, and the rest belonged to the worker. Premji's answer was that an engineer working directly for a competitor was breaking faith whatever the hours. When the work was done on a client's secure floor, the question did not arise, because the office day bounded what an engineer could do. At home that boundary was gone, and both sides found how much of the employment relationship had depended on it. The firms' answer, in the end, was to bring people back. Infosys described 2022 as the year it 'transitioned into a hybrid model of work for our employees and service providers following an extended period of remote work', and from FY2022-23 listed 'Our transition to a hybrid working model' among its risks.[48]

## Packed bags

The turn in demand came in the second half of 2022. Cognizant dated it precisely: 'The rate of attrition began to decrease in the second half of 2022'.[49] For the engineers already employed, that meant fewer outside offers. For the graduates who had been promised jobs during the boom, it meant waiting.

On 5 October 2022 *Business Today* published complaints from new graduates whose start dates at Accenture kept moving. The magazine had already reported similar delays at Wipro, Infosys, HCL Tech, Tech Mahindra and Capgemini.[50] The graduates it quoted were not named. One said: 'I was placed in Accenture as Associate Software Engineer (ASE) via campus placement in November 2021. I received my offer letter in the last week of April this year.' Since then, the start date had been 'pushed time after time'; the latest, 30 September, had now become 'somewhere in 2023'.[51] Another described a postponement two days before the joining date: 'I had packed my bags and booked all tickets to move to the new city, just two days before joining, they postponed the date again. When will they finally make me join, who will pay for my expenses in the meanwhile?'[52] A third had turned down other employers: 'I am riddled with depressive feelings because I rejected TCS, Wipro, and HCL for a job in Accenture.'[53] Others worried about the record it would leave: 'It's a year since I have been waiting to get onboarded. It is going to reflect as a gap year in my CV.'[54]

The graduates also blamed their colleges. The placement cell, the office that had invited the companies to campus and organised the tests, was the link between the college and the factory (Chapter 12). 'The college placement cell should have helped us in this tough time,' said a graduate of CV Raman Global University in Bhubaneswar. A graduate of Galgotias University in Greater Noida said the placement office had stopped answering once students had graduated: 'they don't care for me anymore.'[55] For the colleges, a campus offer was a result to be counted at the end of the year. What happened after graduation was no longer their business.

None of this was new. The bust of 2001-02 had left recruits at Anna University waiting for deferred joining dates (Chapter 11), and the collapse of Satyam in 2009 had stranded a batch of its campus hires (Chapter 16). This was the third time in about twenty years that the factory's newest products took the first shock of a turn in demand. Each time, the offer letter turned out to be a promise the firm could defer at little cost to itself.

At Infosys the numbers turned with unusual speed. Offers of employment fell from 222,500 in FY2021-22 to 114,374 in FY2022-23, and to 26,975 in FY2023-24, a fall of about 88% in two years. Applications fell from 5.9 million to 2.4 million.[56] Headcount, which had peaked at 343,234 in March 2023, fell to 317,240 a year later.[57] Wipro's rounded total fell from more than 250,000 to more than 234,000 over the same year.[58] Cognizant ended 2023 with about 347,700 employees, 7,600 fewer than a year before, and with 254,000 in India.[59] Its own measure of voluntary attrition in its technology services business fell from 25.6% in 2022 to 13.8% in 2023.[60] The engineers who had changed jobs freely in 2021 now stayed put.

## Outside the factory laws

The people who took the delayed recruits' complaints to the government belonged to organisations that had barely existed a few years earlier. The industry had grown without unions, and partly under exemptions from the labour laws that governed factories.

A note published in November 2017 by Arvind Gurumurthy of the law firm Kochhar & Co. set out the position. The Industrial Disputes Act of 1947 required employers with more than 100 workers to get the labour commissioner's approval before retrenching staff, 'but this does not cover IT industry'.[61] The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act of 1946 required industrial employers to publish certified terms of employment covering hours, shifts, leave and dismissal. 'However, by a notification published on January 25, 2014 the Karnataka government exempted IT companies from applicability of Standing Orders Act for a period of five years.'[62] Some provisions of the state's shops and establishments law, on holidays and hours of work, had also been relaxed for IT companies in Karnataka and in some other states.[63] The engineer factory, in other words, was not in law a factory. The note suggested that the exemptions might have been meant to help the industry grow, and recorded that many employees' leaders saw them as giving firms room to treat layoffs as they liked.[64]

On Sunday 20 August 2017 close to 200 IT workers gathered at the YWCA Hall in Koramangala, Bengaluru, to found the Karnataka State IT/ITeS Employees Union, known as KITU. *The News Minute* reported that the meeting came amid 'reports of large-scale layoffs in the IT sector'.[65] The union's general secretary was Vineeth, a testing lead at the Bengaluru software firm MindTree. He was also part of the IT Employees Centre, an association that had worked for IT employees' rights in Bengaluru for eight years and had received complaints about layoffs and maternity leave, but had never been registered as a union. 'What is currently happening is that if an employee is facing a problem, they have to fight in their individual capacity and the companies may not listen to them,' he told the reporter. 'The trade union will help them negotiate and bargain with the management, and even fight.'[66]

Others at the meeting spoke in the same terms. Sethumadhavan, a Wipro employee, said: 'As per our Constitution, we have the right to join a union.' The industry, he said, was facing many challenges, among them layoffs and automation, and 'This is the right time to form an organisation'.[67] Akshaya, an IBM employee on the union's executive committee, described the hours: 'sometimes we are given a project that requires us to work 15 hours a day and we are paid just Rs 25 per extra hour.'[68] The union's president, Amanullah Khan, answered the charge that unions held the country back: 'Trade unions are generally seen as anti-social or anti-national.' He said that neither the industry body Nasscom nor the government would be pleased. 'But we are not individuals anymore.'[69] The report noted that Mohandas Pai, among others, had opposed unions in the IT sector, on the grounds that they would harm a service industry.[70] It added that the Forum for IT Employees in Chennai had also applied to form a union.[71]

On 7 November 2017 the Karnataka labour commissioner certified KITU's formation under the Trade Unions Act of 1926.[72] Kochhar & Co. called it 'the first trade union in India, officially registered and recognised by the labour department, which is dedicated to the information technology ("IT") sector', with about 250 members by its own claim.[73] The word 'first' needs care. The same note acknowledged that 'unrecognised groups' had already existed, and the IT Employees Centre had been working for eight years before KITU was founded.[74] What is documented is narrower: KITU was the first IT employees' union registered in Karnataka, home to the city the reporter called India's Silicon Valley.

The pandemic gave such organisations work. A second body, the Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), based in Pune, took up the cause of Infosys's delayed recruits. According to *The News Minute*, on 20 August 2024 its president, Harpreet Singh Saluja, filed complaints alleging 'the exploitation and unprofessional treatment of over 2,000 fresh engineering graduates selected by Infosys' in its 2022-23 campus drive, for the roles of System Engineer and Digital Specialist Engineer.[75] After the union had first asked the central government to intervene, in June, Infosys had issued about 2,000 final offer letters with joining dates in October 2024. On 3 September the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment instructed the Karnataka government to take 'appropriate action' and directed the state labour commissioner's office to investigate. The directive, signed by the Regional Labour Commissioner, O.P. Singh, said that enforcing labour law in the matter was the state's responsibility.[76] About 700 candidates, the report said, were still waiting for joining dates.[77]

Neither union's membership in these years is documented in the sources used for this chapter. Their influence is easier to see than their size: complaints that would once have been one graduate's grievance against one company were now reaching a ministry in Delhi.

## The gate at Mysuru

Recruits who did join went where Infosys recruits had always gone, to the training campus at Mysuru. In February 2025 the story of the delayed batch reached its sequel there. *Deccan Herald* reported that Infosys had 'admitted to laying off over 300 freshers, who had undergone foundational training at the Infosys Mysuru campus for months'. NITES said the true number was 700.[78] The paper reported that the trainees had been made to sign 'mutual separation' agreements.[79] The union's complaint to the labour ministry said: 'Infosys Ltd has resorted to forcibly terminating recently onboarded campus recruits who had already suffered a delay of two years in their joining after being issued offer letters.'[80] It asked for the dismissed trainees to be reinstated and for penalties under the Industrial Disputes Act.[81]

Infosys's account was different. The company said it had not forced anyone out. It had a 'rigorous hiring process' in which trainees had to clear internal assessments within three attempts, 'failing which they will not be able to continue with the organisation', and this process, it said, had existed for more than two decades.[82] A trainee dismissed in the cuts countered, in the paper's words, that it was the first time the company had laid off such a large number of students.[83] On 13 February the Union Labour Ministry directed the Karnataka Labour Department to take 'urgent necessary actions to resolve the dispute'.[84] The two sides' numbers, over 300 and 700, were not reconciled in the reporting.

Both accounts can be true at once. The final assessment had been part of the factory from the start; Chapter 12 described the fourteen-week course at Mysore and the tests at its end. What changed was the setting. Recruits who had waited two years, while the market that had hired them disappeared, met the old gate at a moment when the company needed fewer of them. The training campus had been built to turn graduates into billable engineers. In 2025 it also served as the way out.

## Offices, and the people in them

The bust brought restructuring as well as slower hiring. In the second quarter of 2023 Cognizant began what it called its NextGen programme, 'aimed at simplifying our operating model, optimizing corporate functions and consolidating and realigning office space to reflect the post-pandemic hybrid work environment'.[85] In 2023 the programme cost $115 million in employee separation costs and $114 million in facility exit and other costs, $229 million in all.[86] The company warned that 'Our NextGen program and the associated reductions in headcount and consolidation of office space could disrupt our business'.[87]

The pandemic's lesson about place had come round again. In 2020 the firms had learned that the work could leave the campus. In 2023 one of them was giving up offices because hybrid working meant it needed fewer of them, and was cutting staff in the same programme. Infosys, for its part, listed among the risks of its own hybrid model the costs of keeping offices 'safe and hygienic' for those who came back.[88]

## The sentence in the risk factors

The most important single sentence of these years for the rest of this book appeared in Infosys's annual report for FY2023-24, filed in June 2024. In its risk factors, among the familiar warnings about competition and pricing, the company repeated a line it had carried in each annual report since 2020: 'due to competitive market conditions and pricing pressures, we are committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts with our clients.' Then it added a new one: 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements.'[89] If those promised gains did not materialise, the filing said, the company's profitability could suffer.[90]

The sentence is short, and it is easy to read too much into it. It does not say that artificial intelligence had cut Infosys's hiring. The same filing pointed elsewhere when it described pressure on demand, warning that clients 'may become cautious and reduce their discretionary spending in the short term or delay their decision-making process', a risk it had listed the year before as well, in the setting of clients closing operations in Russia because of the conflict in Eastern Europe.[91] The fall in offers from 222,500 to 26,975 coincided with the arrival of generative AI tools in late 2022 and 2023. The sources used here show the coincidence in time. They do not show that one caused the other. That argument belongs to the next chapter, and needs more evidence than one risk factor.

What the sentence does show is narrower and, for the book's argument, sharper. Clients had read about software that writes code. They had asked their Indian suppliers to commit, in contract, to doing the same work with less effort, and the supplier bore the risk if the gains did not come. Under time-and-materials billing, a productivity gain that the client captures means fewer billable hours. Under a fixed-price contract, it means a lower price for the same result. Either way the first share of the gain goes to the client, and the firm keeps something only if it beats its own promise. The same filing said that Infosys had 'made significant investments in building our capabilities and training our employees in emerging technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI), generative AI and cloud technologies', and noted that many larger competitors had done the same.[92]

The pandemic years had shown three things about the engineer factory. Its intake was set by clients' discretionary spending, not by the number of graduates: offers at one firm had risen four and a half times in a year and then fallen by nearly nine-tenths in two. Its workers, for a brief period, had the upper hand, and used it by leaving, by moonlighting and, in small numbers, by organising. And its work no longer needed a particular place. By 2024 the clients were asking whether it needed the same number of hours. For forty years the Indian services firms had grown by selling their engineers' time. Now they were being asked, in writing, to promise that the time would shrink.
