# Chapter 8. Satellite and permit (1984-1991)

On a day in 1985 that he called memorable when he wrote about it in 2013, the journalist Janardhan Roye found his Ambassador car stuck behind a bullock cart outside his office on Miller Road in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014). The cart was heavily laden, and it was struggling up the steep slope at the gate of an office block called Sona Towers. Traffic piled up and horns blared until security staff came to the aid of the cart's driver.[1] The people in the neighbouring offices, he recalled, were less interested in the traffic than in the wooden boxes being unloaded.[2]

The boxes were addressed to the Bangalore office of Texas Instruments (TI), the semiconductor company from Dallas. They held the equipment for a two-way satellite link between India and the United States, for data transmission and software development.[3] Among the onlookers, Roye recalled, was Sam Pitroda, the engineer who led the government's telecommunications research centre, C-DOT, and who was often to be seen at its offices on the floor above Roye's. When the contents of the boxes became known, Roye remembered him shaking his head and smiling: 'This can happen only in India.'[4] The building's owner, the Sona Group, tells the same story of a satellite dish arriving by bullock cart.[5]

The dish was small by later standards. Roye gives its capacity as 64 kilobits a second, and he described the antenna as 'like a whale surfacing'.[6] What made it remarkable was not its speed but its permission. Until then, telephone networks and overseas links had been a government monopoly, run by a department whose staff were civil servants. TI's dish was a private line out of the country, approved by the state.[7]

The years from the computer policy of November 1984 to the first Software Technology Parks of 1990 and 1991 were the years of Rajiv Gandhi's government and its immediate successors, and they are often remembered as the moment India's information technology industry began. The record supports a narrower claim. The permit regime was loosened in pieces, and much of what changed was paperwork: duties, licences and the definitions of what counted as an export. The changes that moved software work into India, rather than sending Indian programmers abroad to do it, were mostly about telecommunications, and they came late and slowly. A satellite dish on a bullock cart is a fair emblem of both halves.

## The permit, loosened

Rajiv Gandhi had learned what computers could do at a sporting event. When the Asian Games were held in Delhi in 1982, he, the son of the prime minister, Indira Gandhi, and in the account of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, 'an amateur electronics buff', was given overall charge of organising them. He decided that computers should draw up the schedules, keep the event records, announce the results and do the clerical work. Locally made DCM computers served as terminals at the venues, connected to a Hewlett-Packard server, and software engineers of the government's National Informatics Centre wrote the whole system in six months. The computerisation of the Games was, in Rajaraman's words, 'a resounding success', and it brought the centre's director general, N. Seshagiri, close to Rajiv Gandhi.[8] Organising the Games also brought him into contact with the private computer makers, who told him of their difficulties with 'myriad rules and regulations'.[9] After Indira Gandhi's assassination in 1984 he became prime minister, with Seshagiri as an informal adviser.[10] Rajaraman describes him as a man of the post-independence generation who 'was open minded regarding private enterprise'.[11] The new computer policy followed within weeks. Dataquest's chronology dates it to 19 November 1984.[12]

Its effect on hardware was to turn Indian computer making into assembly. For exporters it did three things. It recognised software as an 'industry', which opened the way to bank loans and duty exemptions. It cut the customs duty on computers imported to write software for export, which, Rajaraman writes, 'Earlier it used to be over 100%'. And it counted engineers sent to work at clients' sites abroad as software exporters.[13]

The policy also carried a promise. According to a World Bank study of 1994 by Nagy Hanna, the government announced its Computer Policy Plan in November 1984 and 'expected software exports to reach US$258 million (or 0.5 percent of the world market) by 1990'.[14] It was a target built, the Bank thought, on 'limited but alluring data' about the world market.[15]

Two years later, in December 1986, came the Policy on Computer Software Export, Software Development and Training; in the Bank's account, 'A major reform in computer software' followed its adoption.[16] Its terms show how far the old logic of foreign exchange still governed the trade. A firm could import a computer duty-free to write software for export, but it had to 'earn 250% of the cost of the imported computer within 4 years'. Where no foreign exchange had been spent, for example 'a non resident Indian bringing a computer with his earnings', the obligation fell to 150 per cent.[17] The rule descends from the bargain Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) had struck over its Burroughs machine in the 1970s: the state would allow imports if they paid for themselves in dollars.

Two clauses pointed somewhere new. 'Software developed in India could be exported using communication systems such as satellite and cable.' And the state-owned Computer Maintenance Corporation's (CMC's) network of IBM mainframes, called Indonet, was opened to exporters at a concessional tariff.[18] Foreign capital was eased too. From 1986 foreign firms could own all of an export-oriented software project, though they were still held to 40 per cent of firms selling software in India.[19] That 40 per cent was the figure that had driven IBM out in 1978. It was now waived for anyone who exported.

The historian Dinesh Sharma sums up the effect of the two policies cautiously. Imports of hardware and software were liberalised, 'though the actual import of hardware still took time'. Bringing software under the Open General Licence, the list of goods that could be imported without a separate permit, meant that 'New software packages and tools started becoming available'.[20] The World Bank was less kind about the system that administered all this. In its account, policy had 'fundamentally focused on ways to make export figures rise', while 'troublesome delays exist for import of computers and spare parts'. 'On the positive side,' it concluded, 'it has pushed firms into exporting. On the negative side, this pushing has been short-term oriented'.[21]

## The dish

TI's link required a negotiation inside the government before it required a bullock cart. Rajaraman, who had served on the government's Electronics Commission, records it briefly. 'The DoE negotiated with the Department of Telecommunication (DoT) in 1985 to allow Texas Instruments (TI) to set up a dedicated satellite communication link to its centre at Dallas, USA.' Over that link, he adds, TI's Bangalore centre sent the software tools it developed to Dallas.[22] The Department of Electronics (DoE), headed by scientists and engineers since 1970, promoted computing; the Department of Telecommunications ran the telephones.[23] Rajaraman judged the result 'the first step in breaking the monopoly of the DoT in communications', with far-reaching consequences for offshore development centres and, later, the Software Technology Parks.[24]

The World Bank added a detail. TI 'obtained a private Intelsat Business Service earth station so that its programmers can communicate with other TI operations using satellite links'. The special measures and approvals TI won, the Bank wrote, 'helped make India more attractive' to other foreign firms, among them Hewlett-Packard, Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), British Telecom, Philips and Westinghouse.[25] Richard Heeks of the University of Manchester, who studied the industry through fieldwork in India, reported that 'TI was known to have cut through many red tape barriers, particularly on imports', and that its arrival was 'constantly reported in the home and international media'.[26]

The date is disputed by a year. TI's own milestone list puts the start of its Indian operations in 1985, the year of the negotiation and of Roye's bullock cart; Heeks dates TI's arrival to 1986. The likeliest reading is that TI arrived in 1985 and its satellite link was working by 1986.[27]

TI's claim to have been first is narrower than the one often made for it. In 1985, its press release says, TI became 'the first multinational to establish a software design center in India and the first to install and use a satellite dish for exporting software'.[28] The World Bank's account of the private earth station supports the second half. The first half needs care. Citibank had set up a software unit in the Santa Cruz Electronics Export Processing Zone (SEEPZ) in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) in 1984, and it was inaugurated as Citicorp Overseas Software Limited on 28 October 1985; in its early years it wrote software for the bank's own use in America and other regions.[29] What TI had first was the private line.

What TI did with it was not the work most Indian exporters did. Its Bangalore engineers wrote software tools, in Rajaraman's account.[30] From 1989, according to the company, the centre designed mixed-signal integrated circuits, chips that combine analogue and digital functions, and built libraries for computer-aided chip design; by 2005 its Bangalore engineers had filed close to 300 patent applications.[31] Rajaraman notes the irony that Indian engineers 'could design large scale integrated circuits, including combined analog and digital systems for TI and other overseas companies but had no foundry to fabricate them in India'.[32] TI showed that a multinational could put design work of a high order in India, which was a different thing from Indian firms doing it.

Heeks thought TI's arrival helped settle the industry in Bangalore rather than Bombay, a shift whose causes Chapter 10 examines.[33] Roye quotes C.S. Murali of the Indian Institute of Science on the dish: 'It turbocharged the IT revolution'.[34] Vijay Chandru, a scientist whom Roye also quotes, argues that Bangalore had been a technical city well before the multinationals came.[35]

## The wires that were missing

The dish mattered because the ordinary wires were so poor. 'In 1982 there was a waiting time of over 2 years to get a land line telephone connection,' Rajaraman writes. Telephones were run by a government department whose staff were civil servants and whose budget was part of the national budget, which in his account meant low investment and poor service.[36] Under Rajiv Gandhi two reforms followed. The telephones of Bombay and Delhi were handed to a public company, Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd (MTNL), with 'operational autonomy'. A second company, Videsh Sanchar Nigam Ltd (VSNL), was set up in 1986 to run overseas communication.[37]

The more visible change came from C-DOT. Formed in 1984 under Pitroda, it was given the task of designing small digital telephone exchanges that could survive rural India's poor power supply and its temperatures of 45 degrees in summer and below freezing in winter. C-DOT 'recruited a highly motivated group of engineers', and they produced a rugged exchange 'in a record time of 3 years'.[38] Alongside it spread the public call office, a telephone booth with a microprocessor that printed the cost of each call so that an attendant could collect it. Because a booth cost little to set up, many small entrepreneurs opened them in towns, villages and on highways. 'Every nook and corner of India was connected within 5 years,' Rajaraman writes. 'The power of microcomputer was visible to all'.[39]

None of this gave a software exporter a cheap line to a client in New Jersey. The overseas gateways stayed with VSNL, and the history published by Software Technology Parks of India, the agency that later took over the parks, records that data links carried a substantial cost.[40] In 1994 the World Bank still listed poor communications links among the industry's weaknesses.[41] It argued that faster, more open data services 'would open major opportunities for off-shore software services activities in India, thereby reducing dependence on body-shopping (on-site delivery of services) and the consequent brain-drain'.[42]

The numbers show how little the policies of 1984 and 1986 had moved the work. Heeks found that in 1988 about 65 per cent of export contracts were carried out wholly at the client's site abroad.[43] Sharma records that after 1986 some firms began 'remote software development', working from India on clients' machines, 'But still there were impediments such as high telecom costs'.[44] What India sold, in other words, was still mostly engineers' time, billed where the client was. The dish and, later, the parks would change where those hours were worked. They did not change what was being sold.

## Fifty counters

Inside India, the most important computer of the decade sold railway tickets. The project matters to this book for what it did to clerks.

The argument over computers and jobs was still live in 1984. When the Reserve Bank of India's Rangarajan committee recommended that every bank do its back-office work by computer, the growth of computing in the banks was, in Rajaraman's words, 'a red flag to the trade unions', and they observed 1984 as an 'anti-computerization year'.[45] Dataquest's chronology adds that in 1984 the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh declared an 'Anti Computer Year' in protest against the bank proposal, with 1 March observed as Anti-Computer Day.[46] The unions were also bargaining. A biographical note on Suresh Dhopeshwarkar, a bank union leader, held by the Indian Labour Archives, records that in 1983 his union 'reached the first ever agreement on computerisation to meet the new challenges'.[47] The state itself had been cautious. The minicomputer policy of 1978 had said it would 'continue to be restrictive in allowing use of computers in areas where labour displacement was likely to be involved'.[48]

The railways faced a problem of scale that no one could solve by hand. In 1984, Rajaraman writes, Indian Railways handled 'over 5 million passengers travelling in over 600 long distance trains with around 50,000 reservation requests'. Passengers stood in long queues. 'Clerks kept numerous ledgers, one for each train', and juggled between them as passengers changed their minds.[49] CMC proposed a computerised system. The railways accepted. The project began in 1984, and 'by 1986 a reservation system was developed and implemented at the New Delhi booking office. It had 50 counters and customers could go to any counter to get a reservation done for any train'.[50] The official audit of the system, by the Comptroller and Auditor General, gives an exact start for a smaller first step: the pilot, called IMPRESS, 'was launched on 15 November 1985' at New Delhi. The government dates the introduction of the reservation system proper to 1986.[51]

To the public, Rajaraman writes, it was 'almost a miracle'. Instead of waiting hours and running from counter to counter, a passenger could get a confirmed reservation within 30 minutes, book connecting and return journeys, and cancel for an immediate refund.[52] For the clerks the change came in the evening's accounts. 'The counter clerks were also happy,' Rajaraman writes, 'as they could reconcile the collected cash against issued tickets with the help of the computer within 30 minutes after closing time whereas in the manual system it used to take at least two hours.'[53] And they kept their jobs. 'There was also no reduction of staff; in fact there was an increase as more counters were operated with more clerks working simultaneously.'[54]

The mechanism is worth stating because the book returns to it. Demand for reservations had been held down by the queues. When the computer let any counter sell a ticket on any train, the railways opened more counters and needed more clerks to staff them. The machine raised each clerk's output, and the extra output was absorbed by demand that had been waiting. Whether that pattern holds for a new technology depends on how much demand is waiting, and that question runs through the last part of this book.

The system ran on a cluster of DEC VAX minicomputers, with terminals at the counters, and was written in FORTRAN. 'The entire software effort was by Indian software engineers', Rajaraman writes, with no foreign consultants involved.[55] He also claims it was the first such transaction system in the world to run on a VAX cluster.[56] Its effect on opinion he does not doubt. 'There was an attitudinal change among both the general public and the white collar workers about computerization', and some bank unions accepted the computerisation they had opposed.[57] He groups the reservation counters with the public call offices and the first electronic voting machines, used in a by-election in Kerala in 1982. Together, he writes, they 'changed the perception of policy makers and the general public about computers and their relevance to India'.[58]

## The Cray that did not come

The decade's best-known story about computers and national pride concerns a machine India did not get. It is usually told as a simple sequence: the United States refused India a Cray supercomputer, so India built its own. The record is more tangled.

In 1984 the Ministry of Human Resource Development gave the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) in Bangalore Rs 500 million, about $42 million at the rate Rajaraman uses of Rs 12 to the dollar, to set up a Supercomputer Education and Research Centre.[59] A national committee visited two companies in the United States and one in Japan and chose a Cray Y-MP. Cray signed a contract to install it at the institute by 1989, and 'Cray was confident of getting a licence to export the computer'. A team led by a US State Department official visited the institute and agreed conditions for its use.[60] Rajaraman, who chaired the centre from 1982 to 1994, 'participated in the entire exercise'. He records the outcome flatly: 'The US government did not give an export licence to Cray before the date specified in the contract'.[61] IISc cancelled the order and bought other machines instead, among them a Control Data Cyber 992 mainframe and, in 1992, an Indian-made parallel computer.[62] 'The denial of Cray was a blessing in disguise', Rajaraman concluded, 'as the money saved was used to buy state-of-the-art powerful parallel computers'.[63]

A second sale, for weather forecasting, was argued over in public in the United States. After the *Christian Science Monitor* reported the planned sale in December 1986, it printed on 8 January 1987 a reply from Dean Churchill, a meteorologist at the University of Washington who studied the monsoon. He thought that 'acquisition of a Cray or Cyber supercomputer might not solve the problem' of forecasting it, because of the lack of observations over the Indian Ocean and the gaps in scientific understanding.[64] That sale appears to have gone ahead in a smaller form: a Cray X-MP/14 was reportedly imported in October 1988 for the National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting.[65] If so, India was both denied one Cray and sold another.

In 1986 the Science Advisory Committee to the Prime Minister, chaired by the chemist C.N.R. Rao, set up a committee under Rajaraman on designing high-performance computers. It was formed, he writes, 'as a consequence of the difficulties encountered by India in buying supercomputers from the USA and Japan', and it proposed 'a mission mode project to build parallel computers with a speed of a giga-flop or above'.[66] A parallel computer divides a problem among many processors working at once; a gigaflop is a billion arithmetic operations on fractional numbers a second. In 1988 the DoE set up the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C-DAC) at Pune with Rs 300 million, about $22 million at that year's average rate, to build such machines. 'The project was successfully completed in 1991.'[67]

C-DAC was not alone. Between 1985 and 1992 the National Aeronautical Laboratory in Bangalore built FLOSOLVER for fluid dynamics, the Defence Research and Development Organisation built PACE in Hyderabad, and the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre built ANUPAM in Bombay.[68] The popular version, in which a single refusal leads straight to a single Indian supercomputer, owes much to later journalism; a 2017 web feature on C-DAC and PARAM, for example, says that the effort began when 'the US stopped the export of a Cray supercomputer'.[69] The defensible version is that American export controls, among other difficulties, pushed the government to fund parallel computing, and that several laboratories were already working on it.

What the episode produced, besides machines, was people. All these projects, Rajaraman writes, 'generated a large pool of engineers adept in designing and programming parallel computers'. Around ten IISc students earned doctorates in the field.[70] C-DAC also built a chip, called GIST, that let personal computers display most Indian scripts.[71]

## Laboratories as schools

The state projects of these years taught more people than they sold products. Most were paid for partly by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), and the pattern began with chip design. In 1984 the DoE used UNDP money, and a much larger sum of its own, to set up centres for computer-aided design (CAD) at two of the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs), at Kanpur and Bombay, at IISc and at Jadavpur University. 'Many scientists went abroad to get trained in the design and use of CAD tools', Rajaraman records, and came back to build tools of their own and teach short courses for industry. They had little choice: 'During the mid 80s the US Government had banned the export of CAD tools in electronics to even educational institutions in India.'[72] The same model ran the Knowledge Based Computer Systems programme from 1985 at five institutions, which worked on parallel computers, medical expert systems and the recognition of Indian scripts and speech.[73]

The longest-lived of these programmes was a network. In 1986 the DoE obtained four years of UNDP funding for Education and Research in Computer Networks (ERNET). It linked the five IITs, IISc, the National Centre for Software Technology (NCST) in Bombay and the DoE 'for exchange of email and other documents', and it was meant to train people in networking.[74] 'ERNET also brought the Internet to India under the leadership of S.Ramani of NCST, Mumbai,' Rajaraman writes, and after the project ended it became the internet provider for India's educational and research institutions.[75]

In all of them the money bought machines and sent scientists abroad, and the scientists came back, mostly to public laboratories and universities, and trained others. Rajaraman's summary is that by 1990 'a good foundation had been laid' in computer-aided design, parallel computing, networking and software engineering.[76] The published accounts do not say where the people they trained went next: into public research, to TI and the export firms, or abroad. The wider supply of engineers grew in the same years: recognised engineering and technology colleges nearly doubled in number between 1980-81 and 1990-91, by the statistics ministry's count.[77]

## The lobby and the department

The industry organised itself in these years. The Manufacturers' Association for Information Technology (MAIT) was founded in 1982, and the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the software firms' lobby, in 1988, with 38 members.[78] Rajaraman thought Nasscom 'fortunate in employing Dewang Mehta as its President in 1991. Mehta was a superb lobbyist for software companies'.[79]

The department the lobby dealt with changed character at the same time. From 1970 to 1988, Rajaraman writes, the DoE had been headed by technocrats and treated as a scientific department, like Atomic Energy and Space. In 1989 'A difference of opinion between two technocrats in the DoE' led to its being taken over by 'a generalist bureaucrat' of the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), and it ceased to count as a scientific department.[80] Yet he credits the generalist who followed. N. Vittal, an IAS officer who took over the DoE in 1990, was in his account 'dynamic and more business savvy' than his predecessors, and 'instrumental in setting up STPs and obtaining a host of fiscal concessions to the software industry by persuading the bureaucrats of the Ministry of Finance'.[81]

## The parks

The Software Technology Parks turned TI's privilege into a public service. Their stated purpose, in the World Bank's words, was to give small and medium firms 'an opportunity to enter the international software export market'.[82] Rajaraman describes what they offered: 'buildings, work stations and continuous uninterrupted power supply', and satellite links through which firms could 'develop software on the computers of their overseas customers from access terminals located in their respective premises'.[83] The World Bank adds a 'single window' for government clearance of imported equipment.[84] 'As the investment required to set up a company in STP was low,' Rajaraman writes, 'this initiative allowed many small entrepreneurs to enter the software services export business.'[85]

Rajaraman says 'The first STP was established in Bangalore in 1990'.[86] By the World Bank's dates Pune came first, in August 1990, two months ahead of Bangalore, followed by Bhubaneswar in December 1990, Hyderabad in February 1991, and Thiruvananthapuram, Gandhinagar and Noida in July 1991.[87] Software Technology Parks of India itself was registered on 5 June 1991.[88]

When the World Bank looked at the parks, about 160 companies had been approved to join, with estimated exports of $7.28 million by 1994, and it judged that 'it may be too early to judge' whether they would live up to expectations.[89] Rajaraman, writing with hindsight, put the parks beside the breaking of VSNL's monopoly and cheaper satellite links as the changes that 'allowed many entrepreneurs to enter the software export business'.[90]

## Half the target

By the end of the decade the export trade had grown fast from a small base. The World Bank's table of the industry shows software exports rising from $24 million in FY1985-86 to $128 million in FY1990-91, the year to March 1991.[91] These, like the other series of the period, count gross earnings, as Heeks warned, before the foreign exchange the trade spent abroad.[92] Even gross, the trade in FY1990-91 was about half of the $258 million that the government had expected by 1990 when it announced its policy in 1984.[93]

That gap is the best test of the claim that Rajiv Gandhi started India's information technology revolution. The strongest case for it is real. The policies of 1984 and 1986 came under his government, Rajaraman ties them to what he learned at the Asian Games, and the telephone reforms taken 'Under the leadership of Rajiv Gandhi', which created MTNL and VSNL, came in the same years as C-DOT's exchanges and the call offices that showed Indians what a microprocessor could do.[94] Roye's account of Bangalore in 2013 repeats the popular version, in which the business climate 'dramatically changed' once Rajiv Gandhi became prime minister.[95]

The case against is also documented. The export trade had begun in the 1970s, and the private computer industry that made his reforms possible had been opened by the policy of 1978, after IBM left. Heeks's series shows no sharp break at 1984. Exports grew by 99 per cent in 1982, 35 per cent in 1983 and 39 per cent in 1984, and by 9 per cent in 1985, 40 per cent in 1986 and 39 per cent in 1987.[96] The trade the policies counted as exports was still mostly programmers working abroad, and the share done in India rose only slowly. Sharma places 'The real breakthrough in software exports' after 1991, 'with the advent of the Software Technology Park scheme'.[97] The World Bank's reading is harsher still: the policies pushed firms to export, but in a 'short-term oriented' way, 'without any specific focus or direction on building sustainable competitive advantage and capabilities'.[98]

A third reading, which the evidence here supports best, puts the weight on infrastructure. The decisive changes were the ones that let work stay in India: TI's private link, negotiated in 1985, VSNL in 1986, the parks in 1990 and 1991. They came in pieces, across several governments, pushed by a technocratic department and then by a generalist one.[99] Rajiv Gandhi's government began that process. It did not finish it.

The permit, the old system of licences and obligations, was loosened between 1984 and 1986 but not abolished; a computer imported duty-free still had to earn back two and a half times its cost in dollars. The satellite, which could make an Indian office part of a foreign company's network, was in 1990 still a privilege granted case by case. By the time the first parks were registered, Rajiv Gandhi was out of office. His party lost the election of 1989, and the coalition that replaced it fell in 1990.[100] He was assassinated during the campaign for the election of 1991.[101] The government that took office after that election inherited a country, in Rajaraman's words, in 'a near default economic situation'.[102]
