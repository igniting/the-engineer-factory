# Chapter 10. Bangalore (1947-2010)

In the early 1970s the journalist Janardhan Roye used to see a young engineer named Vinay Deshpande working long hours in a basement. The basement belonged to the Classic Building on Richmond Road in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), and the firm that occupied it was called Processor Systems India. One evening Roye went down to see him. Deshpande was holding a soldering iron over a circuit board. He looked up, smiled, and explained that he was putting together 'hi-tech firmware, bound for the US'.[1]

Roye wrote the scene down forty years later, in 2013, in an article about how Bangalore had become a technology city. He dated it to about twelve years before the morning in 1985 when Texas Instruments' satellite equipment arrived at Sona Towers by bullock cart, the scene with which Chapter 8 opened. He thought Deshpande typical of the engineers of the 1970s, who worked quietly on their own or alongside the government's industrial, defence and science laboratories.[2] It is one man's memory, and it should be read as that. But it makes a point that the rest of the evidence supports. Bangalore was building electronics for American customers before any American software company had an office there.

Chapter 9 ended with a recruitment advertisement of 1991 that invited sixty-four young engineers to start their careers in Bangalore. This chapter asks why the software trade gathered in that city, and what it did to the city and to the people who lived there. The first answer is older than the trade. Bangalore was a city the Indian state had filled with engineers, laboratories and colleges in the forty years after independence. The second answer is a set of decisions by firms, some of them about engineers and some of them about landlords. The third is a policy that made the city's software parks a kind of export enclave, with their own power and their own links to the world. What the enclave did for the rest of Bangalore is the hardest question, and the evidence on it is thinner than it should be.

## The state's city

N.R. Narayana Murthy, the founder of Infosys, gave his own account of the city's origins in an oral history for the Computer History Museum, recorded in Bangalore in January 2020. 'Right from 1947 when we got independence,' he said, 'the government of India decided to locate its defense oriented high-tech companies in Bangalore, which was away from the border with Pakistan and with China.'[3] He added a reason of geography: the city lay about 250 miles from the Arabian Sea and about 200 from the Bay of Bengal, so that a plane coming to bomb it would be shot down first. That was why, in his telling, Jawaharlal Nehru and his cabinet made Bangalore a defence-oriented, high-technology place.[4] This is Murthy's recollection of the rationale, not a documented decision of the cabinet, and it is given here as his.

The institutions themselves are not in doubt. Murthy named the Electronics and Radar Development Establishment (LRDE), a defence laboratory; Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL); and Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL). His interviewer added the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), and Murthy agreed that space research came 'in the late '70s'.[5] A social scientist studying the city's poor, Supriya RoyChowdhury, describes the same city from another angle. In the decades after independence Bangalore was known as 'a garden city of retirees' bunglows [sic], and for a significant public sector presence', with central and state government enterprises set up from the early to mid-1950s and 'premier scientific research institutions' founded by the central government. 'The city's development in the first four decades was closely aligned with the development of the state sector,' she writes.[6]

The city's growth followed the same timetable. A research group at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) reports on its website that Bangalore's population grew by 76% between 1971 and 1981, a rise it attributes to the public-sector industries. Between 1991 and 2001, the decade of the software boom, growth was 38%, and the city's population in 2001 was 5.7 million.[7] The figures have not yet been checked against the census tables, but the direction matters. Software did not make Bangalore big. It arrived in a city that the public sector had already made large and full of engineers.

The private technology community grew inside that public one. Vijay Chandru, a former professor of computer science at IISc who later founded a life-sciences company, told Roye: 'Bangalore was a tech city well before the arrival of the MNCs (multi national corporations)'.[8] He called it 'the original Beantown', after Boston, and said that from the 1960s 'a host of engineers were engaged in creating hardware and software solutions' for avionics, defence, telecommunications and weather forecasting, mostly in the public sector.[9]

The education half of the story sits in the same institutions. When the Bombay vegetable-oil company Wipro decided to make computers in 1979, it went to IISc. Under a consultancy agreement signed in July 1979, Wipro's research team worked inside the institute's Digital Systems Lab, which was headed by N.J. Rao, and used a development system for Intel's new 8086 microprocessor that the institute had procured because, Rao later said, a company would have found it very difficult to import one.[10] IISc also had a Centre for Electronics Design and Technology, an Indo-Swiss collaboration that taught students to build microprocessor-based systems. Its staff designed the cabinet of Wipro's first minicomputer, the Series-86 of 1981. When the project ended, Wipro's new computer business moved to its first office at 45 Dickenson Road, Bangalore.[11] Several of Wipro's first employees had trained in the lab, and a run of IISc graduates joined the company in its first two years. Y. Narahari, then an IISc engineering student, turned down a Wipro offer in early 1982 to stay on for a master's degree.[12] A company that later sold programmers to the world began as a group of graduate engineers sitting in a university laboratory.

## A flat in Pune

The software trade did not start in Bangalore. Richard Heeks of the University of Manchester, who studied the industry from the late 1980s, wrote in 1998 that it began in Bombay (now Mumbai), 'in the business district around Nariman Point in the late 1970s'. When prices and commuting times rose, part of the industry moved north to the Santa Cruz Electronics Export Processing Zone (SEEPZ), and Bombay remained the centre of the trade 'until at least the mid-1980s'.[13] Then its firms began to run short of people, partly because programmers were leaving for jobs abroad, and the city's high prices and poor quality of life told against it. Firms, foreign investors and would-be founders began to compare Bombay with other places.[14]

Murthy's account of one such comparison turns on housing law. Infosys was founded in Pune in 1981. 'We pretty soon realized that Maharashtra was not the best place to have a startup,' he said, because of the state's tenancy laws.[15] A tenant who had lived in an apartment for seven years could not be made to leave and could claim it as his own. 'With the result, most landlords in Maharashtra refused to give an apartment on rent because of this fear. Unless you were a big company, unless you gave some big bank guarantee, etcetera, etcetera. But no startup could do that.'[16] The founders wanted a state where the law was 'fair between the landlord and the tenant', and Karnataka was one. So the choice narrowed to Chennai, in Tamil Nadu, or Bangalore.[17]

They chose Bangalore, Murthy said, 'because of the availability of engineering talent'. Part of the reason was the defence public sector. The other part was education. In the 1970s and 1980s, he said, 'when there were very few engineering colleges in the country, Karnataka had a disproportionately higher percentage of engineering talent and engineering students in the country.'[18] He gave no year for the move, and the date has not been found in a primary record. A later study put the regional supply in numbers. Writing in 2001 for a Stanford University conference on India's economy, the scholar of Silicon Valley AnnaLee Saxenian reported a finding that 60% of India's computer science graduates came from three southern states, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and that the industry was concentrated there 'largely because of their concentration of engineering manpower'.[19] The private engineering colleges described in Chapter 7 were part of that supply.

## Beer, climate and perception

A common answer to the question of why Bangalore won is that companies liked the weather. The evidence makes this partly true and mostly misleading. Heeks gave the most systematic answer, using a model of how software firms choose locations. He found three factors at work in the mid-1980s and a fourth that took over later.[20]

The first was labour. Bangalore was seen to have 'abundant labour that could be drawn from its long-standing research laboratories, educational institutes and public sector electronics firms', among them IISc and the Indian Institute of Management.[21] The second was quality of life. Bangalore had a reputation 'for a good climate and social life', and IT workers were quickly drawn into the city's social life and its 'pub culture'. Heeks recorded a semi-serious club of senior industry managers, the Beer-drinkers Association of Information Technology, whose members met to discuss strategy on condition that each drank at least six mugs of beer in the evening. Bombay, by contrast, had 'a long and troubled history of both communal and labour relations strife'.[22] The third was infrastructure. 'You could get across town in a matter of minutes in a car,' where Bombay's peak-hour traffic could take hours, and Bangalore promised plentiful power and water.[23]

Heeks added a caution that applies to every account of why industries cluster. From his interviews, 'the perception of these factors as much as their reality has coloured locational decisions'.[24] Climate, then, was real, but it was one element in a perceived quality of life. C.S. Murali of IISc, quoted by Roye in 2013, gave the weather more weight. He listed a liberalised economy, friendly state policies, a skilled English-speaking workforce and the public-sector giants, and then, 'above all', the city's 'inviting salubrious climate and cosmopolitan character'.[25] Murthy's account of the Infosys decision does not mention the weather at all.[26] Heeks and Murthy put engineers first, and even Murali's list begins with them. Climate was a perceived amenity that made the city easier to sell to engineers and managers. As the main reason for Bangalore's rise, the climate story is mostly false.

The infrastructure advantage was the least durable of the four. By 1998 a rival city was already advertising Bangalore's traffic as its weakness, as the section on Hyderabad below shows. By 2005 Saxenian reported that the concentration of technology production had brought 'rapidly rising wages and intensifying congestion', and that engineering salaries in Bangalore were 'now among the highest' in India, yet firms kept clustering there rather than moving to cheaper places.[27]

## Twenty-five rules

Heeks treated the arrival of Texas Instruments (TI) as 'a key event in Bangalore's history'. Once TI 'set up operations in 1986, it was the first proper offshore software facility, using satellite-based telecommunication links, and this was constantly reported in the home and international media'. The company was also 'known to have cut through many red tape barriers, particularly on imports'. From then on, he wrote, the Bangalore 'snowball effect' began.[28]

The dates in the sources differ by a year, and Chapter 6 promised to return to them. V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, records that the Department of Electronics negotiated with the Department of Telecommunications in 1985 to let TI set up a dedicated satellite link to its centre in Dallas.[29] Roye remembered the equipment arriving at Sona Towers in 1985.[30] Heeks dates TI's operations to 1986, and Saxenian dates its earth station to 1986.[31] The accounts fit together if the permission and the hardware came in 1985 and the link was working in 1986, but no document yet in hand gives the day the link went live, and the reconciliation is offered as the most likely reading rather than a settled fact.

Saxenian put a number on the red tape. Citing an interview given in 1996 by N. Seshagiri, a former additional secretary in the Department of Electronics, she wrote: 'When Texas Instruments set up the first earth station in Bangalore in 1986, for example, the process involved removing or breaking 25 different government rules.'[32] Without such links, she added, Indian firms 'had no alternative to providing contract programming on-site', at the customer's premises and usually in the United States.[33]

Other multinationals followed TI, and they explained themselves in terms of skill and risk. One American employer in Bangalore, interviewed in 1996 by the researcher Balaji Parthasarathy and quoted by Saxenian, said: 'we came here because of the skills. We expanded because of the skills.' The risks of distance, of the satellite link, the telephones and the flights, 'were offset by the costs'.[34] The head of another offshore development centre said that five years earlier he would have advised against trusting India with real development work 'because nobody's ever done it. It's not proven.' By the late 1990s he compared his centre's work with that of the company's headquarters in Silicon Valley: 'Technologically, there's zero difference.'[35] Both men are anonymous, and both speak through two layers of citation. They are evidence of what managers said, not of what the work was.

Some of the work was hard engineering. TI's Bangalore centre began by designing tools for chip design. Its engineers went on to design a digital signal processing chip, announced in 1997 under the code name Ankur, Sanskrit for seedling, which Rajaraman says was designed entirely in India and widely used in disk drives and instruments.[36] Saxenian noted that the chip had 'become a standard'.[37] Oracle opened a software development centre in Bangalore in 1994.[38]

By 1998 the snowball could be counted. A Dataquest survey of 558 Indian software companies, reproduced by Heeks, found 152 with headquarters in Bangalore, against 122 in Bombay, 93 in Madras (now Chennai), 86 in Delhi, 34 in Hyderabad and Secunderabad, 27 in Calcutta and 22 in Pune.[39] Heeks warned that the table understated Bombay, which was still home to several of the largest firms. But since the mid-1980s, when Bombay had led on numbers, Bangalore had grown at its expense and had come to be called India's 'Silicon Plateau'.[40]

## The parks and the bargain

The state that had built Bangalore's laboratories now built its export infrastructure too. Chapter 8 told how the Software Technology Parks (STPs) began. Bangalore's opened in October 1990, two months after Pune's and two months before Bhubaneswar's.[41] An STP, Saxenian explained, was 'like an export processing zone for software'. Firms in it received five years' exemption from tax, guaranteed access to high-speed satellite links and reliable electricity, ready office space, duty-free imports of equipment and permission for full foreign ownership, 'in exchange for a sizable export obligation'.[42]

The obligation is worth reading closely, because it states the bargain plainly. A park firm had to earn, net, 150% of the value of its imported hardware within four years, and 'a net amount equal to 150% of their wage bill on an annual basis'.[43] The second rule meant that a firm's Indian payroll had to be earned back one and a half times in exports every year. From January 1995 park firms could sell a quarter of their output in India, and from 1999 half.[44] The local directors of the parks were meant to act as 'friend, philosopher and guide' to the industry.[45] By 1998 the scheme had spread to 25 parks at various stages of planning, besides the government's own, and some were private ventures. The Information Technology Park in Bangalore, Saxenian noted, was a partnership of the Karnataka government, Tata Industries and a consortium of Singapore firms.[46]

The parks moved work from America to India. On Parthasarathy's figures, work done at clients' sites made up 90% of Indian software exports in 1990 and 58% in 1998.[47] Chapter 9 gave a different series, measured over slightly different years, and the two agree on the direction. By 1995, 435 companies were registered under the scheme, accounting for more than 16% of exports.[48]

Karnataka's numbers grew fastest. According to the state's development report of 2007, cited by RoyChowdhury, the number of software export companies in Karnataka rose from 13 to 1,154 between 1991 and 2003, and the state's software exports from Rs 5.6 crore (Rs 56 million, about $2.5 million) to Rs 12,350 crore (Rs 123.5 billion, about $2.65 billion).[49] In the second half of the 1990s Karnataka was, she writes, the only state to grow faster than 8% a year.[50]

## What the work paid

The economics behind the cluster were simple, and Saxenian set them out without ornament. 'India's greatest asset is a large, educated, English-speaking workforce that is willing to work at relatively low wages.' In 1994, she wrote, 'wages for software programmers and systems analysts in India were less than one-tenth of those for their US counterparts, and lower even than other developing countries like Mexico'.[51]

Low wages meant low revenue per worker. The industry employed about 180,000 people in 1998, and annual revenue per employee was $15,000 to $20,000, against about $100,000 per employee in Israel and Ireland.[52] Wages rose fast from their low base: the industry's association, Nasscom, estimated that software pay rose 21% a year in the late 1990s.[53] That eroded the cost advantage that had brought the work. Ashank Desai, chairman of Mastek, which Saxenian called a leading Indian software company, said in 1999 that Indian firms were finding it 'difficult for them to compete only on one differential', which was cost.[54] The largest firms, among them Wipro and Infosys, were seeking bigger contracts, often on a turnkey basis, in which the firm delivers a finished system. 'Rather than competing on the basis of hourly productivity,' Saxenian wrote, 'they aim to accumulate intellectual property.'[55]

How the older trade of sending programmers abroad had been priced is a point on which Saxenian's paper differs from the direct evidence. She wrote that in these contracts 'the amount of software code is specified in advance and revenue is earned per line of code'.[56] She gives no source for the sentence. A World Bank study of 1994 described programmers contracted out 'at an agreed hourly or monthly rate', and Heeks found that in 1988-1998 at least 65% of export contracts were for programming alone, billed on a 'time and materials' basis.[57] The weight of the evidence is with time. Saxenian's own description of how the programmers lived is better supported. Engineers who worked abroad for Indian firms were 'paid their salaries in rupees and provided with minimal allowances for housing and expenses', and some called the business 'resume selling'.[58]

For the programmers, the city was often a waiting room for America. 'Many thus aspire to work for foreign companies,' Saxenian wrote, 'not only for the relatively high wages but also for the opportunity be transferred overseas.'[59] In 1999 Indians received 47% of all H-1B temporary work visas issued by the United States, 55,047 people, and 195,083 between 1989 and 1999, a story Chapter 13 takes up.[60]

Behind them came a large training system. Saxenian, citing Nasscom, reported that Indian colleges and polytechnics trained more than 67,000 computer science professionals a year, and that 'another 200,000 individuals enroll annually in the private software training institutes that have mushroomed in India in the 1990s'.[61] The quality of that training is the subject of Chapter 12. What matters here is that Bangalore drew on it and on the colleges of the three southern states that supplied most of the country's computing graduates.

The gap between those who got the jobs and everyone else stayed wide. Writing in 2013, Roye reported that the average software professional in Bangalore, outside the call-centre trade, earned Rs 50,000 a month, which he put at about $1,000, more than eight times the state's monthly income per head of Rs 6,000.[62] He did not give a source for the figure, and it is a journalist's estimate. But it is consistent with the direction of Saxenian's evidence a decade earlier: fast-rising pay for a skilled minority in a poor state.

## Cyberabad

In November 1998 the chief minister of the neighbouring state of Andhra Pradesh, the 48-year-old Nara Chandrababu Naidu, inaugurated a building called CyberTowers near Madhapur, on the edge of Hyderabad. It was to be the centrepiece of a planned Hi-Tech City. 'Until now, the world has heard of the twin cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad,' he said. 'From today, the world will know of the triplet cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad-Cyberabad.'[63]

*Business Today*'s cover story the next month, written by an economist based in Hyderabad, set the challenge in context. Hyderabad had once been called the nation's second capital, and public-sector firms such as Bharat Heavy Electricals and the Electronics Corporation of India had set up there in the 1970s. But 'the late 1980s saw Bangalore overtaking Hyderabad' as South India's most dynamic and corporate-friendly city.[64] Naidu's answer was to court the largest names in software. 'When Naidu got Microsoft's CEO, Bill Gates, to locate its R&D centre at CyberTowers, he made sure no other company could ignore the city.'[65] Oracle, BaaN, Tata IBM, Satyam, Wipro and others were already there.[66] Microsoft's own announcements have not yet been read for this book, and the details of its centre wait for Phase 5.

Hyderabad's pitch was aimed at Bangalore's weak point. J.A. Chowdhury, a director of the Software Technology Parks of India and, in the magazine's words, a key player in Naidu's Project Cyberabad, put it bluntly: 'Hyderabad is preparing for the future. Other cities will be left behind when traffic-congestion and urban chaos overtakes them.'[67] He described the city as a 'knowledge hub'. It had the first Indian Institute of Information Technology, research institutes, defence laboratories and six universities, so that the supply of computing skills matched the demand.[68] 'Infotech professionals would like to live close to work so that they can work early morning or late into the night,' Chowdhury said, and the magazine added that housing could always be found within ten minutes of the workplace.[69] A Nasscom survey of IT professionals scored Hyderabad 3.40 out of 4 for the availability of qualified people and 3.20 for quality of life, but only average for climate, commuting and its educational facilities.[70]

The same article recorded doubts. Drinking water was short. Large parts of the city had 'grown haphazardly'. V.K. Bawa, one of its best-regarded urban planners, had drawn up a plan in the 1970s that no government had implemented, and he complained: 'The model of Hyderabad's development is regressive; it does not conform to modern concepts of urban growth.'[71] The whole project depended on one politician. If Naidu won the next state election, due in November 1999, the direction was clear. 'If he loses, the imponderables are many.'[72]

Saxenian saw in Naidu something larger than one city's ambition. While the central government had begun liberalisation, she wrote, 'the state governments have pioneered some of the most far-reaching policy innovations in the IT sector', and Naidu had made 'e-governance', the use of computers to deliver public services, his signature.[73] His campaign set off competition from his neighbours. Karnataka's new government announced plans to upgrade its 'overburdened roads', and Tamil Nadu and Kerala drew up IT policies of their own.[74] By 2001 software employment was concentrated, in her words, in Karnataka (Bangalore), Andhra Pradesh (Hyderabad), Tamil Nadu (Chennai), Maharashtra (Bombay) 'and areas surrounding Delhi'.[75] The histories of Pune, Chennai and the Delhi region in these years are not yet documented in the sources gathered for this book, and they are left for later chapters rather than guessed at here.

## The enclave

The sharpest contemporary warning about the cluster came from Saxenian herself, in a paper whose title asked whether Bangalore was 'the Silicon Valley of Asia'. Her answer was that the comparison was 'not only misleading but also distracts attention from the deeper challenges and opportunities that IT affords the Indian economy'.[76] Silicon Valley had grown with a large home market, an educated population and working infrastructure. Bangalore's future, she wrote, depended 'either on continued supplies of low cost of skill or on shifting into higher value-added activities', and comparisons with California 'remain premature at best'.[77]

The parks worked because they were sealed off from the country around them. 'Already most IT development occurs in STPs that are insulated from the day-to-day challenges of doing business in India by dedicated communications links, private power sources, and liberal rules for investment and taxation.'[78] The insulation was a response to real shortages. In 1997 India had 18.6 telephone lines per 1,000 people, against 55.7 in China, and a new connection took more than twelve months to arrive.[79] In 1997 there were 2.1 personal computers per 1,000 Indians.[80] Her 2005 paper made the same point about entrepreneurs: in India they relied 'on private telecommunications facilities and power supplies rather than on the nation's costly and unreliable infrastructure'.[81]

The cost of insulation, she argued, was disconnection. 'Professionals in IT enclaves could become better connected, both economically and socially, to distant regions than to the rest of the Indian economy.'[82] She cited warnings of 'Dutch Disease', in which dollars from a narrow sector, IT being under 1% of GDP, push up the rupee and hurt other industries.[83] And she put the paradox in one sentence: 'Indian workers today are contributing to the development of software to modernize foreign governments and corporations while their counterparts at home remain woefully backward.'[84] The current policy, she concluded, 'risks accelerating the growth of IT as a small, modern enclave in a poor and backward economy'.[85]

## The road not taken

Saxenian did not think the enclave was the only possible Bangalore. India, she wrote, 'already has the design capabilities' to build products of its own, visible in the chip-design work of the foreign development centres and in small local firms such as Silicon Automation Systems and Encore Software. 'However India lacks the environment needed to support experimentation with the application of these skills to new markets.'[86]

Her example was the Simputer, a very low-cost mobile personal computer priced at under Rs 9,000, about $200 by her conversion, built by a Bangalore team drawn from IISc's Department of Computer Science and Automation and Encore. It ran on the free Linux operating system, was 'designed to be open and modular', and carried a smart-card reader for financial transactions. The collaboration between a public institute and a private firm, she wrote, was 'rare in India'.[87] One of Encore's engineers was the same Vinay Deshpande whom Roye had seen with a soldering iron on Richmond Road in the 1970s.[88] Saxenian also drew on remarks by Ashok Jhunjhunwala at a Bangalore conference in December 1999, who argued that a pager sold in India for Rs 2,200 could be made locally for Rs 100 and so be affordable to a fifth of the population.[89]

Her prescription was a level field: 'IT products and services sold domestically' should 'enjoy the same tax benefits as those currently enjoyed by software export units'.[90] That sentence describes, from Bangalore, the logic this book has followed since the 1970s. A tax and infrastructure regime built to earn foreign exchange made it easier to sell software abroad than at home. The structure of the industry reinforced it. Ten firms earned more than half of all software exports, out of 826 exporting companies.[91] What became of the Simputer and of building for India is a question for Chapter 17.

## K.S. Garden

What did the boom do for the people of Bangalore who did not write software? The most careful answer in the sources comes from RoyChowdhury's study of six slums in the inner city, near Lalbagh, the commercial hub of the old city. She surveyed 300 households and held focus groups; the paper, published by Oxford University's Contemporary South Asian Studies Programme, is undated but was released around 2011.[92] She begins with the transformation. The city had 'become the icon of India's information technology revolution'. Real estate prices soared. The large campuses of the public-sector firms became 'shadows of a past': much of their land was put up for sale, units closed, and their permanent workforces were 'downsized and contractualized'.[93]

The new economy grew, she writes, 'through the expansion of capital intensive rather than labour intensive industries, as well as skill-based industries, which offered limited scope of employment, and only to technically skilled personnel'.[94] The poor found work in construction and in the export garment trade, at low wages, and in new service jobs: driving the cars and vans of call centres and outsourcing firms, guarding and cleaning malls and supermarkets, waiting at tables. Whether in factories or services, that work remained 'in the casual/contract mode with no security of service, insurance or pension'.[95] 'In Bangalore, nearly one million poor live in slums,' she reports, and about a third of slum dwellers lived below the poverty line, on less than Rs 2,500 a month, which she converts as $55.[96]

The IT economy reached one of her slums, K.S. Garden, in a particular way. It had the lowest poverty ratio of the six. Families with a member in a government job or a skilled trade could send their children to private English-medium schools or to computer training.[97] 'About 50 youths in the slum had managed to get some kind of computer training. Of these, some 25-30 women were working as receptionist in small offices, or showrooms, at cash counters in small super markets.'[98] They had left the low-paid work of their parents. But the gain was fragile. In the focus groups, 'even those with some computer training frequently remained unemployed or only partially employed', and where someone had found a foothold, 'a connection through family or network had worked in most cases'. RoyChowdhury's conclusion is plain: 'there had not been any systemic or policy based effort to incorporate the urban underclass into the city's growing economy'.[99] In the same slum, coolie labour and domestic service still accounted for 52.3% of households surveyed.[100]

These are collective voices, reported by a researcher. Roye's article of 2013 names two individuals, both young women from the villages and towns around the city. Anusaya, from Immadihalli village, worked at an ice-cream kiosk in a shopping mall in Whitefield, one of the new technology districts, for Rs 6,000 a month, about $100 by Roye's conversion. 'I work here in the day, go to evening college,' she told him. 'The money I earn meets some of my family expenses and funds college fees.'[101] Ramya Gowda, from Malur, worked at a doughnut counter. 'I got this job because I learnt English and have finished school,' she said. 'I'd like to study more, especially computers, and work in a big company like Yahoo!'[102] Neither was a software engineer. Both were working at the edge of the economy that software had built, and both described education, English first and computers next, as the way in.

Roye recorded what the corridors had replaced. The campuses of Whitefield, Electronic City and the Outer Ring Road stood largely on what had been farmland or swamp; many farmers on the city's edge had sold their land; the city had fewer trees, lakes and open spaces, and was by his account the third most expensive in India.[103] He ended the article in a bar on Lavelle Road, where a young software worker from Thanisandra told him: 'Seriously uncle, the city has arrived!'[104]

## Three readings

The evidence supports three readings of what happened in Bangalore, and they are not exclusive.

The first is a story of agglomeration. A city filled with public-sector engineers and laboratories attracted a few early movers, TI, Wipro and Infosys among them, and the cluster fed itself as employees left to found firms nearby.[105] On this reading Bangalore's software industry was the delayed return on forty years of state investment in defence, science and engineering education.

The second reading is Saxenian's, that Bangalore was not and could not quickly become a Silicon Valley. It depended on cheap skill and an export regime, and its best engineers were drawn towards America.[106] The evidence of TI's chip and the offshore centres' claims of parity shows the capability was real. The evidence of revenue per worker shows how little of it was being used.

The third reading is about the city. The industry created a well-paid professional economy with private power, private links and few ties to the city's poor, who found work driving its vans and guarding its malls.[107] The two urban histories that would test this reading most fully, Janaki Nair's history of twentieth-century Bangalore and James Heitzman's study of its planning in the IT era, have not yet been read for this book, and neither have the ethnographies of Bangalore's software workers. Until they are, the professionals' own account of their city is missing from this chapter.

## Inside the parks

What filled the parks at the end of the 1990s was routine work done at great volume. Saxenian listed it: hundreds of American and European companies outsourced 'coding, maintenance and Y2K solutions' to Indian firms, helped by the twelve-and-a-half-hour time difference that let Indian programmers work on American computers after their users had gone home.[108] To win that work at scale, the firms turned to certificates. By December 1999, 137 Indian companies held either ISO 9000 or Level 2 of the Capability Maturity Model, a scale for grading how disciplined a firm's software process is, and ten were rated at Level 5, the highest.[109] Saxenian noted that only six American companies held that rating.[110] The next chapter follows the two things that made the late 1990s the trade's boom years: a deadline at midnight on 31 December 1999, and the opening of American stock markets to Indian software firms.
