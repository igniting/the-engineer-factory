# Chapter 13. H-1B (1990-2015)

Some time in 1992 a group of Indian businessmen in Silicon Valley gathered to meet a visitor from Delhi, India's Secretary of Electronics. His flight was delayed. While they waited, according to the sociologist AnnaLee Saxenian, they 'began to informally share complaints about the difficulties of running a business'.[1] The waiting men were not newcomers. Among them were Suhas Patil, a former professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who had founded the chip company Cirrus Logic; Prabhu Goel, founder of Gateway Design Automation; and Kanwal Rekhi, who had started and run the networking firm Excelan until it merged with Novell.[2] Each had built a company in America, and, as Saxenian put it, had succeeded 'in spite of their lack of contacts or community support'.[3]

Patil put the discovery in a sentence that the *San Jose Mercury News* printed two years later: 'as individuals we are brilliant, but collectively we amounted to nothing'.[4] Satish Gupta, another early member, said much the same thing in the same article: 'When some of us started our businesses we had nobody we could turn to for help'.[5] Out of the delayed flight came a club with monthly meetings, called The Indus Entrepreneurs (TiE). Its founders chose 'Indus (rather than Indian)', Saxenian wrote, so that Pakistanis, Bangladeshis and Nepalese could belong too, though the Bay Area members were almost all Indian.[6] TiE's own history dates the founding to December 1992, which fits Saxenian's account.[7] The first annual conference, in 1994, 'attracted over 500 people'. In 1997 more than 800 came to two days of talks on how to start a company, raise money and take it public.[8]

The founders belonged to what Saxenian called 'an older generation of Indian immigrants'.[9] Two years before their meeting, Congress had passed a law that would bring a much larger and very different wave of Indian engineers: younger, trained in India, hired for a few years rather than for a career, and very often employed by firms whose real workforce was in India. That law created the H-1B visa. This chapter follows the visa from 1990 to 2015: how it worked, how Indian it became, who sponsored it, and what it did to the colleges and companies at home, to families in Indian cities, and to American workers at the other end of the trade.

## A visa with a cap

The Immigration Act of 1990 amended American immigration law to admit foreign nationals 'to work temporarily in the United States if hired by a U.S. employer to perform work in a specialty occupation', in the words of the Congressional Research Service (CRS), the research arm of the US Congress.[10] A specialty occupation was one that required 'theoretical and practical application of a body of highly specialized knowledge' and at least a bachelor's degree. The visa lasted up to three years and could be renewed for three more.[11] The number of new visas subject to the limit was capped at 65,000 a year. That cap, the CRS noted in 2025, 'is the same now as when it was established in 1990', although Congress raised it for a few years around the turn of the century.[12]

The law sat on top of an earlier one. Before 1965, national quotas favoured immigrants from countries already well represented in the United States. The 1965 reform replaced them with an overall cap and preferences for relatives and skilled workers.[13] Saxenian tied the Indian presence in Silicon Valley to that change: the great majority of the region's Indian workers in 1990 had arrived after 1965.[14] The 1990 Act added a temporary route for skilled workers, tied to a particular employer.

The CRS has described the programme as one that 'epitomizes the tensions' between American employers competing for talent worldwide and the possible harm to American workers of their doing so.[15] The protection for American workers was a promise on paper. Before petitioning for a worker, the employer filed a labour condition application with the Department of Labor, attesting that it would pay 'the greater of the actual wages paid to similar employees or the prevailing wages for that occupation' and that the working conditions of other employees would not suffer.[16] The CRS added a dry sentence that explains much of what followed: 'The LCA is a statement of intent rather than a documentation of actions taken'.[17] In 2007 the CRS described how the check worked in practice. The department reviewed each application 'for completeness and obvious inaccuracies', and only if someone later complained would it investigate.[18]

For its first few years the cap did not bind. A table compiled by the National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP), a pro-immigration policy group, from government data shows 48,600 new petitions issued against the 65,000 cap in fiscal 1992, and between 54,000 and 62,000 in each of the next four years.[19] The visa was not free to use. A 2011 survey by the GAO, the US government's audit office, cited by Khanna and Morales, put the legal and administrative cost of each H-1B hire at between $2,300 and $7,500.[20] In those early years it was not yet mainly a programmers' visa. The economists Gaurav Khanna and Nicolas Morales cite a 1992 government report finding that computing and related occupations accounted for only 11% of H-1 visas in 1989. By 1999, according to the immigration service, close to two-thirds of H-1B visas went to computer-related occupations.[21]

Chapter 9 described how the new visa changed the economics of sending Indian programmers to American client sites. 'In 1993, the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work,' Khanna and Morales write, 'as they were to be paid the prevailing market wage'.[22] The B-1 was a business visitor's visa, meant for meetings and negotiations, not for work. The H-1B carried a wage promise. On this reading, one reason the Indian firms moved more of their work offshore in the 1990s was that the visa made on-site labour dearer.[23] The gap between Indian and American pay was still enormous. Khanna and Morales cite a 1992 report that an Indian computer professional earned about $5,486 in 1989, at a time when Microsoft was offering software engineers $40,000 plus relocation and a green card.[24]

## Running out

The cap began to bind in the middle of the decade, as the American technology industry grew. The CRS recorded that the 65,000 limit 'was reached for the first time prior to the end of FY1997, with visa numbers running out by September 1997'. The next year's numbers ran out in May 1998.[25] Both Congress and the Federal Reserve, the CRS wrote, worried 'that a scarcity of labor could curtail the pace of economic growth'.[26]

Congress responded twice. In 1998 the American Competitiveness and Workforce Improvement Act raised the cap to 115,000 for fiscal 1999 and 2000, but 'that annual ceiling of 115,000 visas was reached months before FY1999 and FY2000 ended'.[27] The government's own counting failed under the strain. The immigration service 'admitted in autumn 1999 that thousands of H-1B visas beyond the 115,000 ceiling were approved in FY1999', and hired auditors to find out how.[28] In 2000 Congress raised the ceiling again, 'to 195,000 for three years', and expanded training programmes for American workers.[29]

The 1998 law also tried to make the visa pay for its own side effects. By 2013 an employer filing a petition paid a base fee of $325, a $500 fee for fraud prevention, and in many cases $1,500 under the 1998 Act 'to fund the training of United States citizens'.[30] Firms that relied heavily on the visa faced extra duties. An 'H-1B dependent' employer, generally one with at least 15% of its workforce on H-1Bs, had to attest that it had tried to recruit American workers and had not laid any off in the 90 days before or after hiring a visa holder.[31]

Then the market turned. Petitions approved for H-1B workers, counting both new hires and extensions, peaked at 331,206 in fiscal 2001 and fell to 197,537 the following year.[32] The CRS observed that 'the economic downturn in the information technology sector appeared to have diminished demand'.[33] NFAP's table shows how far below the raised ceiling the new petitions fell: 163,600 in fiscal 2001, then 79,100 in 2002 and 78,000 in 2003, against a cap of 195,000.[34] For two years more than half the visas went unused.

The scarcity returned as soon as the ceiling dropped. 'When the H-1B annual numerical limits reverted to 65,000 in FY2005,' the CRS wrote, 'that limit was reached on the first day'.[35] A separate allowance of 20,000 visas for holders of American master's degrees had been added in 2004.[36] In April 2007 the immigration service received about 119,193 petitions subject to the fiscal 2008 cap on the first two days it accepted them, and chose among them by a computer-generated random draw.[37] From the mid-2000s, the visa was allocated by lottery.[38] A young engineer's chance of an American job now depended partly on luck.

## How Indian the visa became

Indians did not dominate the visa at first. Saxenian, writing in 2001 and citing a 2000 study of the visa by Lowell, reported that the number of Indian H-1B holders grew steadily from 1989, became the largest national group in 1994, doubled by 1996 and quintupled by 1999. In 1999 Indians received 47% of all H-1B visas issued, 55,047 people; the next largest groups, from Britain and China, had 6% or less each.[39]

The share kept rising, but the published figures measure different things and must be read with their labels. For fiscal 2005 the CRS reported that India was 'the leading country of origin for newly arriving H-1B workers, comprising almost half (49.0%) of all of the new arrivals', with China a distant second at 9.16%.[40] The same report repeated an undated government estimate that 'nearly three-fourths of all of the systems analysts and programmers are from India'.[41] By 2014, according to immigration service data cited by Khanna and Morales, about 70% of H-1B visas went to Indians; China, the next largest, had received only 8% in 2012.[42] A visa that Congress had written for any skilled foreigner had become, in its largest use, a channel between Indian engineering colleges and American information technology.

Who sponsored those visas is a separate question, and the answer is less simple than the common belief that the visas mostly went to Indian outsourcing firms. Khanna and Morales tabulated approvals by employer from immigration service reports for 2012 and 2013. In 2013 Infosys led with 6,298, followed by Tata Consultancy Services with 6,258 and Cognizant with 5,186. Then came Accenture (3,346), Wipro (2,644), HCL Technologies (1,766), IBM's Indian subsidiary (1,624), Mahindra Satyam (1,589), Larsen & Toubro Infotech (1,580), Deloitte (1,491) and iGate, which had absorbed Patni (1,157). The year before, Cognizant had topped the list with 9,281.[43]

The authors drew the conclusion from the table: '10 out of the top 11 H-1B firms have Indians as their primary employment base'.[44] Three of those ten were not Indian companies. Cognizant was headquartered in the United States, Accenture in the Bahamas and IBM in the United States, but their main workforce, like that of Infosys or Wipro, was in India. Only Deloitte, tenth on the list, employed mainly Americans.[45] The largest sponsors, in other words, were firms built on the offshore services model, whoever owned them. Whether these firms took most of all H-1B approvals cannot be settled from these sources, because they give no matching totals for initial and continuing petitions in those years.

The sponsors were also only part of the visa's use. The CRS noted that since fiscal 2000 most H-1B workers had been exempt from the cap, because they were extending an existing status or worked for universities and research bodies.[46] The lottery decided who got in. Extensions, and for some a pending application for permanent residence, decided how long they stayed.[47]

## Who went, and what they had learned

Most temporary visa workers had been educated before they arrived. A 2010 national survey of American college graduates, tabulated by Khanna and Morales, found that among foreign-born workers on temporary work visas whose highest degree was a bachelor's, 94% had earned it abroad. Among those with a master's, 77% had.[48] Since most H-1B holders were Indian, most of that education had been done in Indian colleges, without an American degree in between.

That was a change from the earlier generation. The Indians already in Silicon Valley in 1990, Saxenian found, had mostly come for graduate study. The 1990 census counted 28,520 Indians in the region's workforce, 98% of them immigrants; 87% of the Indians in its high-technology industries had arrived after 1970 and 60% after 1980.[49] National Science Foundation figures she cited show that 79% of Indians who received American science and engineering doctorates in 1990-91 were still working in the United States in 1995.[50] Many were graduates of 'the prestigious Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) or Indian Institutes of Science (IISs)', she wrote, and were united by professional as well as ethnic ties; a sizable group had grown up in Africa and never lived in India.[51]

The IITs had been feeding America for decades. The historian Ross Bassett reports a study of IIT Bombay showing that of its electrical engineering graduates of 1973-77, 'slightly over 40 percent ended up abroad, the vast majority in the United States'.[52] The pattern outlasted the period of this chapter. A study published in 2023 by Prithwiraj Choudhury, Ina Ganguli and Patrick Gaulé, which followed students who sat the IIT entrance examination, found that 'among the top 1,000 scorers on the exam, 36% have migrated abroad, rising to 62% for the top 100 scorers'.[53]

The H-1B also gave Indian students a reason to take an American degree first. The 2004 allowance of 20,000 extra visas for holders of American master's degrees, Khanna and Morales note, 'may incentivize students to obtain their degrees in US universities'.[54] The scale of that student route in the 1990s and 2000s is not documented in the sources for this chapter. The figures in hand begin only in 2017.

The two routes together supplied much of America's computing workforce. A national survey of college graduates showed that 55% of foreigners working in computing fields in 2003 had arrived on a temporary work visa or a student visa, and a Department of Commerce estimate for the late 1990s was that 28% of programmer jobs went to H-1B holders.[55] What the two routes added up to can also be measured over time. In 1990 immigrants (defined as people born abroad who arrived after the age of 18) made up 11.06% of computer scientists in the United States with a bachelor's or master's degree. By 2000 they were 18.59%, and by 2010 27.82%.[56]

## The pull on the colleges

The most important effect of the visa may have been on the people who never used it. Khanna and Morales built an economic model of students and firms in both countries to test a hypothesis: 'that Indian students enrolled in engineering schools to gain employment in the rapidly growing US IT industry via the H-1B visa program. Those who could not join the US workforce, due to the H-1B cap, remained in India, enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector'.[57] Those who went and came back when their visas expired brought skills and technology with them.[58] As the authors put it, workers who could not get a visa 'or return to India after their visa expires are a ready supply of labor for firms in India'.[59]

Their model's answer is that the visa 'induced Indians to switch to computer science (CS) occupations, increasing the CS workforce in India by 4.9% in 2010'.[60] The same model finds a cost on the other side. The visa 'induced US workers to switch to non-CS occupations, reducing the US native CS workforce by 7%'.[61] Indian IT output was 1.8% higher, and the combined income of the two countries 1.5% higher, than it would have been without the programme.[62] These are estimates from a calibrated model, not counts. They describe a world without the H-1B that never existed. What they offer is a mechanism: the American prospect pulled Indian students towards engineering and computing, and the cap kept most of them at home, where the Indian firms hired them.

Over the same years, according to Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, information technology and business process outsourcing grew from 1.2% of India's gross domestic product in 1998 to 7.5% in 2012.[63] That mechanism fits what Chapter 12 found in the colleges. Khanna and Morales describe the workforce it produced, using industry data. In 2002 Indian software engineers had 'a median age of 26.5 years, and 58% of them had less than three years of experience', according to Nasscom, the industry's lobby group. A Nasscom-Hewitt survey 'found that 88% of firms visited engineering colleges to recruit, and 47% recruited only there'.[64] The pull worked against research. Citing a 2002 study by Arora and Athreye, Khanna and Morales report that the number of engineering doctorates in India fell from about 675 in 1987 to 375 in 1995.[65] The best students were drawn towards early, well-paid jobs in industry, at home or abroad, rather than towards the laboratories.

## Silicon Valley's Indians

By 2000, 53% of Silicon Valley's scientists and engineers were foreign-born, Saxenian reported, and about 20,000 of them were Indian.[66] Many had found their careers stopped short. 'Many Indian engineers complained about a glass ceiling in the region's established companies, and responded by starting their own businesses', she wrote.[67] She quoted Prabhat Andleigh, as reported by the *Los Angeles Times* in 1996: 'Why do you think there are so many Indian entrepreneurs in Silicon Valley? Because they know that sooner or later they will be held back'.[68]

The first association was a professional one. The Silicon Valley Indian Professionals Association (SIPA) was founded by Prakash Chandra, whom Saxenian described as 'a typical Silicon Valley engineer'. He started at the chipmaker Advanced Micro Devices in 1984 and worked at Intel, the start-up MIPS and Cadence.[69] According to Chandra, 'many Indians didn't see a career path beyond what they were doing', and SIPA's early meetings dealt with individual career strategy.[70] Visits by Indian government delegations in the early 1990s pushed SIPA to try to 'fill the information gap' between the two countries, and by 1999 it had about 1,000 members.[71] Chandra himself went back to India in 1992 to work for Wellfleet, which became Bay Networks.[72] TiE, the club born of the delayed flight, began the same year and turned to mentoring and, through its members, investing in younger South Asian founders.[73]

How many companies these engineers started is one of the most repeated and least examined numbers in the story. Saxenian's 1999 count used a Dun & Bradstreet database of 11,443 technology firms founded in Silicon Valley between 1980 and 1998, and identified as 'immigrant-run' all companies whose chief executives had Chinese or Indian surnames.[74] By that measure 774 firms, 7%, were run by Indians, with $3.6 billion in sales and 16,598 jobs in 1998.[75] The Indian share of new start-ups rose from 3% in 1980-84 to 9% in 1995-98.[76] She was explicit that this was a proxy, 'using immigrant-run businesses as a proxy for immigrant-founded businesses', which missed founders who had handed the chief executive's job to someone else.[77] Her interviews suggested that this was common, because venture capital 'has often been tied to the requirement that non-Asian senior executives be hired'.[78] A later study by Wadhwa and colleagues produced the higher and more often quoted figure of 15.5%, but it counted any immigrant founder over 1995-2005, which is a different measure.[79] Any share of Indian start-ups is only as good as its definition.

## Drain, then circulation

Saxenian's phrase 'brain circulation' is often read back into the 1990s. Her own evidence for India in that decade points the other way.

The exception she described in detail was Radha Basu. Basu left 'her conservative South Indian family' for graduate study in computer science at the University of Southern California in the early 1970s and joined Hewlett-Packard.[80] In the mid-1980s, after the Indian government invited her to set up one of the country's first foreign software subsidiaries, she spent four years building HP's centre in Bangalore. When she went back to California in 1989 it employed 400 people.[81] The first office, she told Saxenian in 1997, 'consisted of a telex machine on her dining room table'.[82] Customs officials could not accept that software could be exported over a satellite link without something physical to inspect. For five years, Saxenian recorded, Basu copied HP's data onto tapes and shipped them to the United States so that the exports could be registered.[83] Doing business in India, she told Saxenian, needed patience: 'You can't just fly in and out and stay in a five-star hotel and expect to get things done like you can elsewhere'.[84] When officials talked of a 'single window of clearance' for foreign trade, she would joke: 'where is the window?'[85]

Basu was unusual. Saxenian wrote in 1999 that 'few Indian engineers choose to live and work permanently in India'. Unlike the Taiwanese, Indian engineers who went home 'typically do so on a temporary basis'.[86] Young engineers in India preferred American multinationals as 'a ticket to Silicon Valley', and Bangalore software companies reported turnover of 20-30% a year, 'primarily because so many workers jump at the first opportunity to emigrate'. On balance, 'the "brain drain" of skilled workers to the United States continued unabated throughout the 1990s'.[87] The engineers who stayed in America played what she called 'an important, but largely arm's-length, role', persuading their employers to send work to India.[88] The reason was cost. Programmers' wages were 'ten times lower in India', and the fully loaded cost of an engineer there was 35-40% of the American figure.[89] Much of the work was still what the trade called body shopping, which Saxenian defined narrowly as programming at the customer's site on 'time and material' contracts billed by the programmer-hour. In 1990 on-site services were about 90% of the value of Indian software exports, and in 1995 still 61%.[90] At the end of the decade much of the work in India was on the year 2000 problem, which she described as work that 'generates significant exports but no new intellectual property'. One observer, quoted by her from *Forbes* in 1997, called it 'Grunt work by Indians and thinking by the Yankees'.[91] The big Indian firms, she judged, 'have been so profitable playing the wage gap that they have had few incentives to address higher value-added segments of the market'.[92]

Writing in 2005, she repeated the point: 'few U.S.-educated emigrants returned to either India or China during the 1980s and 1990s'. It was the technology recession that 'triggered an upsurge in cross-regional entrepreneurship'.[93] The comparison she drew was with Taiwan and Israel. There, returning immigrants had carried the Silicon Valley model of venture capital home from the early 1980s, and technologists had 'returned by the thousands'.[94] Israeli entrepreneurs, she wrote, built on the country's military research in internet security and telecommunications, while Indian entrepreneurs mobilised 'thousands of underemployed English-speaking Indian engineers to provide software development services for American corporations'.[95] India's diaspora had sent work home rather than companies. Inside India, Saxenian found, the 1990s turnover of 20-30% a year did not spread ideas between firms as it did in Silicon Valley. It mainly pushed up wages, by 25-40% a year. Learning happened 'primarily within the firm and through long-distance relationships', not with local universities or laboratories, even in Bangalore, home of the Indian Institute of Science.[96]

By the mid-2000s the trade itself was changing who travelled. In Chennai, the anthropologists Chris Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan followed a group of engineers. Of their twenty younger software engineers, seven had been or were on overseas assignments in 2005, but only two had spent more than a year in the United States; 'the rest, as is now increasingly common, were away for only a few months'.[97] They argued that 'the very logic of "offshoring"' meant that fewer people moved 'as the work travels to the site of labor, instead of the reverse'.[98] For many engineers the American posting became a stint in a career spent in India.

At the Chennai firm they studied, which they called ICS, on-site assignments 'lasting several months or even a year or two' were 'a normal part of working life'.[99] Their individual stories show the range. Ravi (a pseudonym, as are all names in the study), a project manager of 33 at a large Chennai firm, had spent a year on-site in England, 'where he earned plenty of money and got "good exposure"', but came home to be with his elderly father and had no wish to live abroad.[100] Balaram, 37, left the same company in 1995 for Australia, worked there in information technology for eight years, and came back in 2003 because 'the work available in Chennai is far more interesting and challenging, and the industry's growth prospects are much better'.[101] Anuradha, a project manager in her early thirties with a degree in electrical and electronic engineering from a college in Coimbatore, had done on-site work in Britain and America and worked for ICS in America from 2000 to 2002. She had made it a condition of her marriage that she and her husband would come back to Chennai after making some money.[102] With a young daughter, she now refused on-site assignments, and said she did not need any more 'exposure'.[103]

## The parents

Migration also reached into Indian homes. Fuller and Narasimhan found that Chennai was 'said to be full of lonely old folk whose sons and daughters send them dollars, but only ever come on short visits'. Other parents visited their children so often that they became 'bored, exploited childminders marooned in American suburbia under "house arrest", to quote one elderly grandfather'.[104] One informant told them that overseas migration 'damages the proper reciprocal relationship between parents and children in a way that migration within India does not'.[105] Children abroad could send money. They could not provide the daily care that sons and daughters were expected to give their parents.[106]

## The B-1 channel

The H-1B was not the only door. The business visitor's visa that Khanna and Morales described as giving way to the H-1B in 1993 was still in use two decades later. The clearest record of how comes from a legal settlement, and it must be read with care.

On 30 October 2013 Infosys filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission a settlement agreement with the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Texas, the State Department and the Department of Homeland Security. Under it, 'Infosys agrees to pay a full settlement amount of $34,000,000'.[107] The agreement separates what both sides accepted from what the government alleged.

Both sides accepted that 'Infosys failed to maintain accurate I-9 records for many of its foreign nationals in the United States in 2010 and 2011', the forms that record whether an employee may legally work. This included 'a widespread failure to update and re-verify the employment authorization status of a substantial percentage of its foreign national employees'.[108]

The government alleged more, and Infosys denied it. The allegation was that Infosys 'knowingly and unlawfully used B-1 visa holders to perform skilled labor' in jobs that would otherwise have gone to American citizens or to legitimate H-1B holders, for purposes that included 'increasing profits, minimizing costs of securing visas, increasing flexibility of employee movement'.[109] According to the government, invitation letters described trips as 'customer discussions and related business development activities' when the work included 'coding and programming'.[110] It quoted a 'Do's and Don'ts' memorandum given to B-1 travellers: 'Do not mention activities like implementation, design & testing, consulting, etc., which sound like work.' Another line read: 'Please do not mention anything about contract rates'.[111] The government alleged that workers were told to give consular officers the destination named in the labour condition application when they were going elsewhere. In one case, it said, a worker was told to say Seattle when his real destination was Bentonville, Arkansas.[112] And it alleged that on 29 September 2009 Infosys staff were directed by email to convert a time-and-materials contract, which disclosed the names and billing rates of everyone on a project, into a fixed-price contract, which disclosed only the total, in a way that hid the B-1 holders' skilled work.[113]

Infosys's answer, in the same document, was that its use of B-1 visas 'was for legitimate business purposes and not in any way to circumvent the requirements of the H-1B program'. It said the memorandum 'was not intended to deceive U.S. officials' and had been withdrawn in 2011, and that contract changes were 'made to comply with internal Company guidelines and rules regarding billing practices'.[114] Infosys 'denies any allegations of civil or criminal wrongdoing and/or liability'.[115] The settlement resolved the claims without resolving that dispute.

Two points can be made safely. The first is that the older on-site channel had not closed when the H-1B opened, and the agreed facts show that one of the largest sponsors had not kept accurate track of whether its foreign employees in America were authorised to work. The second concerns the contract forms. Whatever happened in that 2009 email exchange, the government's description of the two forms is accurate. A time-and-materials contract names the people and their rates. A fixed-price contract names only the price. Earlier chapters showed the Indian industry growing up on the first kind. The difference between them, who is visible and who captures the savings, returns in the chapters on artificial intelligence.

## Ninety days in Orlando

The other side of the trade has a name and a voice. Leo Perrero had a bachelor's degree in information technology and, by his account, 'twenty years of hard work' in the field.[116] He had worked for Disney in Orlando 'in some capacity for well over ten years', on the point-of-sale system through which visitors paid for food and merchandise.[117] In the holiday season of 2014 he received a meeting invitation from a senior executive and, he later told the Senate Judiciary Committee, his mind 'buzzed with thoughts of a promotion or a bonus'. He walked into a small conference room with about two dozen colleagues.[118] He recalled the executive's words: 'All of you in this room will be losing your jobs in the next 90 days. Your jobs have been given over to a foreign workforce.' Severance pay depended on training their replacements.[119] Later that day, he recalled, he had to tell his children at a church pumpkin sale that the family could not buy one.[120] Only '4 people would be directly rehired by Disney of the hundreds that were lost'.[121]

He described the handover in three stages. In the first, the newcomers sat beside the Disney staff and watched them work. 'Everything was recorded with audio and video. Then the very next day the recordings were put down on paper and read right back to us'.[122] In the second the incumbents worked 'side by side with our replacements'. In the third they watched 'the foreign workers completely take over our jobs'.[123] On 31 January 2015 they handed in their badges and laptops. His desk, chair and computer, he said, went to a worker 'who was just flown in to America weeks before'.[124] Of the severance he had accepted, he said: 'Sadly, I choose the money over America'.[125]

His is one witness's account, given in 2016 to a hearing on reform, and it should be read as such. He named neither the contractor that supplied the replacements nor their nationality, and this chapter does not supply them. His complaint was aimed at two things together: visa holders 'physically being flown in from foreign countries' and 'the growing use of foreign remote offshore workers'.[126] To the worker in the conference room, the H-1B and the offshore centre were one system. The firms at the top of the sponsor tables earlier in this chapter did indeed run both.

What he described in the first stage was a transfer of knowledge in the most literal sense. The tacit work of a team, what it knew but had never written down, was recorded, transcribed and read back, so that people paid less could do it, first beside the old staff and then without them. A quarter of a century of the visa had refined a method for moving work from one set of people to another by writing it down. A decade later, written records of how work is done would become the raw material for machines.

Perrero's complaint was not new. The next chapter goes back to 2002, to the first American backlash against offshoring, and to the scale the Indian firms reached in spite of it.
