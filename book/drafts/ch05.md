# Chapter 5. The year IBM left (1973-1984)

Some time in 1977, a group of senior executives from International Business Machines (IBM) went to see India's new minister of industry. They had already been to the top. The prime minister, Morarji Desai, had heard them out and told them to see the minister, George Fernandes.[1] The meeting, as the science journalist Dinesh C. Sharma later wrote, turned out to be decisive.[2]

'IBM was very cocky,' Fernandes told Sharma in 2006, in the only account of the meeting that survives. The executives, he recalled, told him they had refused to accept what the French president, General Charles de Gaulle, had told them about diluting their equity. His reply, as he remembered it: 'if you think the General succumbed to you, I am telling you that I am not succumbing to you. You get out.' And then, for emphasis, he added that yes, he had said it: you get out.[3]

On 1 June 1978 IBM closed its Indian operations.[4] The company that had brought most of India's computers into the country, rented them out, maintained them and trained the people who ran them was gone.

What India lost, and what it gained, was not what either side expected. A rule written to save foreign exchange pushed out the largest foreign computer firm. A licensing policy then let a handful of small Indian companies build computers from imported chips. Because those companies were not allowed to import software, they wrote their own, and the engineers who did that work became, by the account of the period's leading participant-historian, the skilled programmers India later sold to the world. The hardware itself did not last.

## The renting business

IBM had sold its machines in India since 1950.[5] From 1965 it built a business on key punches made in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) and exported, whose foreign-exchange earnings paid for used IBM 1401 computers, which IBM refurbished, repainted and rented to Indian customers with maintenance and help with software included.[6] The rents drew charges of profiteering, but V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, also credits IBM with training a whole generation of India's maintenance engineers and programmers (Chapter 3).[7] The model gave IBM about two-thirds of the 217 computers counted in India in 1974, and by Sharma's later estimate about 80 per cent of the market.[8]

The same model made IBM a target. Sharma calls it IBM's 'As is' policy, and judges that it 'kept the technology in Indian market one or two generations behind the latest'.[9] He dates the government's pressure to 1967, when the Electronics Committee under the physicist Vikram Sarabhai wanted IBM to stop importing old machines and leasing them to government departments at inflated rents.[10]

## Forty per cent

The legal hinge was a law about money. In 1973 India amended the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA). Its purpose, in Rajaraman's words, was 'to conserve foreign exchange', and under it foreign companies 'except those considered essential were to dilute equity to 40% and take an Indian partner'.[11] Any company with more than 40 per cent foreign equity had to obtain fresh approval from the Reserve Bank of India to continue operating.[12] FERA was one more of the devices, like import licences and export obligations, by which the Indian state rationed its dollars.

Two years later Parliament added a charge of its own. After the Comptroller and Auditor General raised questions about IBM's dealings with the Railways, the Public Accounts Committee of Parliament, chaired by Hirendra Nath Mukherjee, opened 'a full-scale enquiry into the functioning of IBM' in November 1975.[13] The committee found, as Sharma reports it, that IBM had 'defrauded the country of enormous revenues by resorting to various unfair practices like transfer pricing under the garb of inter-company billing system, misuse of import entitlements'.[14] Transfer pricing here meant setting the price at which IBM's own affiliates sold machines to its Indian arm, which decided how much foreign exchange left the country. 'IBM was found to dump used and obsolete mainframe computers in India at inflated prices,' Sharma writes of the same enquiry.[15]

IBM bargained. Rajaraman says it first refused to dilute, 'as it was not its global policy'. It then proposed a manufacturing unit, wholly owned and wholly for export, whose earnings would pay for computers imported for sale in India, and offered to invest 26 per cent of its profit in a research centre in India.[16] Sharma describes the offer in more detail. IBM would split its Indian business in two. Card manufacture and the data centres would go into one company with 60 per cent Indian equity, as FERA required; the export factory would be the other, fully owned. 'But it was not willing to dilute its equity in its marketing and maintenance business', which Sharma calls its core business.[17]

IBM, Rajaraman writes, was 'very confident that it would be considered "essential"'. It had over 150 installations in India, at the Railways, the Planning Commission, textile and petrochemical companies, and many research laboratories and educational institutions. 'IBM's data centres were widely used and its service was excellent'.[18] The Department of Electronics (DoE) still hoped for a negotiated solution. Then, in March 1977, the government changed.[19]

IBM's British rival made the other choice. ICL diluted its equity, formed International Computer India Manufacturing Ltd (ICIM) with an Indian partner, and in 1977 began making a computer series called the ICL 2900 in India.[20] It shows that a foreign computer firm could stay on FERA's terms, and so that leaving was at least partly IBM's choice. It does not show whether anyone in Delhi wanted IBM to stay.

## The minister

The 1977 election ended thirty years of continuous Congress rule. A coalition led by Morarji Desai took office.[21] George Fernandes was made minister of communications and moved very soon afterwards to industry, replacing Brij Lal Verma.[22]

He brought a history with him. 'As a trade Union leader in Bombay,' Sharma writes, 'Fernandes had led anti-computer agitation of workers in the 1960s'.[23] The man who now held IBM's file had been on the other side of the union campaigns against office computers. During the election campaign he had said that India did not need 'foreign technology for making brassieres and tooth paste'.[24] He was also pressing Coca-Cola to share its secret formula if it wanted to stay in India.[25]

In his 2006 interview Fernandes gave two reasons for his stand. One was IBM's attitude in the meeting. The other was that, in his view, IBM's computers 'were taking away jobs of Indian workers'.[26] The second reason ties the exit to the long Indian argument about automation and work.

Sharma's own verdict is careful. The audit, the parliamentary enquiry, the FERA rules and pressure from the Department of Electronics had already made it hard for IBM to stay, and Fernandes's arrival was the last nail in the coffin. 'The file on IBM and Coca Cola came to him as FERA cases, and he took the decision based on recommendations of the FERA committee. It was well within the purview of policy and law, but helped Fernandes score a political brownie point'.[27] What the minister did, in Sharma's view, was to shut the window in which IBM and Coca-Cola might have renegotiated.[28] By 1978 Coca-Cola, IBM, Mobil and Kodak had quit India or applied to do so.[29]

## Who left whom

Whether IBM was thrown out or chose to go has been argued ever since. Rajaraman's account is that it chose to leave rather than give up control: 'After prolonged negotiations the government was unwilling to relax FERA for IBM. Consequently IBM decided to withdraw from India in 1978'.[30] Sharma's is that FERA only eased an exit whose real cause was a power struggle between a multinational and the government.[31] A report written at the time points the same way. In April 1978 the West German trade weekly *Computerwoche* wrote that specialists thought the Electronics Commission might have brought on the confrontation deliberately, accusing IBM of making excessive profits from outdated machines and of largely blocking the local development of new systems. IBM replied that it had proposed replacing its 1400 series in 1970, and again in 1972, and that the government had taken up neither plan.[32]

Shiv Nadar, a founder of one of the Indian firms that grew in IBM's absence, gave a third account to Knowledge at Wharton in 2006. When the interviewer began to say that Fernandes had evicted IBM in 1977, Nadar cut in: 'IBM wasn't evicted. The real story was different.' In his account the government had ruled that if a product was made in India, its source code had to reside in the country. Equity dilution, he said, did not hurt companies much, but the source-code requirement did. 'That was the main reason why IBM decided to leave India, as did Coca-Cola'.[33] No other source mentions such a rule for computers; Coca-Cola's secret formula was certainly an issue, and Nadar may have run the IBM and Coca-Cola cases together.[34]

The fourth account is expulsion. The historian Dwaipayan Banerjee's 2026 history of Indian computing is described by its publisher as treating IBM's departure as a decisive expulsion, one act in a project of technological independence, and a reviewer summarises it as saying that IBM was 'eventually expelled in 1977 by the union-backed Janata Party'.[35] Fernandes's own words support that reading at the level of political intent.[36] The accounts agree on most of the facts. The rule made IBM's way of doing business in India impossible unless it gave up control of its core business; IBM would not; the minister closed the door loudly. 'Expelled' and 'chose to leave' describe the same event from opposite sides.

## June 1978

The unwinding was quick. In April 1978 *Computerwoche* reported that IBM, which under FERA could have kept only 40 per cent of its Indian subsidiary, against the company's worldwide policy, would end all its activities in India by the end of the year.[37] The refurbishing work in Bombay stopped. The computers IBM had rented out 'were sold to the customers at their book value which was almost zero'.[38] A group of former IBM employees formed a company called International Data Machines (IDM) and took over the card factory and the data centres.[39]

Maintenance went to a state company that had been waiting for it. The Department of Electronics had set up the Computer Maintenance Corporation (CMC) in 1976, Rajaraman writes, 'to maintain imported computers foreseeing such a contingency'.[40] It had cost Rs 53 million to establish,[41] about $5.9 million at the 1976 average exchange rate.[42] IBM sold CMC the spare parts for its 1401s and other machines at a nominal price, and CMC took over the maintenance of IBM's installations across the country.[43] Indian users, *Computerwoche* reported, were soon paying much more for data processing, and CMC was said to be using its new monopoly to charge fees even higher than IBM's.[44]

What happened to IBM's people is disputed. Rajaraman's account is smooth: 'Most employees of IBM joined CMC. There was thus no disruption of operations of computers'.[45] The trade magazine Dataquest's chronology says that T.B. Finn, who became IBM India's general manager in 1976, laid off a third of the workforce within four months, and concludes that IBM had already made up its mind to leave.[46] A 2018 report in the Deccan Chronicle on a reunion of former IBM India staff in Mumbai, forty years after the exit, said there had been around 150 employees at the end, and that many had been left with almost nowhere to go. Some went back to their home towns to look for work, some found jobs, and some started businesses.[47] One of them, K.R. Naik, laid off by IBM, later worked for ORG Systems, one of the Indian computer makers that grew in IBM's place, and helped organise the reunion.[48]

When IBM left there were fewer than 1,000 computers in India, and software exports were, in Rajaraman's word, insignificant.[49]

## The Sondhi policy

The new government reviewed the electronics industry. A committee chaired by Mantosh Sondhi, with Biswajit Nag as its secretary, found 'long delays in importing computers due to the cumbersome procedures of the DoE'. It proposed simpler procedures, and it proposed that private companies be allowed to make computers.[50] In 1978 Nag became secretary of the Department of Electronics.[51]

The minicomputer policy that followed opened a door but kept a hand on it. It favoured what it called systems engineering companies, which need not make their own processors or peripherals. Small entrepreneurs were preferred for dedicated machines for data entry, accounting, invoicing and typesetting. Foreign collaboration was in general not allowed, though foreign equity of up to 40 per cent was permitted in special cases. Every manufacturer needed a licence. Production was capped at Rs 20 million in value,[52] about $2.4 million at the 1978 average rate,[53] and there was a limit on the number of machines. Only the public-sector Electronics Corporation of India Limited (ECIL) would be allowed to make 32-bit computers, the larger machines of the time.[54]

One clause carried the labour settlement of the previous decade into the new one. The policy 'would continue to be restrictive in allowing use of computers in areas where labour displacement was likely to be involved'.[55] The state that let private firms build computers still limited where those computers might replace clerks.

Rajaraman's judgement on the policy is double. It 'did not completely free the industry from the "licence-permit raj"', but it let private companies into a field that had been reserved for the state.[56] Many licences went to traders who never made anything. The firms that did manufacture, by his list, were DCM Data Products in Delhi, ORG Systems in Baroda (now Vadodara), Hindustan Computers Limited in Delhi, PSI Data Systems in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), Wipro in Bangalore, Patni Computers in Pune, IDM in Bombay and Zenith Computers in Bombay.[57] Two of them left founders' and institutional accounts detailed enough to show what the work was like.

## An attic in Delhi

Shiv Nadar and his colleagues were engineers in the calculator division of Delhi Cloth Mills (DCM), a textile company. The calculators had moved from digital circuits to chips, and the more advanced chips went into programmable calculators, which Nadar later called the forerunners of personal computers. A few of the engineers thought they could build a computer.[58]

The state, in Nadar's telling, was not only an obstacle. The government's Electronics Commission 'was interested in making entrepreneurs out of executives and engineers. They asked us to write a report' saying they could build an eight-bit machine, and they did. 'Youthful exuberance and age were on our side'.[59] They launched Hindustan Computers Limited (HCL) in August 1976, two years before IBM left. Getting permission to make computers was the hard part. They could not expect a licence themselves, so they found a partner that had one, the government of Uttar Pradesh, and formed a joint venture. 'Interestingly, our toughest challenge was not designing or making computers but getting the government's approval to produce them'.[60]

The money came from the founders, who had first set up a company to sell calculators from their own savings and used its earnings for HCL. In 1975 six of them, among them Nadar and Arjun Malhotra, pooled Rs 175,000 in a calculator-trading company, Microcomp, most of which went on buying an office in Bombay. Once they had the licence they incorporated Hindustan Computers Limited with equity of Rs 2 million (20 lakh), about $223,000 at the 1976 average rate. A quarter came from the Uttar Pradesh government, and the 49 per cent meant for the public was taken up by Microcomp.[61]

The people were young. 'I was 31, and I was the oldest,' Nadar said. When candidates came for interviews, the founders told them first to consider the size of the company they were joining. 'We offered a monthly salary of Rs. 2,000,' he said, a sum 'more than what Citibank was paying at that time. Otherwise, how do you get someone to work in an attic with a bedroom, a table and a chair?'[62] Rs 2,000 a month was about $223 at the 1976 average rate.[63] It is the first salary this book can document for a job in an Indian computer company, and it is worth holding in mind. The export trade that followed was built on the gap between a rupee salary like this one and the dollar price the same skill could fetch abroad. Each hire added 5 per cent to the company's overheads, Nadar said, so they judged each one closely. They took five students from the Indian Institute of Management in Calcutta (Kolkata since 2001) and told them that if they did things right they should become entrepreneurs themselves. 'Did any of them become entrepreneurs? No one did'.[64]

HCL's engineers wrote software as well as building hardware. Before they could build their computer they had to create the system software that made the parts run together.[65] In the 1980s they wrote a relational database, which they called Genesis, and Nadar insisted they wrote it 'from scratch'.[66] HCL's first computer, the HCL 8/C of 1977, was meant to replace IBM 1401s, Malhotra recalled for the Computer History Museum. Because 1401 users were used to punched cards, HCL sold separate data-entry machines that recorded onto floppy disks. To ride out power cuts, the computer was wired to a truck battery that held its state through a cut, and a maintenance engineer had to call every two weeks to top up the battery's distilled water.[67]

Nadar's view of IBM's departure is the opposite of the nationalist story. 'If IBM had stayed on, we would have grown faster. See, IBM was fundamentally creating the market for computers in India.' After IBM left, HCL had to create the market by itself, and 'At that time no one saw the computer as a necessity. It was a convenience'.[68]

## Vegetable oil and the 8086

Wipro's road into computers ran through a university. In the late 1970s Wipro Products made vegetable and refined oils.[69] It decided to move into computers, and in July 1979 it signed a consultancy agreement with the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) in Bangalore, through the institute's consultancy centre, worth about Rs 1.4 million (14 lakh),[70] about $172,000 at the 1979 average rate.[71] On 5 October 1979 the institute's registrar wrote to Ashok Narasimhan, Wipro's general manager for finance and planning, that the institute had 'undertaken a project for the design and development of microprocessor-based minicomputer systems' for Wipro, with the know-how to be handed over 'in another fifteen months'.[72]

Sridhar Mitta, who joined Wipro as research and development manager in 1980, explained in 2019 why a company like his could now enter the business at all. The world, he said, was moving 'From the expensive vertically integrated systems where computer vendors had to develop components' themselves towards 'more open and cheaper, horizontally integrated systems', in which a maker could buy sub-systems and peripherals from different vendors and put them together.[73] A microprocessor bought from Intel could be the heart of a machine designed in Bangalore.

Buying even the tools was hard. N.J. Rao, who ran the institute's Digital Systems Lab, recalled that in 1979 Intel released a development system for its new 8086 processor. 'It would have been really difficult for a company to import the Intel Microprocessor Development System,' he said. 'So IISc procured it'.[74] The university served as a way round the import licence. The Wipro team worked out of Rao's lab. Azim Premji, Wipro's chairman, said the institute set up a committee 'to advise us on what computer we should make'.[75]

The Wipro Series-86 came out in 1981. It used the Intel 8086 processor and Intel's Multibus, a standard set of connections for plugging circuit boards into the machine, and its operating system was licensed from Sentinel Computer Corporation, an American firm. A team at the institute's Centre for Electronics Design and Technology, led by Kishore Babu, designed the case.[76] By Mitta's account the first year's revenue was Rs 20 million (2 crore),[77] about $2.3 million at the 1981 average rate.[78] The new company moved to its first office at 45 Dickenson Road in Bangalore.[79] Forty years later Premji told the institute's convocation that 'it would be quite accurate to say that the Wipro IT business was born in IISc',[80] a founder's tribute on a ceremonial day.

The Wipro story also names some of the first employees, which founders' stories rarely do. Y. Narahari, then an engineering undergraduate at the institute and later the chair of one of its engineering divisions, remembered that Victor Jayakaran, from the Digital Systems Lab, was one of Wipro's first hires. 'Out of the first few employees of Wipro, several were trained in this lab,' he said. 'IISc students were given an opportunity to work for a start-up like Wipro'.[81] Many of the institute's graduates joined Wipro in its first two years, among them Vallab Kulkarni, Srikant Seshadri, Anand Talwai and Ravi Bail.[82] Narahari himself was offered a job at Wipro early in 1982. He chose instead to stay for a master's degree in the institute's School of Automation.[83]

## Programmers made by scarcity

Rajaraman's description of what these firms did is the core of this chapter. They 'designed their systems from scratch using imported microprocessors, support chips, peripherals, and local components'. They 'designed and fabricated printed circuit boards, mounted and soldered all the components and tested the systems'.[84]

Then came the constraint that turned hardware makers into software writers. 'As software could not be imported they had to develop operating systems, device drivers, compilers and application programs. UNIX was the operating system of choice as it was easily available'.[85] Unix, developed by Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie at AT&T in 1969, was itself written in C, a high-level programming language, and because American regulators did not allow AT&T to market it, versions circulated almost freely. The one developed at the University of California at Berkeley was popular, and Indian minicomputer makers modified and adopted it.[86] A committee of the Reserve Bank of India chaired by Rangarajan, which recommended that every bank computerise its back offices, made Unix the standard operating system for bank computers.[87] The banks' demand, Rajaraman writes, was large 'and could not be ignored'.[88]

His conclusion follows. 'Thus during 1978-1984 a large number of systems programmers in India became experts in UNIX development and usage. They also adopted C widely as UNIX was written in C. This expertise came in handy when India entered the software services export market in the late 80s'.[89] Systems programmers write the software closest to the machine: operating systems, compilers that translate programs into machine instructions, and the drivers that let a computer talk to its disks and printers.

The only reference Rajaraman gives for this passage is a personal communication in 2012 from Vinay Deshpande, an engineer of the period. It is a participant's judgement, not a count.[90] A second participant, independent of that source, makes the same link. Malhotra said that HCL wrote its own operating systems and compilers 'because we technically weren't allowed to import software unless it was in source code form', and that this was 'why UNIX became so popular in India over time'.[91] He states the rule more precisely than Rajaraman: software could come in, but only as source code. The claim is plausible and partly evidenced.

The new firms also drew people from the state, among them engineers trained at ECIL.[92] After 1978, Rajaraman writes, 'ECIL found it difficult to compete with these companies and lost many of their designers to the private sector companies'.[93] In his account the years from 1978 to 1984 saw 'considerable local design and development'. Firms 'designed mother boards and device drivers, modified the UNIX OS and wrote compilers from scratch', and competition drove prices down.[94] The profession was being built from three directions at once: IBM's former staff moving to CMC, IDM and the Indian makers; ECIL's engineers moving to private firms; and new graduates from IISc, the IITs and the management institutes taking jobs in attics and converted offices. The universities began to respond in the year IBM left, when IIT Kanpur started India's first undergraduate degree in computer science; a national panel proposed a new Master of Computer Applications degree in 1980, and the private training company NIIT was founded in 1981 (Chapter 7).[95]
## Screwdrivers

The 1978 policy did not end the permit system. Infosys, founded in 1981 to sell programming abroad rather than build computers, found the import rules as hard as HCL had found the manufacturing rules: by N.R. Narayana Murthy's later account, a licence to import a single computer took about three years.[96] In 1981 the Electronics Commission appointed another committee under Rajaraman, which 'recommended liberalization of import of computers for genuine exporters'.[97] The trade was still tiny.[98] Nor had the argument about computers and jobs gone away. Seven years after Fernandes told IBM that computers took Indian jobs, the bank unions, who read the Rangarajan committee's push to computerise as a warning sign, observed 1984 as an 'anti-computerization year' (Chapter 8).[99]

The period ended with a new prime minister and a new policy. Indira Gandhi was assassinated in 1984 and Rajiv Gandhi succeeded her.[100] He had organised the computerisation of the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi, using locally made DCM terminals, and had heard there, in Rajaraman's account, the private makers' complaints about the rules that hampered them.[101] That year's computer policy, announced on 19 November,[102] let any manufacturer make 32-bit machines and removed the limits on numbers.[103] It also allowed assembled boards, with their microprocessors and interface electronics already mounted, to be imported, and cut the duty on them.[104]

Rajaraman saw what that meant for the engineers who had been soldering their own boards. 'It changed manufacturing to assembly and system integration.' Imported boards were cheaper and more reliable than boards made in India from local parts and imported chips. 'Thus local manufacturing of boards slowly disappeared'.[105] Within two years the growth of computers went up by 100 per cent and their cost fell by half, and the old guard called the new kind of production 'screw driver' manufacturing.[106] A friend of Rajaraman's described his own job as a consultant to Indian PC makers: finding firms in Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong to supply motherboards, disk drives, monitors, power supplies, cables and cases, and timing the deliveries to local 'manufacturers' who assembled them, 'put their labels on them' and printed a manual.[107] The ideal of self-reliance, the cornerstone of the Bhabha Committee's report, was in Rajaraman's words 'truly and completely buried'.[108]

He found one failure larger than the rest. 'The only major failing was not investing enough resources to develop a local integrated circuit manufacturing industry.' Indian engineers could design large integrated circuits for Texas Instruments and other foreign firms, 'but had no foundry to fabricate them in India'.[109] A committee on semiconductors had reported in 1973, and nothing tangible was done until 1978; even then, he writes, the investment was too small.[110]

What India kept was the skill of making computers work. The engineers who had written operating systems and compilers because software could not be imported, and the graduates trained to design business systems, were about to find a market abroad. The same 1984 policy that ended board-making recognised software as an industry, cut the customs duty on computers imported for software export, which had been over 100 per cent, and made one more change that would shape the next decade. 'Sending engineers abroad to develop and maintain software for clients at their sites and profits earned thereby was recognized as "software export"'.[111] By FY1990-91 software exports, insignificant when IBM left, had reached $131.2 million on the estimate of Richard Heeks of the University of Manchester.[112] Rajaraman dates the start of that trade to these years and gives it the name the industry used, 'body shopping'.[113] India had tried to make its own computers and was now assembling other people's. What it would sell instead was an engineer's time, and from 1984 an Indian engineer's hours at a client's desk abroad counted as an Indian export.
