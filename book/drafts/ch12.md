# Chapter 12. The engineer factory (1998-2012)

Kalpakkam, south of Chennai, is the site of a nuclear power station. In the early 2000s one of the engineers at the station had a son whom the anthropologists Chris Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan, in their published study, call Raghu. Raghu grew up in Kalpakkam, took a degree in electronics and communication engineering at a college in Coimbatore, and joined one of India's six largest software companies, which the anthropologists call Indian Computer Services (ICS). When the researchers met him in Chennai he was 22, unmarried and a Tamil Brahman, a member of a community that made up about 2.5 per cent of Tamil Nadu's population and that the study found prominent in the city's software firms.[1]

Raghu worked on production support for a British client's system. That is the work of keeping software that is already running in use: fixing what breaks, answering the client's requests, handling the next fault. He told the researchers that every day brought a new problem to solve, so he was never bored. But in their words he 'thinks he is not learning much', except how to turn down demanding foreign clients, and he wanted to leave the industry soon and study for an MBA.[2] In the meantime he enjoyed the money. After one year in the job he earned 200,000 rupees a year (about $4,400). His father, after thirty years as an engineer at the nuclear station, earned 400,000.[3]

Raghu stands near the start of a machine that, between the late 1990s and the early 2010s, turned out software workers on a scale no country had tried before. It had two halves. One was a mass of new engineering colleges, most of them private, which multiplied the number of young Indians with an engineering degree. The other was inside the firms: recruiting drives that tested hundreds of thousands of graduates a year, and training campuses that turned engineers of any branch into programmers in a few months. This chapter is about both halves, and about who got in. It is the chapter that gives this book its title, because the factory it describes produced the workforce whose future the last part of the book examines.

## Colleges by the hundred

For forty years after independence India built engineering colleges slowly. The government's statistical compendium counts 31 engineering and technology colleges in 1950-51, 149 in 1980-81 and 289 in 1990-91.[4] Then the pace changed. There were 486 such colleges in 1997-98, 678 in 2000-01, 1,077 in 2001-02 and 1,562 in 2005-06.[5] The country added more engineering colleges in those eight years than in the previous five decades together. These are counts of institutions, not of students, but they show where the growth came from.

V. Rajaraman, the computer scientist whose history of Indian computing has guided earlier chapters, describes 'a rapid expansion of engineering colleges between 1998 and 2010', and 'a rapid growth of private engineering colleges and colleges offering MCA courses' (the Master of Computer Applications, a three-year postgraduate degree in programming for science graduates).[6] The legal basis came in stages, which Chapter 7 traced through the courts. Rajaraman adds that 'Private corporate bodies were allowed to set up Universities in 2002', and that under this policy private universities and private colleges 'proliferated'.[7] The supply of MCAs grew even faster than that of engineers. Rajaraman puts the number graduating at about 7,000 in 1998 and 120,000 by 2010.[8]

Quality did not keep pace, and the people who said so were the employers. Rajaraman, writing around 2012, notes that graduates of the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) and the National Institutes of Technology, about 10,000 a year, were excellent, but that 'industry employers opine that the graduates of other institutions are not employable as soon as they graduate and need further training'.[9] The regulators tried to respond. The All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE), the statutory body that approves technical colleges, set up a National Board of Accreditation, and the University Grants Commission created an assessment council to inspect and grade institutions. Rajaraman calls these steps 'essential to control the quality of students graduating from private educational institutions which were proliferating'.[10]

The expansion was uneven across the map. Around 2000 the economist AnnaLee Saxenian reported an estimate that sixty per cent of India's computer science graduates came from three southern states, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.[11] The south's colleges fed the south's software cities. In Chennai, Fuller and Narasimhan found that of twenty software engineers under thirty on whom they had information, only three had studied outside Tamil Nadu. The rest could stay at home, they wrote, because 'young men and women, including Brahmans, can nowadays obtain good engineering education in the state'.[12]

By the early 2010s the building had outrun demand. The earliest year in the series of approved places used for this book is 2012-13. In that year AICTE had approved about 1.55 million undergraduate engineering seats, and about 37.6 per cent of them were empty, according to an analysis of the council's data by the education non-profit Avanti Fellows.[13] Approved undergraduate intake peaked at about 1.7 million in 2014-15. By 2016-17 almost half of the seats, 49.5 per cent, had no student in them.[14] The colleges had been built for a market that, as the end of this chapter shows, was already starting to change.

## Any branch will do

The second half of the factory explains why so many colleges were worth building. The large software firms did not hire computer scientists in any great number. They hired engineers, and then made them into programmers themselves. Rajaraman sets out the logic plainly: 'The IT companies' common complaint was that there were not enough employable graduates with computer science degrees. Instead they recruited all engineering graduates with aptitude in computing; however they could not be directly placed as members of project teams.'[15] So, he continues, 'All major IT companies had in-house training programmes ranging from 12 weeks to 6 months.'[16]

A mechanical, civil or electrical engineer would do, if he or she could pass the firm's aptitude test. That made a degree in any engineering branch a ticket to the best-paid jobs open to most graduates, and it gave families a reason to pay for one. The industry body's own projection, reported by researchers at Duke University, was that 84.8 per cent of India's four-year engineering graduates in 2004 took branches it classed as IT-related, a broad category that included electrical, electronics and instrumentation engineering as well as computer science.[17]

The scale of recruitment is clearest in the filings of Infosys, the Bangalore company whose American share listing Chapter 11 described. Its numbers are the company's own, but they were made to American securities regulators and they are consistent from year to year. In the year to March 1998, Infosys received about 70,870 job applications, tested about 21,350 applicants, interviewed about 5,800 and made about 1,790 offers, of which about 1,300 were accepted.[18] Ten years later, in the year to March 2008, it reported screening over 885,000 applications, testing over 227,000 applicants, interviewing more than 97,000 and making about 46,300 offers.[19] Offers had grown about twenty-six times. The filings count all 'offers of employment' without separating new graduates from experienced recruits, so the growth in fresher hiring alone cannot be read from them.[20]

The number of applications behaves oddly. The year to March 2005 shows about 1,348,800, more than the 885,000 of three years later, even though offers more than tripled in between.[21] Both filings use 'screened' in one place and 'received' in another, so the change of wording does not explain the fall. Offers are the steadier measure. Headcount tells the same story. Infosys had about 9,400 IT professionals in March 2002, about 36,800 employees in March 2005, about 91,200 in March 2008 (of whom about 18,700 worked outside India) and about 150,000 in March 2012.[22]

Infosys was not unusual in kind, only in what it published. Rajaraman writes that 'every major software company was recruiting around 8000 graduates each year' by the end of the period, and that with attrition of 15 to 20 per cent and workforces 'of the order of 120,000', 'the replacement numbers themselves were over 15,000 each year'.[23]

How dependent the colleges had become on this hiring was shown by the one year it stopped. The year to March 2001 had been one of record placements at Anna University in Chennai, when 65 per cent of the 70 companies that came to recruit on its campus were from the IT industry. By January 2002, after the dot-com bust described in Chapter 11, the university had placed only about 320 students, against 1,200 at the same point a year earlier. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) had taken 54 of its students against 177, and Infosys 46 against 126. The director of the university's placement centre, N. Baluswamy, told *The Hindu Business Line*: 'I would be happy if the companies do not put on hold the offers they had made.'[24] The firms came back quickly, and on a much larger scale. In the year to March 2004 Infosys alone made about 13,600 offers of employment.[25]

## Mysore

Before there was a training campus, there was a training building. In 2001 the magazine *Business Today* described the Infosys headquarters campus in Bangalore, where in a three-storey chrome and glass centre 'fifty of the country's best software instructors put every batch of engineers recruited by the company through a gruelling three-month training programme'.[26] By 2002 the company had also established a Leadership Institute, for training its managers in leadership, on a 200-acre campus in Mysore (now Mysuru), elsewhere in Karnataka. That was not yet a campus for new graduates.[27]

The trainee campus came next, and Mysore became the factory floor. In its filing for the year to March 2005, Infosys reported that it had 'recently completed the construction of an employee training facility in Mysore'. The facility could 'house 4,500 trainees at any one time' and train about 12,000 employees a year.[28] The company said it did not fully use new employees on client work while they were in training, 'particularly during our 14-week training course for new employees'.[29] Three years later the capacity was 4,800 at a time and about 15,200 a year, on a campus of 336 acres.[30] By the year to March 2012 the filing had a new name for it. 'We train the new engineering graduates that join us at our Global Education Center in Mysore,' the company wrote. 'With a total built-up area of 1.44 million square feet, the Infosys Global Education Center can train approximately 14,000 employees at a time.'[31] The course had lengthened too. The same filing describes a '20-29 week training course for new employees'.[32]

The firm also reached back into the colleges. Through a programme it called Campus Connect, Infosys worked with engineering colleges so that their own teachers could give industry-related training. In the year to March 2012 it reported more than 190 workshops for more than 4,500 college teachers.[33] The boundary between the college and the company, which in 1998 had been the campus interview, was becoming harder to see.

What the trainees made of Mysore cannot be told from these sources. The filings give capacities and square feet, not voices, and no published account by a trainee of that period was found for this chapter. The company's own press releases about the campus's opening were not obtained.

## The pyramid and the bench

The shape of the workforce followed from the shape of a project. In its 1999 registration statement, Infosys described a typical assignment. A small team of 'two to five IT professionals' visited the client to fix the scope of the work. The project managers then went back to India 'to supervise a much larger team of 10 to 50 IT professionals'.[34] A few people at the top, many at the bottom: the pyramid. Its base had to be refilled every year with new graduates, and the growth of the base was what moved people upward. In 2001 the company's head of human resources, Hema Ravichandar, told *Business Today*: 'We are growing so fast that many people with two to three years experience are getting into managerial roles.'[35]

The firms measured how much of the pyramid was earning money. Infosys called this utilisation. 'We define employee utilization as the proportion of total billed person months to total available person months excluding support personnel,' its filings said.[36] In the year to March 2012, utilisation of billable staff was 69.0 per cent including trainees and 75.9 per cent excluding them, down from 72.1 and 79.6 per cent the year before.[37] 'This decrease in employee utilization rates adversely affected our profitability for fiscal 2012,' the company told investors.[38]

Put the other way round, at any moment in that year nearly a third of Infosys's technical staff, counting trainees, was not billed to a client. Some were in training. Others were between projects, or waiting for one to start. The industry called this the bench. In the firms' own terms it was not waste. Most work was still billed by the time spent on it: in the year to March 2008 only 31 per cent of Infosys's services revenue came from fixed-price projects, and most of the rest was billed on a 'time-and-materials basis'.[39] A firm paid that way must be able to staff a new contract at once, so it keeps trained people waiting. But the bench is a cost that grows with every hiring wave, and it is the first thing cut when demand falls, as Chapter 11 showed for 2001-02 and Chapter 14 shows for 2008-09.

Turnover was the other side of the pyramid. Infosys estimated the industry's attrition rate, the share of staff leaving each year, at 'approximately 20%'. Its own rate was 10.5 per cent in the year to March 2004 and 9.7 per cent a year later, rising to 13.4 per cent in the year to March 2008 and 17.0 per cent in the year to March 2011, before easing to 14.7 per cent a year later.[40] People in Chennai talked about job-hopping constantly. Fuller and Narasimhan found less of it than the talk suggested: among the more senior ICS staff they knew, at least half had spent all or most of their working lives in the company.[41]

## One in twelve

Who passed through the gate? The best evidence for these years comes from the Chennai study. Between August 2003 and February 2005, Fuller, an anthropologist at the London School of Economics, and Narasimhan, who did most of the fieldwork, studied IT professionals in the city.[42] Most of their interviews were at ICS. [43] They interviewed nineteen of its staff and described their sample as 'tiny' and 'the haphazard product of the problems of access' in an industry concerned with security. 'All names used are pseudonyms,' they wrote.[44] Everyone from the study named in this chapter is a real person, given a false name by the authors, and most of their words come to us as the authors reported them.

At ICS the gate was a series of filters on school and college marks. Students needed 'a consistent record of high examination marks before they can sit the company's aptitude test and proceed to an interview, where only one out of twelve is offered a job'.[45] Starting salaries, in the middle of the range for large software firms, were 'around 15,000 rupees per month' for people in their early twenties (about $330), and could 'easily double within a few years'.[46] That put a new recruit's own income near the level that only about 6 per cent of Indian households reached in 2001-02, according to estimates by the National Council of Applied Economic Research cited by the authors.[47]

The study also shows where these recruits came from. 'Most younger people we have interviewed have fathers (and occasionally mothers) who are or were employed in professional or managerial positions in government, or public- or private-sector firms, and the only exceptional fathers are a few small businessmen or farmers.'[48] In this sample, IT did not lift families into the middle class from below. It moved the children of the salaried middle class, many of them the children of government engineers and officials, into a richer, private-sector version of the class they were born into. That is one city and one firm. It is still the most direct evidence in this research against the claim, often made, that the software industry created India's middle class.

Inside, the work was organised around appraisal and deadlines. Line managers appraised staff every six months. Those who beat their targets could expect quick promotion and pay rises, and those who fell short were told they would be 'let go'. Most staff worked a 40 to 50-hour, five-day week on flexitime, and when deadlines pressed, extra hours were expected from men and women alike. The work was done by teams of software engineers under project managers, from a handful of people to a hundred or so, some of them at the client's site abroad and the rest in India. The offices were modern and open-plan, and young men and women, many of them unmarried, mixed there more freely than in most social settings in Chennai.[49]

The gate had a keeper. ICS's personnel manager, whom the authors call Ramakrishna, was in his late thirties, held an MCA, and described his job as 'tough and challenging'. In the 'war for talent' among the leading firms, it was his responsibility to make sure ICS recruited enough good people, above all by picking the best graduates from the top colleges. He earned about 70,000 rupees a month, plus stock options, and through much of 2003-04 he was negotiating for a rise while threatening to leave for a job in Bangalore.[50]

A young man the anthropologists call Shoaib shows what the move felt like. He was 29, ran the company's intranet and website, and had a degree in computer science from a Muslim college in Chennai and an MCA. His father had been an engineer in the state electricity board. After his father's early death Shoaib was offered a post at the board, and turned it down. He said he would never work in the public sector, because there you always 'have to bow your head to somebody'.[51] He was also, the authors noted, more worried than most of his colleagues about the future: 'there is no security in this job'.[52]

Older managers in Chennai's manufacturing firms watched the move with some resentment. Sampath, the personnel manager of a bus and lorry maker the study calls Chakra Vehicles, said the IT industry had 'disturbed the social fabric', with its 'quick promotions', 'fancy designations' and 'phenomenal compensation'.[53] One of his colleagues, the authors report, said that at campus interviews by the late 1990s only the weakest men still wanted to join Chakra, because everyone better was chasing IT salaries. Sampath himself, after more than twenty years, probably earned about 60,000 rupees a month, a level a competent IT professional could expect to reach in half the time.[54]

Where the money went is less glamorous than the stereotypes. As far as the authors could tell, most of it went on housing, and for those with children on private schooling. Young professionals, often with loans, bought flats or houses much earlier than their parents had, so that people in Chennai said housing now came before children. The IT staff they met did not, in the authors' judgement, seem especially preoccupied with consumer goods.[55] Most of the young people they met had never even considered a government or public-sector job. One ICS assistant manager had turned down a post at the Indian Space Research Organisation because he thought it would be unchallenging and monotonous.[56] The city did not always welcome its new earners. Raghu shared a flat with two other young men in IT, and they were unpopular with their neighbours, who feared they would smoke, drink, cook meat and make a nuisance of themselves. In fact, the authors found, they never cooked at all. They ate every meal out, came home on weekdays only to sleep, and had friends round at weekends to drink beer.[57]

## Marks, English and caste

The Chennai study's most discussed finding is about caste, and the authors were careful with it. ICS, like many other IT companies in the city, 'is said to be dominated by the high-status "Forward Castes"', they wrote, especially Tamil Brahmans, who made up about 2.5 per cent of the state's population, 'and it is our strong impression that this is true'. But 'no accurate figures exist', and the guesses of people in the industry varied widely.[58] In a later paper they repeated that Tamil Brahmans were 'disproportionately well represented' in south India's IT industry, and that 'no figures to confirm this assertion exist', although all the circumstantial evidence pointed that way.[59] The study's terms are the ones used here. In its usage, 'Forward Castes', Brahmans among them, are those that cannot benefit from the reservation system of quotas in education and public employment, which is operated for the 'Backward' (and 'Most Backward') and 'Scheduled' Castes.[60]

The interest of the study lies less in the impression than in the explanation. Many people in Chennai said the reason was reservations. Tamil Nadu reserved 68 per cent of state-sector posts and college seats for the Other Backward Classes and the Scheduled Castes and Tribes, 'and many people say that this is why many private companies employ a lot of Brahmans. Yet reservations alone cannot fully explain upper-caste predominance in IT companies.'[61]

The authors offered three further reasons. First, Brahmans had become an increasingly urban community, more likely than others to have 'the social skills and cultural knowledge, including fluency in English, needed to work in global IT companies and to pass their selection tests in the first place'.[62] Second, the firms' own rules did the sorting. 'Because ICS allows only candidates with consistently high marks to apply for posts, its selection policy in practice favours Forward Caste candidates who, from a tender age, have been motivated to achieve repeated scholastic success.'[63] Third, Brahman families placed a high value on education, notably in mathematics and science, and they were the best-educated community in the state. The authors dismissed as 'fanciful' the popular idea, held by many Tamil Brahmans themselves, that the caste had a special aptitude for software because of its traditional religious learning.[64]

A footnote shows how small the measurable differences were. In 2004 the cut-off mark for admission to electronics and communication engineering at Anna University, the most competitive course in the state, was 293.28 out of 300 for Forward Caste candidates, 290.57 for Backward Castes, 283.67 for Most Backward Castes and 276.13 for Scheduled Castes.[65] The gap at the top was less than three marks in three hundred. The authors' point was that the belief in needing exceptionally high marks shaped how children were raised, even where the arithmetic was less harsh than families thought.[66]

The argument does not need the firms to have looked at caste at all. A selection system that screened on marks, then on an aptitude test, then on an interview in English, reproduced the advantages of urban, educated, upper-caste families on its own. It is one city and one firm, and national figures on the caste composition of IT employment were not verified for this book. But the mechanism the anthropologists describe is visible elsewhere. In 2014 Aspiring Minds, a company that sells employment tests to employers, argued that firms recruited from a limited set of colleges because 'the college name is a key signal, and resumes from unknown colleges aren't shortlisted'.[67] By its own estimate, more than half of the graduates who met its benchmark for IT services jobs were studying outside the top 750 colleges, and so ended up 'invisible to most employers'.[68] It also estimated that a student from a third-tier college, with the same test score and marks as a student from a first-tier one, had 24 per cent lower odds of getting a job, and if hired was paid 66,000 rupees a year less.[69] The claims come from a firm with an interest in selling another way to find such students. They point in the same direction: a filter that worked by college name fell hardest on students from the new private colleges and the smaller towns.

## Women in the factory

At ICS, 'approximately one-fifth' of new recruits were women, close to the share across the industry.[70] According to Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, 79 per cent of India's software professionals were men in 2001-02, a share it expected to fall to 65 per cent by 2005.[71] In the colleges the gap was narrower. Among undergraduates enrolled in engineering and technology courses at AICTE-approved institutions in 2012-13, 33.7 per cent were women, according to the council's figures as compiled by the Society of Women Engineers.[72]

What the Chennai researchers heard about women surprised them, and it differs from what studies of Western software firms had found. Almost everyone they met, men and women alike, 'insisted to us that both sexes have equal technical skills'.[73] In an English software company another researcher had found women caught in a 'double bind', judged either insufficiently feminine or insufficiently technical. Nobody in Chennai, the authors wrote, thought female software engineers inadequately feminine.[74] The difference between men and women at ICS lay elsewhere: 'Married women professionals consistently give their families higher priority than their work.'[75] Many men wanted 'home-loving' wives who would not work. So women in IT preferred husbands in IT, and men in IT tended to prefer wives who were not.[76] One leading company, the authors reported, had started an in-house online matrimonial service to encourage its staff to marry each other, and so reduce the number of married women who resigned to follow husbands working elsewhere.[77] The managers of two small software firms, one of them a woman, 'admitted to reluctance about recruiting women, precisely because their family duties take them away from work'.[78]

The study's women show the range. A project manager in her early thirties, whom the authors call Anuradha, had made it a condition of her marriage that she and her husband return from America to Chennai. Her daughter was looked after by both sets of grandparents, and she 'firmly believes that the IT industry has helped to empower women'.[79] Jayashree, about 35, an assistant project manager with an MSc in physics from IIT Madras, had a different experience. Childcare for her two children was 'an endlessly negotiated series of arrangements' among hired help, her parents and her parents-in-law. Her husband, she complained, did not do his share at home; many chores, she joked, had been 'outsourced'. She later resigned from ICS and did contract work for it from home.[80]

Two younger engineers on Jayashree's team, called Sarojini and Parvati, were in their mid-twenties. Sarojini had an electrical and electronics degree from Coimbatore and joined ICS as a trainee in 2002. She told the researchers she found the work exciting and challenging, though her first ambition had been medicine, and she insisted that any husband would have to let her keep working. She put her determination down less to the IT industry than to her family's matrilineal Nayar tradition and her own working mother. Later in 2004 the company sent her to New Jersey.[81] Parvati, with a BSc and an MSc in information technology, found her work boring after six months. A year later she was a team leader, determined to get a posting in America. One of her weekly tasks was to search an internet matrimonial site for a husband for her elder sister, and the profiles asking for 'home-loving' wives increasingly irritated her.[82]

The authors' verdict held both halves together. 'Plainly, the IT industry has not revolutionised the position of Indian women.'[83] Yet when they compared their informants with the upper-middle-class Chennai women studied by Patricia Caplan in 1974-75, for most of whom 'employment outside the home is not considered at all desirable', they judged that 'real change has occurred'.[84] Young women in IT appeared to 'command greater respect and have more bargaining power within their families', particularly over whom they married.[85]

## Routine work

What were the factory's products made for? Fuller and Narasimhan answered in a sentence that matters for the rest of this book. 'Because software engineering in companies like ICS or TCS has progressively become more routinized and reliant on standard packages, IIT graduates are over-qualified and can secure more technically demanding, better-paid jobs.'[86] Most new recruits in the major firms, they went on, 'have only a bachelor's degree in engineering', usually from one of the leading colleges in Tamil Nadu.[87]

The people doing the work said similar things. 'Software engineering does not demand exceptional ability, as some experienced people acknowledge,' the authors wrote. Shoaib went further and claimed that any reasonably intelligent school-leaver could learn the job. He was exaggerating, they said, 'but we have met several IT professionals who are bored because their work is too easy'.[88] Raghu, keeping a British client's system running, thought he was learning little. Parvati was bored at six months and a team leader at eighteen. Sarojini found it challenging.[89] The firms did not need all their recruits to be stretched. They needed them to be trainable, reliable and available.

Outside observers drew the same line. The Duke University team discussed in the sidebar below divided engineering graduates into 'dynamic' engineers, capable of abstract thinking and high-level problem solving, and 'transactional' engineers, who might have the fundamentals but were 'typically responsible for rote and repetitive tasks in the workforce'.[90] It was 'the jobs of transactional engineers', the team wrote in 2005, that were 'easily outsourced' to relatively low-paid engineers in countries like India.[91] The phrase was American shorthand, and it was unkind to many Indian graduates who did far more than that. But it described the bulk of what the factory was designed to supply.

That was the factory's design, and it fitted the business that Chapter 11 described. A client abroad specified a system; a small team onsite fixed the requirements; a large team in India built, tested and maintained it, to documented methods, on standard packages, billed by time. A trained graduate of almost any engineering branch could do most of that work after three months in Mysore. The design succeeded. It also defined the kind of work, documented, routinised and divisible, that later chapters show new software tools learning to do.

## Sidebar: How many engineers does India really produce?

No figure about Indian education is quoted more often, or more loosely, than the number of engineers the country turns out each year. In the mid-2000s, typical American press articles said that in 2004 India had graduated 350,000 engineers and China 600,000, against about 70,000 in the United States.[92] Later, figures of well over a million a year appeared, including in Rajaraman's own history. Neither kind was a count of graduates.

The first serious attempt to check came from Duke University. In December 2005 a team of graduate students led by Gary Gereffi, a sociologist, and Vivek Wadhwa, an executive teaching engineering management, tried to compare like with like.[93] They found that the large Indian and Chinese figures 'include not only four-year degrees, but also three-year training programs and diploma holders', and that they had been set against American four-year degrees alone.[94] Their own estimate for India in 2004, built on projections by Nasscom, was 215,000 graduates in engineering, computer science and information technology. Only 112,000 of them held four-year bachelor's degrees; the other 103,000 had three-year diplomas.[95] Per million people, they reckoned, the United States produced about 750 such graduates a year, China about 500 and India about 200.[96]

The Duke team also found something more striking. Nasscom's projections rested on government data published with a 'three- to four-year lag'. And 'the most recent official Indian graduation data are from 1993'.[97] For more than a decade of the boom, in other words, the Indian government had published no official count of the engineering graduates the industry was hiring. The lobby group of the industry that employed them was, in the Duke report's words, the 'de facto authority'.[98]

Later figures do not settle the question. Rajaraman gives the best-known growth series: 'In 1998 the number of engineering students was around 135,000 and in 2010 it grew to 1,300,000, a growth of almost 10 times.'[99] Elsewhere in the same history he describes what appears to be the same figure differently: 'Currently India graduates around 1.3 million engineers per year'.[100] Neither passage defines it. A total enrolment of 135,000 in 1998 is far too small for 486 colleges teaching four-year courses, so the 1998 figure is most likely one year's intake. By the same reasoning, 1.3 million in 2010 is most likely intake or approved seats rather than graduates. This book treats it as seats.

Two other figures help. In 2014 Aspiring Minds wrote of 'the six hundred thousand engineers that graduate annually', without giving a source.[101] And the regulator's data, as analysed by Avanti Fellows, show about 1.55 million approved undergraduate engineering seats in 2012-13, of which 37.6 per cent were empty.[102] If the Duke and Aspiring Minds figures are roughly right, India's output of four-year engineering graduates grew about five times between 2004 and 2013, from about 112,000 to about 600,000. That is consistent with the tripling of college numbers between 1997-98 and 2005-06.[103]

The answer, then, depends on the question. If it is how many engineering places India approved each year by the early 2010s, the answer is about 1.5 million. If it is how many students took up those places, it was fewer, since a third or more stayed empty. If it is how many four-year engineering graduates India produced, the best estimates are about 112,000 in 2004 and perhaps 600,000 by 2013, neither of them an official count. The often-repeated claim that India produces 1.5 million engineers a year matches the seats, not the graduates. The real number grew very fast. It was never as large as the figure that travelled.

## Trainable, not skilled

By the early 2010s a second number had joined the first. Only a quarter of Indian engineers were employable, it was said, or only a fifth. Both figures can be traced, and both come from parties with an interest.

The 'quarter' comes from the McKinsey Global Institute, the research arm of the consultancy. As the Duke study reported it, 'McKinsey concluded that only 10% of Chinese engineers and 25% of Indian engineers can compete in the global outsourcing arena', citing language, the quality of education, culture and other factors.[104] The McKinsey study itself was not read for this book. Its question was not whether Indian engineers could find work, but how many could compete for outsourced work from rich countries.

The 'fifth' comes from Aspiring Minds. Its 2014 report tested 'more than 1,20,000 engineering students from 520+ engineering colleges' who graduated in 2013, on the company's own test, against benchmarks it said it had built with client firms, including 'six of the top-ten IT services companies in India'.[105] It found that 'only 18.43% of them are employable for the Software Engineer-IT services role'.[106] For software jobs at product companies, among students of computer-related branches only, the figure was 3.21 per cent.[107] These are the shares of a tested sample that met the vendor's benchmark for a role. They are not measures of whether graduates found jobs, and the company that produced them sold the test.

Read for what it measures, though, the report says something precise about the factory. The IT services figure, it explained, was calculated under the firms' 'current hiring philosophy', in which a recruit was not expected to have software skills already but was trained 'over a period of 3 to 6 months'. So 18.43 per cent was the share judged 'trainable into software engineers within a period of 3 to 6 months'.[108] The firms' bar was trainability, not skill. That was the bar Rajaraman described and the one the Mysore campus was built for.

The same report contained an early warning. 'The IT services industry is not growing at the same pace as before and the growth of entry-level jobs is diminishing,' it said. Because firms were hiring fewer people and wanted to cut training costs, there was 'a slow but definite trend towards hiring candidates who already have decent expertise in programming'.[109] This is a vendor's observation, made by a firm that sold programming tests. It is still the first documented sign, in the sources gathered for this book, that the factory's demand curve was bending.

The price of a place in the factory points the same way. The figures are rough and measure different things. ICS's starting salary of about 15,000 rupees a month in 2003-05 came to about 180,000 rupees a year.[110] A study by the news site Moneycontrol, as reported by the trade publication *HR Katha*, put the median starting pay at large IT firms in 2012 at 245,000 rupees a year (about $4,600).[111] Adjusted for consumer prices, the first is worth about 660,000 rupees at 2024 prices and the second about 480,000.[112] One is a single firm and the other a median, so the comparison can only be suggestive. It suggests that as the gate widened, the price of entry fell.

## The other way out

For the young engineers in Chennai, one prize mattered more than pay rises or promotions. Overseas assignments, the anthropologists found, were important for careers because they provided 'exposure', the industry's word for new experience. Those who had not been abroad hoped to go soon.[113] Among their twenty engineers under thirty, seven had been on assignments abroad by 2005. Most had gone for only a few months.[114] Fuller and Narasimhan thought that offshoring would reduce such travel, because flows of people 'may diminish as the work travels to the site of labor'.[115]

The factory had been built to keep the work in India. But Sarojini was in New Jersey, and Parvati was set on America.[116] For a generation of Indian engineers, the road out of the factory still ran through the United States, and through one American work visa in particular. That road is the subject of the next chapter.
