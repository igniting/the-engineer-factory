# Chapter 3. Self-reliance and its limits (1962-1977)

On 18 July 1968 the government of India called a special meeting of its Standing Labour Committee in New Delhi to discuss a single subject: automation. It was one of the tripartite bodies in which government, employers and trade unions argued out labour policy.[1] For this session the Ministry of Labour also invited workers' and employers' representatives from the Life Insurance Corporation (LIC), the State Bank of India, the railways and the oil companies, organisations that would not normally have had a seat at the table.[2] The machines that had brought them there were computers, and there were not many of them. The country had 30 computers in 1965 and 88 in 1968.[3]

The workers' side asked for something close to a ban. Their statement, as the government later printed it, argued that 'since the country had an enormous volume of unemployment and was short of technological and capital resources the general orientation of policy should be against automation'. Exceptions could be allowed where there was a real compulsion, but until guidelines were agreed there should be a freeze, and employers who had already installed automatic machinery should be asked to stop using it.[4] The employers replied that some concerns had become so large and complicated that computers were a necessity, that automation created more employment across the economy in the end, and that fears of large-scale displacement were unwarranted because automatic processes had been introduced in only a few establishments.[5] The Labour Minister of Maharashtra proposed a tripartite sub-committee, with experts, to draw up national guidelines.[6]

The meeting ended without agreement. Summing up, the Labour Minister said there was no consensus, that the government would bear the different views in mind, and that the existing procedure for screening computer imports would continue. In the meantime the government went on allowing automation 'on a selective basis'.[7]

The argument in that room did not end in 1968. It set a machine that could do part of the work of salaried office staff against the jobs of those staff, and it asked who should bear the cost and who should keep the gain. The employers said growth would absorb the people displaced. The workers said a poor country could not afford to make them redundant. Nearly six decades later the same argument returned, over software that could write software, and the office workers whose jobs were in question were the programmers whose industry this book follows. The book comes back to it in its last chapter.

Between the war with China in 1962 and the general election of 1977, the Indian state tried to do two things with computers at once. It wanted India to design and build its own, as a matter of national self-reliance. And it wanted to stop computers from taking the jobs of the country's organised clerical workers. Both aims grew out of scarcity: of foreign exchange, which India needed to buy machines abroad, and of jobs, which the computer seemed to threaten.

## Repainted machines

Before 1970, V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, writes, there was no government policy on computers beyond the 1958 Scientific Policy Resolution.[8] The rule that mattered was about money. 'Foreign companies were permitted to manufacture and sell computers provided foreign exchange was not required. Computers were not considered a priority area that deserved foreign exchange outflow.'[9]

IBM found a way to work within that rule. It had had a commercial outlet in India since 1950 and began manufacturing in 1963, making punched cards at a factory in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) and, at Kurla in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995), the model 029 key punch (a machine for punching data onto cards) and other unit-record equipment.[10] The key punches were exported to IBM branches in South-East Asia, and the foreign exchange they earned paid for imports of used, disassembled IBM 1401 computers.[11] Around 1965 many 1401s were being replaced in America and Europe by IBM's new System/360, and IBM could import the old machines at a low inter-company price. In Bombay they were dismantled, their worn parts replaced, and then reassembled, painted and rented to Indian customers.[12] The government licensed IBM to recondition 68 of them over three years, from 1968 to 1970. IBM had applied to recondition System/360s instead.[13] Its British rival ICL ran the same trade from a plant in Poona (now Pune), exporting card equipment and using the proceeds to sell its systems in India.[14]

By 1970 the 1401 was the most popular computer in India, with about 80 installations. Indian Railways had more than a dozen; most Tata group companies and the big textile firms had them; the first computer at Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) was a 1401. For smaller firms IBM ran data centres in the major cities.[15] ICL, by comparison, had 48 of its 1901A machines assembled by the public-sector Bharat Electronics between 1965 and 1975.[16]

The machines were not cheap. The annual rental for a high-end 1401, with four tape drives and two disks, was about Rs 850,000, some $113,000 at the 1970 exchange rate.[17] The devaluation of the rupee in 1966 gave IBM a windfall, because its rentals were quoted in dollars.[18] IBM 'was in fact accused of profiteering', since rentals in America were much lower. Its defence was that the rental included free maintenance and the services of a systems engineer who analysed the customer's needs, designed the software and even helped with programming: IBM was selling a computing service, not renting a machine.[19] In that arrangement the engineer's time had no price of its own. It came bundled into the rent of the machine. The industry that grew up later would turn the arrangement round, and sell the time itself.

That service is where many Indians learned the trade. Rajaraman, who is not uncritical of IBM's prices, credits it with recruiting and training very good technical and sales staff. 'A whole generation of maintenance engineers and programmers were trained and nurtured by IBM. In short IBM set a bench mark for professional operations in India.'[20]

The state was already moving against the trade in repainted machines. By the early 1970s the new Department of Electronics had declared in its annual report that 'no further manufacturing programmes based on the renovation of 'As is' machines will be accepted in future as a matter of national policy'.[21] The question was what would replace them.

## A plan for 500 computers a year

The 1962 border war with China persuaded the government that electronics and communications mattered for defence and for long-run industrial growth. In 1963 it appointed a committee on electronics under Homi Bhabha, the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission.[22] The committee's report was ready by 1966, but Bhabha was killed in an air crash before formally submitting it.[23] It was presented to the government only after his death, and a committee under Vikram Sarabhai was set up in 1966 to carry out its recommendations.[24] The central ones were a Department of Electronics in the central government and an Electronics Commission with wide financial and executive powers.[25]

The committee's computer subcommittee was chaired by R. Narasimhan, head of the computer group at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR) in Bombay.[26] It projected India's needs for ten years ahead. One very large computer would be imported for a national facility. Three to five medium-large machines would be imported and run as Regional Computer Centres, shared like power utilities. All smaller computers would be made in India from Indian components, about 500 a year for ten years.[27] Its report argued that 'attaining self-sufficiency in systems engineering and fabrication is of fundamental importance from the point of view of the defence and security of our country'.[28]

In 1970 the plan was debated at a National Electronics Conference at TIFR, attended by people from universities, companies, government, laboratories and the armed forces.[29] Rajaraman, then on the faculty of the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Kanpur, argued that India lacked a home market large enough to build up in the textbook order, from components to circuits to systems. India should go backwards, design systems first and import the components, and pay for the imports with exports. 'Foreign exchange can also be earned by software export,' he said. In his history of 2012 he added: 'However, my comment was not taken seriously.'[30]

The chairman of the conference, Sarabhai, who had succeeded Bhabha, took a larger view. In his concluding remarks he called computing 'far more fundamental, of wider significance than any other field of electronics', and of 'particular relevance to our developing country where resources are scarce'. Multinational companies had a contribution to make, but not, he said, at the cost of a major national effort.[31] On that thinking the government started a computer division at the Electronics Corporation of India Ltd (ECIL), a public-sector company set up at Hyderabad in 1967 under the Department of Atomic Energy to commercialise electronics developed at the atomic research centre at Trombay.[32]

## The gatekeeper

The Department of Electronics (DoE) was established in 1970 and the Electronics Commission, by most accounts, in 1971. M.G.K. Menon, the director of TIFR, became secretary of the department and chairman of the commission.[33] Narasimhan later described the two big decisions that followed. The first was to develop and manufacture small and medium computers in India, so that within a set time most of the country's needs in that range could be met from Indian production. The second was to centralise all computer purchasing through global tenders.[34]

The DoE also became the gate through which every imported computer had to pass. In 1970 responsibility for import licences for computers was transferred to it as a 'single window clearance', and it drafted rules that judged each request by its essentiality, whether an equivalent machine was made in India and how much foreign exchange it needed.[35] The rupee was not convertible and the trade balance was permanently in deficit, so that 'obtaining foreign exchange for import of equipment required elaborate justification'.[36] The quadrupling of oil prices in 1973 made foreign exchange scarcer still. Unless the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) or a foreign government was paying, clearance to import a computer usually took one to three years.[37] The delays hurt universities, laboratories, industry and especially companies that wanted to import computers in order to export software.[38]

Foreign policy closed other doors. During the 1971 war with Pakistan the United States, which favoured Pakistan, embargoed exports of electronics and computers to India. India's first nuclear test in 1974 brought a further embargo on advanced electronic equipment, including high-end computers and scientific software.[39]

Private Indian firms, several of them already making electronic calculators, wanted to build computers. The DoE kept delaying the policy that would set the rules for them, partly because many components and peripherals would have to be imported and partly to protect the ECIL programme it was funding.[40] In the meantime, Rajaraman writes, the DoE 'became very unpopular' as legitimate requests from universities, government science departments and industry were 'inordinately delayed'; and 'no private Indian company was allowed to manufacture computers'.[41]

## A cottage industry in Hyderabad

ECIL's computer division was headed by S. Srikantan, who had designed the Trombay Digital Computer 12 (TDC-12) at the atomic research centre. It was a 12-bit real-time machine, similar in design to the PDP-8 made by the American firm Digital Equipment Corporation, and it was commissioned at Trombay in 1969.[42] ECIL had no commercial computer of its own on which to develop software, so it sent much of the work out to TIFR, IIT Kanpur and the Indian Institute of Management at Ahmedabad; with ECIL's own engineers, they produced an operating system, an assembler and BASIC and FORTRAN compilers.[43] The TDC-12 was followed by the TDC-312 in 1974, the 16-bit TDC-316 in 1975 and, in 1978, the Micro 78, built around a microprocessor.[44]

Between 1971 and 1978 ECIL sold 98 computers.[45] A TDC-316 cost Rs 3 million, about $360,000 at the 1975 rate.[46] ECIL had shown, in Rajaraman's judgement, that computers could be made in India from local components with imported chips and peripherals. 'It however worked more like a cottage industry', because its volumes were small. Most of its machines went to atomic energy establishments, government departments and universities that depended on government grants and were 'constrained to buy' from it. 'Only 4 machines were sold to the private sector.' The machines were overpriced and short of software.[47]

ECIL's most important work was hidden from view. The Air Defence Ground Environment System (ADGES), a chain of radar stations along India's borders to detect hostile aircraft, could not be bought abroad without revealing sensitive information to foreign vendors. TIFR designed the system and its software. Each installation used three ruggedised TDC-316s and cost Rs 30 million; 25 were deployed by the Indian Air Force between 1969 and 1984, and the income sustained ECIL's other development work.[48] Besides the 98 machines sold commercially, 75 ruggedised TDC-316s went into this project.[49] 'In the published literature this important contribution of ECIL is not mentioned,' Rajaraman writes.[50]

The most lasting product was people. ECIL trained 'over a thousand engineers in designing systems and developing systems software', and many of them later joined private computer makers and software companies to lead groups there.[51] For an Indian engineer in the 1970s who wanted to build computers rather than operate them, ECIL was one of very few possible employers.

## The clerks' case

The computer met its strongest opposition not in factories but in offices. The labour movement already had a template for dealing with new machines. In 1957 the 15th Indian Labour Conference had adopted a Model Agreement on rationalisation, which promised no retrenchment and no loss of earnings for existing workers, an equitable sharing of the gains among the community, the employer and the worker, and prior consultation with the unions before new equipment was installed.[52] Automation in new process industries and in manufacturing aroused little opposition. Computers in offices, the government's Committee on Automation later found, 'evoked serious misgivings among the employees'.[53]

According to that committee, the issue gained national attention because of the 'unilateral decision' of LIC's management, in the mid-1960s, to introduce a computer. The All India Insurance Employees' Association started an agitation, and the central trade union organisations took up the wider question of automation.[54] The unions' case, as the committee summarised it, was that automation was 'a painful luxury, and ... a labour saving device which has no place in a developing economy marked by labour surpluses and scarce capital resources'. They held that the loss of jobs, existing and potential, was built into any scheme of automation, and that using an expensive computer for routine office work was a misuse of a costly tool.[55] The employers answered that automation was simply an advanced stage of mechanisation, indispensable for industrialisation and better management.[56]

The dispute reached Parliament. On 24 November 1966, the day before the insurance employees' one-day token strike against the computers, the Finance Minister, Sachindra Chaudhuri, told the Lok Sabha that LIC had decided on computers in 1964 and installed the first at Bombay in November 1965. The switch would take some three years. '383 jobs would disappear but the very process of computerisation would create 225 new jobs.' The net loss of 158 was, he said, insignificant beside the 1,500 or so new jobs LIC created every year, and no one would be retrenched.[57] Four months later George Fernandes, then a member of the Lok Sabha, asked how many computers LIC had installed, at what cost, and how many employees had lost their jobs. The Minister of State for Finance, K.C. Pant, answered that one computer, made by IBM, had been installed at Bombay, and that no one had lost a job.[58] When another member asked whether the computers would harm the corporation's capacity to create jobs, and whether that was wise, the new Finance Minister, Morarji Desai, replied: 'It will be an unwise policy if we do not do this.'[59]

The tripartite meetings did not settle the matter. The Indian Labour Conference discussed automation in July 1966 and agreed that the Model Agreement should apply. The Standing Labour Committee returned to it in May 1967 and could not agree on clerical automation at all.[60] Union conventions in 1966 and 1967 demanded an immediate ban.[61] The one safeguard the government had, a requirement that any import using foreign exchange be cleared by the Labour Ministry, barely touched the problem. Most new computers were assembled in India, not imported, so they escaped screening. Between 1967 and 1971, 94 computers were introduced in India and only five proposals were referred to the Labour Ministry.[62] IBM's way around the foreign-exchange rule was also, in practice, a way around the labour rule.

## Inside the computer rooms

The Committee on Automation, which grew out of the July 1968 meeting, produced the best surviving picture of where India's computers were and who worked around them. By August 1971 it counted 140 computers in the country: 10 in government departments, 39 in public-sector undertakings including the railways, 55 in private firms and 36 in research institutions, technical institutes and universities.[63] Bombay alone had 35, a quarter of the total.[64] Nearly all were new. 'As many as 124 of the 140 computers in India have been installed only since 1965,' the committee wrote.[65]

Most did office work. Payroll, billing, invoices and financial statistics dominated, partly because these jobs had already been partly mechanised on punched-card machines.[66] The railways used their computers for wagon movements and accounts; the State Bank of India reconciled some 70,000 inter-branch transactions a day; Delhi Cloth Mills and Calico Mills used linear programming to choose the cheapest blend of cotton.[67] The committee heard the charge that some firms bought computers as status symbols, 'to keep up with the 'Joneses''.[68] When it asked 40 public and private organisations why they had installed computers, most named better service, faster tabulation and better management decisions. Only five named a reduction in labour costs.[69]

The committee's case studies show how the work changed hands. Computer departments created new posts, many at higher grades: systems analysts and programmers above all. Punch-card operators and machine operators were moved into the new sections, and much of the computer staff was recruited from inside the organisation and trained. Several existing employees, the committee found, 'got the benefit of better jobs and better pay', while the pay of the remaining clerks did not rise.[70] Some clerical workers now had to work second and third shifts to keep the expensive machines busy, 'a practice associated usually with blue-collar jobs'. Union representatives told the committee that computerisation had put clerical staff under more strain, requiring greater speed and fixed schedules.[71]

The question of jobs was harder to measure than either side admitted. A works study team in Maharashtra, looking at six establishments, followed 741 employees displaced by computers: 531 were given other jobs, 93 were retrained for the computer section, 71 were absorbed elsewhere in the organisation and 46 in expansion.[72] Almost nobody was sacked. The unions' point was that this was not the whole story. In one unnamed establishment studied by the committee, the staff on branch clearance and government accounts fell from 380 in November 1966 to 304 in January 1970, while the volume of work rose; the old methods would have needed 516 people for the 1970 volume.[73] The committee concluded that although there had been no direct retrenchment, the immediate effect of the computer was a fall in the demand for clerks, because vacancies left by natural wastage were filled by surplus staff rather than new recruits.[74] It was the next generation of clerks, not the present one, who lost.

Few managements asked their workers first. Of 42 units for which the committee had information, 35 had introduced a computer without prior consultation, and only seven had consulted their unions.[75] 'No instance was brought to our notice in which a proposal to instal a computer was dropped due to disagreement with union.'[76]

## The Committee on Automation

The committee was set up by the Ministry of Labour in July 1969 under R. Venkataraman, a member of the Planning Commission, and finished its work under the economist V.M. Dandekar. Its members included industrialists, an economist, a computer specialist and two members whose notes to the report speak for labour.[77] It was candid about its limits. It was 'greatly handicapped because of the inadequacy of available data', had found no comprehensive study of the effects of automation in India, and relied on questionnaires, case studies and oral evidence.[78]

Its recommendations, signed on 18 May 1972, tried to give each side something.[79] Computers in education, research, government statistics and defence should be allowed, with protection for existing staff.[80] Commercial and industrial users, public or private, and expressly including consultancy services, should be examined case by case.[81] Where possible their needs should be met from shared computer service centres, since a computer facility was 'in the nature of a public utility' and should preferably be in the public sector.[82] And 'all use of computers by commercial and industrial establishments ... should be subject to a prior agreement with the workers'.[83] A firm wanting a computer would write a Justification Report setting out the jobs to be computerised, the expected benefits and the likely effects on employment and pay. Experts chosen jointly by management and workers would assess it, and disputes would go to a National Bipartite Machinery of employers and unions.[84] The committee urged that India's needs in hardware and software be met 'as far as possible from indigenous sources', which would save foreign exchange and create jobs.[85]

Every member signed, but three explanatory notes show how far apart they remained. Satish Loomba wrote that in a capitalist economy automation served profit, so Indian workers had to oppose it in general; he agreed to the recommendations 'as a practical policy at the present juncture'.[86] G. Ramanujam warned that automation could reduce employment: 'It can rob labour of its personality, creative effort, sense of achievement and drag down work itself to boredom.' He signed because of the prior-consent safeguard and the public-sector computer centres.[87] The industrialists Naval H. Tata, Babubhai M. Chinai and B.D. Somani argued from the report's own figures that the effect on employment would be negligible, a couple of thousand people over a decade or more even if half the country's large companies installed computers, enough 'to dispel the exaggerated apprehensions entertained by some sections of the trade unions'. They too signed.[88]

How far the recommendations were applied is not clear from the record. Rajaraman, writing as a participant in the period, judges that the committee 'prescribed in 1972 strict controls' and that its report 'constrained the introduction of computers in banks, insurance companies and many private companies'. He attributes its hostility partly to the 1401s themselves: small machines used for payroll, accounts and stores, which vocal white-collar workers saw as labour-saving devices.[89] In 1975 Parliament's Public Accounts Committee added its own caution: in a country with large-scale unemployment the use of computers for labour-saving work 'may not be desirable or may even be detrimental'.[90] The DoE had to move cautiously.[91]

## Training the state's own

The state that restricted computers also trained people to use them, and much of the education of these years ran through its institutions rather than through universities. IBM had trained the first generation of programmers and maintenance engineers on its rented 1401s, ECIL its thousand systems engineers, and IIT Kanpur ran short programming courses for people from other institutions until 1975.[92]

The DoE's largest training venture was the National Centre for Software Development and Computing Techniques (NCSDCT) at TIFR, set up in the mid-1970s with money from the DoE and UNDP and directed by Narasimhan from 1975 to 1985.[93] Narasimhan saw the training of enough software engineers as a major challenge. 'Formal educational channels are wholly inadequate to cope with a demand of this magnitude,' he wrote. NCSDCT's answer was a one-year evening course in computer science, held at the Victoria Jubilee Technical Institute in central Bombay and linked by telephone lines to the PDP-10 computer at TIFR. Preference went to people already working in software, employers were persuaded to pay, entry was by a fairly rigorous test, and classes took 60 to 80 students at a time, taught largely through self-study, assignments and imported textbooks.[94]

The DoE built other institutions too. The National Informatics Centre, founded in 1975, set up a large computer centre in New Delhi for central government data processing.[95] Regional Computer Centres followed: at Poona in 1974, with an ICL mainframe offering cheap computer time to students and software exporters; at Jadavpur University in Calcutta (Kolkata since 2001) in 1977, where students in eastern India had free access to a Burroughs mainframe; and at Chandigarh.[96] In 1976 the DoE created the Computer Maintenance Corporation (CMC) to maintain imported computers and, foreseeing IBM's departure, the machines IBM would leave behind.[97] These bodies employed engineers and programmers on government terms. Of NCSDCT's successor, Rajaraman notes that a core group of its scientists 'later became leaders in the profession'.[98]

## A small door marked export

One DoE decision of these years mattered more than its authors could have known. Rajaraman records that, 'recognizing the potential of software export', the department allowed computers to be imported exclusively for software export, and that the first policy paper on this was published in 1974.[99] It was the idea Rajaraman had put to the 1970 conference, turned into an import rule: a computer could be justified if it earned foreign exchange by producing software for foreign clients. TCS, the Tata group's computer services unit, used the rule to import a mainframe and earn foreign exchange from programming work done for clients abroad.[100]

What matters here is the mechanism. In a decade when almost every computer import was suspect, the one sure justification was export earnings, and the export that India could make was programming. The machine was to be paid for with the time of the people who programmed it. That is the first form of the bargain this book follows.

## Four explanations

The record agrees on what happened between 1962 and 1977. It disagrees on why India did not build a computer industry. Rajaraman's verdict is that 'as a poor country with a low demand for computers it was strategically incorrect to try to design computers starting at the component level during 1970-1980 and hope to be completely "self-reliant"'.[101] Narasimhan had argued that building second-generation computers required production know-how that only large-scale manufacturers had or could command.[102] The historian Dwaipayan Banerjee's 2026 history of Indian computing, as its publisher and reviewers describe it, blames Cold War politics, trade imbalances, the monopolies of American corporations and the technocratic elite's preference for profitable technical services; one reviewer, Nikhil Menon, sums up the outcome as 'reliance, not self-reliance'.[103] The Committee on Automation's records add a fourth force. Organised clerical labour made the computer politically costly in exactly the sectors, insurance, banking and the railways, that would later be among its largest users.[104] These explanations do not exclude one another. The state was at once an obstacle and a builder, and both roles appear in the same documents.[105]

## Coda: 1977

In 1975 Indira Gandhi declared a state of emergency after a court ruled her election invalid; civil rights were suspended and opposition leaders arrested. The emergency ended in 1977, and in the general election that followed the Congress party lost power for the first time since independence. A coalition led by the Janata Party formed the government, with Morarji Desai as prime minister.[106] Ten years earlier, as Finance Minister, Desai had told the Lok Sabha of LIC's computer: 'There is going to be no retrenchment on this account.'[107]

The change reached computer policy quickly. M.G.K. Menon was replaced as secretary of the DoE, and in 1978 the government at last announced a minicomputer policy.[108] The same year IBM left India.[109]

The self-reliance programme had produced fewer than a hundred commercial computers and more than a thousand trained engineers. The labour settlement had made the office computer something a firm had to justify to its workers. And the import rules had left one opening. In the summer of 1970, when the Committee on Automation took evidence in Bombay, one of the delegations in front of it came from a small Tata unit that called itself a consultancy, and among its members was F.C. Kohli.[110] The clerks' unions had made the computer hard to justify at home. Kohli's unit would justify it by selling the work abroad.
