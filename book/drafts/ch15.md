# Chapter 15. The other employers (1985-2024)

On 24 March 1998 Microsoft announced from its headquarters in Redmond, Washington, that it planned to open a software development centre in Hyderabad. The press release was short and hedged. The centre would open in the second half of the year, 'Subject to clearance by regulatory agencies', and an exact date was 'unavailable'.[1] It would work on one narrow problem: making Microsoft's Windows NT operating system and its BackOffice server programs work with computers that did not run Microsoft software. 'The development center initially will employ about 25 people,' the release said, in a company that then had more than 25,000 employees worldwide.[2]

Paul Maritz, the group vice-president for platforms and applications, chose his words with care. 'The majority of our development work will always be based in the United States, but we have a great deal of faith in the IT skills of India,' he said, adding that although the centre was 'initially relatively small', its work would be 'highly valued and strategically important'.[3] Moshe Dunie, a vice-president in the Windows division, called it 'an extension to our product team in Redmond'.[4] Its general manager would be Srini Koppolu, who had spent more than nine years in product development at Microsoft and would move from Redmond to Hyderabad. He would report to S. Somasegar, a director of Windows NT, who stayed at headquarters.[5] The company said the decision came at the end of 'a long evaluation process that began over a year ago', and only once it had found 'a compelling task for the center, the right location and the right management'.[6]

Twenty-five people, one task, a manager flown in from the parent and a promise that the real work would stay at home: this was how a foreign product company entered India at the end of the 1990s. The earlier chapters of this part of the book followed the Indian services firms, which sold the hours of Indian engineers to clients abroad. Beside them, and less noticed, grew a second kind of employer: the Indian centres of foreign companies, which hired the same graduates to work on the parent's own products and systems. By 2024, on the count of an industry body and a consultancy, these centres employed about 1.9 million people.[7] This chapter follows them from a satellite dish in Bangalore to the 'global capability centres' of the 2020s, and asks what kind of profession they offered.

## A dish on the roof

The first of them had arrived thirteen years before Microsoft. Texas Instruments (TI), the American chip maker, describes its own start in a list of milestones it published for its twentieth anniversary in India in 2005: '1985 TI begins operation in India as the first multinational to establish a software design center in India and the first to install and use a satellite dish for exporting software.'[8] The claim to be first is the company's own. How TI obtained its satellite link, by working around a long list of government rules, is told in Chapters 8 and 10.[9]

What the milestone list shows is the kind of work the centre did. In 1989 TI India set up a design centre for mixed-signal integrated circuits, chips that handle both analogue and digital signals. From 1990 it designed application-specific products. In 1995 it opened a centre for the signal-processing cores used in hard-disk drives, and in 2000 centres for third-generation mobile and wireless network chipsets. In 2004 it moved into a new seven-acre design facility in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014).[10] None of this was billed by the hour. It was engineering for TI's own products, done by TI's own employees, in India. At the twentieth-anniversary event in August 2005 TI's chairman, Tom Engibous, showed two mobile handsets that the company said were the first designed entirely in India, one developed with the Indian manufacturer BPL. Several of the chips its Indian engineers were then designing used 65- and 90-nanometre manufacturing processes, which TI described as the most advanced in the industry.[11]

Hewlett-Packard (HP) came through a returning engineer. Radha Basu had left India for graduate study in computer science at the University of Southern California in the early 1970s and built a career at HP in Silicon Valley. In the mid-1980s she came back to serve on an electronics industry task force, and, in AnnaLee Saxenian's account, the government invited her to set up one of the country's first foreign subsidiaries. She spent four years building HP's software centre in Bangalore. When she returned to California in 1989 it employed 400 people.[12] Basu told Saxenian that doing business in India required patience: 'You can't just fly in and out and stay in a five-star hotel and expect to get things done like you can elsewhere.'[13] Saxenian adds that Indians educated in the United States were 'pivotal' in setting up the Indian facilities of Oracle, Novell, Bay Networks and other Silicon Valley companies.[14] Motorola's Bangalore centre claimed in 1993 to be the first in the world assessed at Level 5 of the Capability Maturity Model, a scale of software process discipline; that company claim is examined in Chapter 11.[15] Oracle (1994), SAP (1998) and Intel (1999) also opened development centres in India in that decade.[16]

Back offices came at the same time. Raman Roy, later called the father of Indian business process outsourcing, helped American Express and then, from 1996, GE to set up what he called captive shared-service centres in India: units owned by the parent and working only for it.[17] Chapter 14 follows that story. The word 'captive' stuck, and it describes the first wave well. These centres were owned by their clients. They did not compete for contracts.

How much did the work in these centres differ from the work at headquarters? Saxenian, writing in 2001, quoted the chief executive of one American company's offshore development centre in Bangalore, interviewed by another researcher in July 1996. He explained why his company had waited until the 1990s. Earlier, he said, he would have advised 'do anything, bodyshopping, subcontracting, modular work, but don't give full dependability here [in India] because nobody's ever done it.' By the mid-1990s new products and new versions of old products came from Bangalore. 'Technologically, there's zero difference,' he said.[18] It is a manager's claim, reported second-hand, and Saxenian called the evidence for such upgrading 'anecdotal'.[19] But it marks the ambition. Microsoft's Maritz, two years later, was more cautious about where the important work would be done.

## The captive that became a vendor

The most revealing case of the first wave is a company usually counted among the services firms. Cognizant Technology Solutions went public in New York in June 1998. Its prospectus opened its corporate history with a plain sentence: 'The Company began its software development and maintenance services business in early 1994 as an in-house technology development center for The Dun & Bradstreet Corporation and its operating units.'[20] Those units included the market researcher A.C. Nielsen and Dun & Bradstreet's own information and software businesses. In November 1996 Dun & Bradstreet spun off a group of businesses, this one among them, to form a new parent company, Cognizant Corporation.[21] The development centre then sold its services to outside clients. The captive had become a vendor.

The prospectus shows who ran it. Its chairman and chief executive, Wijeyaraj (Kumar) Mahadeva, had been Dun & Bradstreet's vice-president for corporate strategy and, from 1993 to 1996, chairman of Dun & Bradstreet in India and China; he had led the team that set up the Indian business in 1994. He held a Harvard MBA and a master's degree in electrical engineering from Cambridge.[22] Its president, Lakshmi Narayanan, had joined the Indian subsidiary as chief technology officer in 1994 after nineteen years at Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), where he had been a regional head.[23] The first Indian services firm had trained the man who ran the American captive.

The prospectus also shows where the work was done and by whom. At 31 March 1998 Cognizant had about 270 people in North America, at its offices and on clients' sites, of whom only fifteen were 'United States citizens or permanent residents'. It had about 30 in Europe, and 'approximately 870 persons on a full-time basis in its offshore software development centers in India'.[24] About 74% of its technical professionals were in India.[25] Its leased offices were in Chennai and Calcutta (now Kolkata); its executive office in New York occupied 3,800 square feet.[26] The engineers on clients' sites in America were, almost all of them, Indians on work visas. Cognizant explained its model in the same words as any Indian firm: basing technical work in India gave it 'access to a large pool of skilled, English-speaking IT professionals with which to service customers on a cost basis significantly lower than in developed countries'.[27] It also warned investors that 'wages in India are increasing at a faster rate than in the United States'.[28]

The prospectus describes recruitment and training in detail, and it is one of the clearer records of how a foreign-owned employer turned Indian graduates into programmers in the 1990s. Each year the company visited 'approximately 45 premier colleges and technical schools in India'. It judged candidates on academic results, 'a written aptitude test measuring problem-solving skills' and a technical interview, and it also hired experienced staff from other firms.[29] New recruits went through 'an intensive orientation and training program' that taught them the company's own software engineering method, which it called QVIEW.[30] By 2002 Cognizant said it gave newly hired programmers 'five months of combined classroom and on-the-job training'.[31] The degree got a graduate through the door. The employer taught the trade.

Cognizant then grew like the firms it was compared with. At the end of 2002 it had about 1,425 people in North America, 240 in Europe and 'approximately 4,500 persons' in its Indian development centres.[32] At the end of 2007 it had more than 55,400 staff worldwide, 'a majority' of its technical professionals in India.[33] At the end of 2012 it had about 156,700, of whom about 124,100 were in the Asia Pacific region.[34] By then it was also the largest single sponsor of petitions for the American H-1B work visa.[35]

The lesson is that the line between a foreign company's captive and an 'Indian' services firm was never fixed. A captive could be spun out to sell to others, as Cognizant was. A services firm could be bought by a multinational: IBM acquired the Indian outsourcing firm Daksh.[36] What stayed constant was the work, done by engineers in India for clients or parents abroad.

## A mirror in Bangalore

Product companies came for engineers, not for services. On 13 October 2004 Google's founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, spoke to reporters in Bangalore, the third stop of a visit that had taken them to New Delhi and Hyderabad. Google had opened offices in Bangalore and Hyderabad earlier that year.[37] 'One approach we are taking is that Bangalore is where we run a mirror exactly of what we have in the United States in terms of development,' Brin said.[38] Researchers in Bangalore would work with their American colleagues to devise new services and write the software, including search by speech, search in more languages, personalised search and features for the Gmail service.[39] Brin said Google had been 'too late' in tapping Indian talent: 'We would have preferred to do it sooner.'[40]

The man doing the hiring was Krishna Bharat, the engineer who had created Google's news service. 'We don't have a cap (on how many to hire),' he told the Associated Press. 'We will take in as many people as we can, if they meet our global standards.'[41] The phrase 'global standards' marks the difference from Microsoft's careful start six years earlier. Google did not describe its Bangalore office as a satellite with a defined task. It described it as a copy of the parent.

## January 2007

Two announcements in the last week of January 2007 showed how far the multinationals had come. On 29 January Accenture's chairman and chief executive, William D. Green, told reporters in Bangalore that the consultancy would have 35,000 staff in India by the end of August, up 52% on a year earlier. That would give it more employees in India than in the United States, where it would have just over 30,000.[42] Most would work in what Accenture called its Global Delivery Network, about 65,000 people worldwide, in a company of 160,000. 'There is more outsourcing work than we can do,' Green said.[43] The reporter, John Ribeiro, noted that multinational services companies including IBM, EDS and Capgemini were expanding fast in India, 'helping push up wages and attrition rates' among software and back-office staff. Accenture put its own staff turnover in India in the 'mid to high teens'. It planned to train its Indian staff in business consulting, had opened a consulting analytics centre in Delhi, and had sent four Indian managers to run an operation of 3,700 people in China.[44] The Indian centre was already exporting managers.

Three days later *InformationWeek* reported that IBM's 'Indian workforce now stands at 53,000, up from 39,000 a year ago and 23,000 two years ago'. If that rate continued, IBM would have more than 70,000 people in India during 2007, 'almost 25% of its current worldwide workforce of 300,000'.[45] 'IBM workers in India are engaged in everything from routine coding to high-end system design,' the article said; a year earlier the company had moved all its development of software for service-oriented architectures, a way of building large systems out of networked services, to Bangalore.[46] India was also IBM's fastest-growing market, with sales up 37% in 2006.[47] For a company that had shut its Indian operations in 1978 (Chapter 5), the return was complete.

Accenture and IBM were not captives. They were services firms that happened to be American, and their Indian centres did the same kind of work as the Indian firms, for the same kind of clients. They belong in this chapter for another reason. They hired from the same colleges, on the same campuses, and by 2007 they were among the largest employers of Indian software engineers. For an engineer leaving college in 2007, 'working for a foreign company' could mean a chip-design team at TI, a product team at Google or Microsoft, a back-office captive at a bank, or a delivery centre at IBM or Accenture. The employers were foreign. The work ranged from research to routine maintenance.

## Counting the centres

The first aggregate count of foreign companies' research and development centres came from a consultancy. Zinnov, a consultancy whose clients include the multinational centres it counts, reported in early 2008 that there were about 594 multinational R&D centres in India employing about 146,760 people, according to a summary published by the trade paper *EE Times*. More than half, 312, were in Bangalore; Pune, the Delhi area, Hyderabad and Chennai followed far behind. The largest groups worked on telecommunications and networking, on semiconductors and chip-design tools, and on consumer electronics.[48] The paper's headline was that India's R&D drive was slowing. Zinnov counted 93 new centres opened in 2004 and only 24 in 2007, and it reported rising costs and attrition of 8% to 20% a year.[49] These are a consultancy's figures, from a firm whose clients were the centres it counted, and they are given here from a summary of the article.

A clearer view of the labour market comes from two scholars, Sarosh Kuruvilla and Aruna Ranganathan, who studied the Indian outsourcing industry from fieldwork in 2004-07 and wrote in mid-2008. Drawing on figures from Nasscom, the industry's lobby group, they put the number of software professionals employed in India at 297,000 in 2005, 398,000 in 2006 and about 562,000 in 2007. The three largest software firms hired more than 72,000 people in the year to March 2007.[50] Turnover in 2007 was about 20% a year in software and 50-60% in back-office work.[51] In 2003, 77% of firms had met their recruiting needs through 'lateral' hires, experienced people taken from other employers rather than graduates from college.[52]

Kuruvilla and Ranganathan named the multinationals as one cause. The human resources managers they interviewed were content for attrition to be high among new entrants, but not higher up, and 'the entry and expansion of large MNCs such as IBM, Oracle, and Accenture into India have resulted in considerable "poaching" of developers, project managers, and high-level executives'.[53] Their own judgement, which they said rested on no systematic study, was that the main reason software engineers left was not pay but 'the lack of opportunities to work on cutting-edge technology'.[54] That is exactly what the product companies' centres claimed to offer. In research and development itself the authors found 'relatively few current shortages' in July 2008, except for what they called 'desperate shortages of visionaries' in software R&D, and they warned that the Indian education system could not quickly raise its output of scientists and engineers to meet future demand.[55]

The authors also give a portrait of the workforce, from a Nasscom survey of 2004. The average software professional was about 28 years old. Three in four (76%) were men. Half had been with their employer for more than three years. Some 81% had degrees, and 69% of the whole were engineers.[56] A table assembled from a Nasscom-Hewitt pay survey gives median pay in the Indian software industry in 2004, in US dollars: about $6,560 for a software engineer, $14,820 for a team leader, $30,816 for a project manager and $63,125 for the head of a large development unit.[57] The table does not separate multinational centres from Indian firms.

The Indian firms knew who their rivals for staff were. Infosys told American investors in its annual report for the year to March 2002 that it had long faced strong competition for employees from large multinationals that had set up, and were still setting up, offshore operations in India, as well as from Indian companies.[58] By the year to March 2009 its warning had widened: hiring by technology companies, especially in India, and worldwide competition for skilled professionals might cause a shortage of qualified staff in the places it hired.[59]

## Captives and careers

Did the other employers offer a different career? The evidence in hand is thinner than the question deserves. The executives' statements describe product work: Brin's 'mirror', TI's chip design, IBM's 'high-end system design'.[60] TI reports that the Indian business magazine *Business World* named TI India the 'Best Company to Work for in India' in 2003 and second best in 2004.[61] These are employers describing themselves.

The one comparative study in hand concerns back offices, not engineering. Kuruvilla and Ranganathan distinguished 'captives', 'BPO firms owned by U.S. client firms such as Dell, EDS, and HP that service only their owners', from 'third-party firms' that served many clients, and noted that consolidation was 'eroding the distinction'.[62] They found turnover of 15% to 20% a year in five captive firms, 'although even some captives reported rates as high as 30%'. A survey of seven large third-party call centres found an average of 30%, ranging from 12% to 62%.[63] Their conclusion: 'captive call centers are able to provide more promotions to higher levels than "third-party" call centers, because the market stability they enjoy fosters longer career paths'.[64] A captive's work did not depend on winning the next contract. That stability, on this evidence, made room for a ladder.

Whether the same was true for software engineers at the multinationals' development centres, and whether they were paid more than engineers at the Indian services firms, the sources in hand do not say. A pay premium at the multinational centres is often asserted. It is not documented here, and this chapter does not claim one. No engineer at one of these centres speaks in his or her own words in the sources consulted; every voice in this chapter so far belongs to an executive, a consultant or a scholar.

## Sidebar: Why did Eastern Europe become a nearshore rival, and why couldn't it scale?

In January 2007, the same week he announced that Accenture would soon have more staff in India than in America, William Green looked at the alternatives. 'There will not be another phenomenon like India,' he said, and he expected no other country to overtake India in Accenture's headcount for at least ten years.[65] His colleague Basilio Rueda, who ran the firm's Global Delivery Network, explained why. Accenture had opened service centres in Eastern European cities such as Prague and Bucharest, but, he said, it was not possible to increase the staff there easily, and the cost of staff was far higher than in India. The Eastern European centres were 'near-shore' locations, chosen to serve European clients from close by with cultural affinity.[66]

The firm that tested the nearshore model most fully was EPAM Systems, a services company listed in New York whose engineers worked mainly in the former Soviet bloc. In its annual report for 2021 EPAM wrote: 'Our largest delivery centers are located in Ukraine, Belarus and Russia'. At the end of that year it had 12,389 delivery professionals in Ukraine, 9,416 in Belarus and 8,933 in Russia, out of 52,617. India came next, with 4,349, followed by Poland, the United States, Hungary and Mexico.[67] EPAM explained its choice of countries in terms an Indian firm would have recognised: a supply of qualified software engineers, 'established educational infrastructure', an industrial base and government tax incentives.[68]

Then, on 24 February 2022, Russian forces attacked Ukraine.[69] EPAM's annual report for 2024 records the cost. Business continuity plans, relocation, the company's 'humanitarian commitment to our people in Ukraine' and 'the cost of our phased exit from Russia' had 'resulted in materially increased expenses'.[70] The company sold its remaining holdings in Russia on 26 July 2023.[71] And it reported a new fact about itself: 'During 2024, India became our largest delivery location, measured by the number of delivery professionals'. It had 10,072 there at the end of 2024, having added 3,006 during the year. Ukraine, with 8,764, down from 9,113 a year earlier, remained 'a significant delivery location'. EPAM's delivery staff as a whole numbered about 55,100.[72]

What do these figures show? They do not show that Eastern Europe failed a market test. A war is not a market. EPAM's Ukrainian engineers kept working through it, and the company said its operations there had not been significantly affected.[73] They show something narrower. Before 2022, a firm built on Eastern European engineers had about 30,000 of them in three neighbouring countries and grew there for years. When those countries became a risk, the company turned to India to grow, and more than doubled its Indian delivery staff in three years, to the point where India overtook Ukraine. Rueda's remark of 2007 points to one reason: the pools of engineers in Prague or Bucharest were not large enough to grow quickly, and they were dearer.[74] The sources in hand do not give the graduate output or wages of Poland, Ukraine or Romania that would let that point be tested, and the comparison rests on one consultancy's executives and one firm's filings.

## A new name

In August 2019 Amazon opened a campus in Hyderabad that it described as 'our first owned campus outside USA and the single largest building in terms of area and head count for Amazon globally'. The company's vice-president for real estate, John Schoettler, wrote on its Indian blog that the building had '15,000 work points across 1.8 million square feet' of office space, three amenity floors and twelve office floors, and lifts able to move 972 people at once.[75] The largest building of one of the world's largest companies was not in Seattle. It was in the city where Microsoft had started with twenty-five people.

By then the industry had a new name for such places. A 'global capability centre', or GCC, is a foreign company's own unit in India, whether it writes software, designs chips, runs finance and human resources, or analyses data. The term covers the old development centres and the old captives alike. In September 2024 Nasscom and Zinnov, the lobby group and a consultancy that sells advice to GCCs, published a report on them. As Reuters summarised it, about 1,700 companies had GCCs in India, employing 1.9 million people, and the centres' market size was $64.6 billion in the year to March 2024.[76] Zinnov's own summary counted more than 2,975 separate units, over 400 new centres in five years, and claimed that multinationals now did nearly a third of their global engineering in India.[77] The 'revenue' of a GCC needs care. A captive centre does not sell on the open market; its income is, in the main, what its parent pays it for the work. The figure measures the size of the work, not sales won.

The report also made projections. By 2030, it said, between 2,100 and 2,200 companies would have GCCs in India, employing between 2.5 and 2.8 million people, and the market would be worth $99-105 billion.[78] Those are forecasts by interested parties. In May 2026 *Business Today* reported the next edition of the same report: for the year to March 2026, it counted 2,117 GCCs employing 2.36 million people, with revenue of about $98.4 billion, close to the figure projected four years ahead of time.[79] These too are the industry's own figures, reported in the press; the report itself has not been read.

How large is 1.9 million? Nasscom, in its review of February 2025, restated the size of the whole Indian technology industry after counting GCCs more fully: about 5.67 million employees in the year to March 2024. It said that the industry's export revenue was now split equally between 'Global MNCs (including GCCs)' and Indian service providers.[80] On these figures, roughly a third of India's technology workforce in 2024 worked for the other employers' own centres. Both numbers come from the lobby group and its partner, and the definition of a GCC is theirs, so the share is an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

The work itself was said to be changing. Zinnov's summary claimed more than 500 centres devoted to artificial intelligence and machine learning, a pool of more than 120,000 people working on AI, and about 6,500 senior 'global roles' held in India, including more than 1,100 held by women.[81] It also pointed to smaller cities, where costs could be 25-30% lower.[82] State governments competed for the centres. Karnataka's GCC policy for 2024-29, as Zinnov summarised it, aimed to double the number of GCCs in the state to 1,000 and to create 350,000 jobs by 2029.[83] The Union Budget of February 2025 announced a national framework to bring GCCs to smaller cities.[84]

None of this made the GCC new. The institution goes back to TI's dish in 1985, to HP's 400 people in 1989, to the captives of American Express and GE, and to the 594 R&D centres Zinnov counted in 2008.[85] Cognizant itself began as one. What changed by 2024 was the name, the scale, the spread from software and internet companies to banks and insurers, with retailers, health-care and drug companies expected next, and the claim that senior roles, not only tasks, were now based in India.[86]

## The circle closes

The last document in this chapter is a services firm's annual report. At the end of 2024 Cognizant had 'approximately 336,800 employees', of whom 241,500 were in India and 42,800 in North America; women made up about 38% of the workforce.[87] Seventy-two per cent of the staff of a company that had gone public in New York with an executive office of 3,800 square feet now worked in India.[88] Accenture, IBM and EPAM had gone the same way, each in its own measure.[89]

Among its competitors Cognizant now listed, beside Accenture, Infosys, TCS and Wipro, 'clients' in-house technology resources, such as GCCs'.[90] Its risk factors were sharper. 'We face competition from clients' in-house technology resources, such as GCCs, which may provide a lower cost alternative to our services,' it told investors. And for staff: 'We compete for employees not only with other companies in our industry but also with companies in other industries, such as software services, engineering services and financial services companies, as well as our clients' GCCs.'[91]

A company that began in 1994 as one American firm's captive centre in Chennai now named other firms' captive centres as a threat to its business and to its hiring.[92] The circle had closed. Clients that had once sent work to Indian vendors were building their own Indian teams, staffed by the same graduates from the same colleges.

This matters for the argument of the rest of the book. A services firm sells its engineers' hours, and the price of an hour is set in a contract with a client. A GCC employs engineers to build and run its parent's own products and systems, and any gain in their output belongs directly to the parent. When tools arrive that let one engineer do the work of several, the two kinds of employer face different questions. The services firm must decide who keeps the saving, and the client will ask for it. The GCC's parent simply keeps it. Chapter 19 returns to that difference.

The other employers also tested a claim the Indian industry made about itself: that it had built a profession, not just a cost advantage. The multinational centres showed that Indian engineers could do the parent's core work, not only its maintenance. They did not show who would answer for the work when it went wrong. That question, of trust and of governance, is where the next chapter begins, inside the services industry itself and inside the state.
