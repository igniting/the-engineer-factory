The Engineer Factory

Chapter 1. The statistician's machines (1931-1966)

In February 1959 a small team of physicists and engineers in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) finished assembling a computer, and then had to stop work on it. The machine stood in the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), which had been working on it since 1955. It held 2,700 vacuum tubes, 1,700 germanium diodes and about 12,500 resistors, and it drew around 18 kilowatts of power.1 Valves at that density give off a great deal of heat, and the institute had no suitably air-conditioned room in which to run them.2 'Unfortunately, owing to the lack of air-conditioning facilities, the work had to be suspended till almost the end of 1959,' its designer, R. Narasimhan, wrote a few weeks after the machine finally entered service.2 Testing began only in mid-November, with an auxiliary air-conditioning system brought in for the purpose, and the computer was commissioned for routine work in the third week of February 1960.3

The men who built it had learned as they went. When the project began, the team numbered about six, most with a master's degree in physics and a specialism in electronics. Narasimhan described them in 1999: 'Except for one member (namely, myself), none had been outside India, and none of the participants in the project, including myself, had any experience in either using a computer or operating one, let alone building one.'4 Their machine would later be named TIFRAC, and Narasimhan would call it 'the first fullscale electronic digital computer designed, built, and operated in India'.5

A year's delay for want of an air conditioner is a small story. It also captures much of this chapter. India's first computers were acquired, and in one case built, by a handful of scientists working for the state. They were not bought by firms or sold in a market. They were limited less by ideas than by things that had to be paid for in foreign currency or manufactured at scale: machines, spare parts, cooling, memory. And the most lasting thing they produced was not hardware. It was a small group of people who now knew how computers worked.

Two institutions, in two cities, took two different routes. In Calcutta (Kolkata since 2001), the Indian Statistical Institute (ISI) bought its machines, first from Britain and then from the Soviet Union. In Bombay, TIFR decided to build one. By the mid-1960s both had arrived at the same place. ISI's attempt to build a machine of its own failed, and TIFR, having built TIFRAC, chose to buy an American computer to replace it. How and why they got there is the first part of the story of how India came to sell software services rather than computers.

Computers before machines

The Indian Statistical Institute was founded in Calcutta in 1931 under Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis.6 In its early decades the word 'computer' meant a person. Mahalanobis designated some of the institute's statistical staff as computers, and they calculated and tabulated the institute's large sample surveys, beginning with those of the jute crop.7 Machines arrived slowly. By late 1951, according to a National Sample Survey report of 1953 quoted by Kris Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors, the institute had two new models of IBM tabulators, the punched-card machines then used for counting and sorting large volumes of records.8

What turned this into a demand for electronic computers was national planning. Mahalanobis helped set up the National Sample Survey in 1950, a continuing household survey that produced data on a scale no team of clerks could tabulate quickly.9 The historian Nikhil Menon argues that the decision to plan India's economy from the centre, and the 'explosion of official statistics' that came with it, provided the justification for the pursuit of computers. ISI's influence, he writes, culminated in its effectively authoring the Second Five Year Plan of 1956-61.10 Menon stresses that India sought computers for planning and development, not for military use.11

V. Rajaraman, an engineer who took part in much of what this book describes, carried much of the early computer-science teaching at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Kanpur (Chapter 2) and later wrote a detailed history of Indian computing. He puts the two founders of Indian computing side by side. 'Nehru's confidants, P.C.Mahalanobis and Homi J.Bhabha, influenced policy making in government,' he writes; one was a statistician, the other an atomic scientist.12 India's Planning Commission, chaired by the Prime Minister, drafted five-year plans and allocated resources. The Second Plan, for 1956 to 1961, was drafted by Mahalanobis, with an emphasis on heavy industry: steel plants and large hydroelectric projects in the public sector.13 A plan of that size, built from survey data, needed arithmetic on a scale that a room of clerks with tabulators could not supply quickly enough. That, more than any interest in the machines themselves, is why a statistician became India's first buyer of computers.

Mahalanobis first saw an electronic computer at Harvard in 1947.14 Three years later ISI opened a computing laboratory under the engineer S.K. Mitra, and by 1953 the laboratory had built an analogue computer from Second World War surplus parts. It could solve simultaneous linear equations in up to ten variables.15 At about the same time, at the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), the young Rajaraman was working on a differential analyser, another kind of analogue machine.16 An analogue machine represents numbers as physical quantities such as voltages, rather than as digits, and it could not do the general-purpose arithmetic that planning required. Mahalanobis suspected that it would not be adequate, according to Menon.17 He wanted a digital computer, and India could not make one.

A Cold War shopping trip

For four years Mahalanobis tried to buy an American machine. Between 1950 and 1954 he held informal talks in Washington about acquiring an American UNIVAC.18 Menon, who has worked through the archives, writes that Mahalanobis had been fixed on the UNIVAC, a machine famous in American popular culture, since the 1940s, and that the best American machines would cost a million dollars.19 The talks failed. One reason, Menon found, was a secret dispatch sent from the US Embassy in Delhi to the State Department in 1953, which described Mahalanobis as a Soviet sympathiser.20 The prize committee that honoured Menon's peer-reviewed article summarised the same point: perceptions that Mahalanobis was too close to the Soviet Union hampered his search for an American computer.21

He turned east. In February 1954, at a lunch hosted by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru, Mahalanobis pressed the Soviet ambassador, Mikhail Menshikov, for help with a computer, arguing in effect that the Soviet Union could hardly object to sharing what the Americans already knew.22 That spring and summer he wrote a run of letters about computers to his colleague Pitambar Pant, who ran ISI's office in Delhi, from Geneva in April, London and Prague in June, and Moscow in July.23 The letters survive in Pant's papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library. Their contents have not been seen for this book, but their dates trace the journey.

In Moscow that summer Mahalanobis, Mitra and a delegation of Indian scientists visited the Institute of Precision Mechanics and Computer Engineering, which was building a computer with the backing of Nikita Khrushchev, and Mahalanobis persuaded the Soviet authorities to supply India with a machine.24 Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors write that some eight months after the Menshikov meeting the Soviet Union and the United Nations agreed to fund India's purchase of a digital computer, which they identify as the British HEC-2M.25 Soviet correspondence on the matter went directly to Mahalanobis rather than through diplomatic channels, which irritated the Indian ambassador in Berne, Y.D. Gundevia. It was not, Menon notes, the first time Mahalanobis had trodden on diplomatic toes.26

A computer without support

The first electronic digital computer to work in India was British, and it came on hard terms. Mahalanobis ordered it from the British Tabulating Machine Company (BTM), which sold keypunches, sorters and mechanical calculators in India. 'BTM agreed to sell a computer to the ISI in 1954 though without any technical support,' Rajaraman writes. 'The ISI was to install and maintain the machine using its own scientists and engineers.'27 His account of the purchase draws on the memoir of one of the engineers involved.28 In a later interview he gave the company's reason: BTM was not willing to install and maintain a machine in India because only one was coming to the country.29

So ISI sent two engineers, Mohi (Monimohan) Mukherjee and Amresh Roy, to England in December 1954 to watch the machine being made. The computer was the HEC-2M, the Hollerith Electronic Computer Model 2M. It had been designed by A.D. Booth at Birkbeck College in London and was assembled in BTM's workshop, and the two men gathered there the knowledge they would need to install and keep it running.30 Menon gives the price as 18,500 pounds sterling, paid by the Government of India, which lent the machine to ISI. He describes three metal cabinets, six feet high, ten feet long and seven feet wide in all, and says the machine took nine months to build, three to ship and two to assemble.31

When it reached Calcutta is disputed. Rajaraman, following Mukherjee's memoir, gives July 1955 and says the machine was working in August 1955.32 Menon, working from archival sources, writes that it arrived in two crates in early 1956.33 The book follows the engineer's account for the date of installation and notes that a historian working from ISI's papers dates the arrival later.

The HEC-2M was a small machine even by the standards of the day. Its memory was a magnetic drum, a rotating cylinder that stored 1,024 words of 24 bits each, and it had to be programmed in machine language, in binary code, with punched cards for input and output.34 'Around a dozen people learnt to program the computer which was used for some statistical calculations,' Rajaraman writes.35 It also handled calculation requests from the Planning Commission, the National Sample Survey and other scientific institutions.36 And it was hard to live with. At ISI, in Rajaraman's words, the 'HEC-2M was found to be a very difficult machine to use'.37

The dozen people who learned to program it make up most of India's computing profession in the mid-1950s. 'In 1955 only a few dozen scientists and engineers in India knew about computers,' Rajaraman writes.38 There were no courses to take. What they knew came from watching machines being assembled abroad and from operating manuals.

The URAL and the Russian manuals

Mahalanobis started looking for a second machine, and the obstacle was money from abroad. It was difficult to get funds, Rajaraman notes, 'particularly foreign exchange funds'.39 A computer had to be paid for in dollars or pounds, not rupees. The solution again came from the Soviet side. A URAL-1 computer was ordered in 1958 with United Nations assistance.40

The URAL was a first-generation machine built with vacuum tubes. It had a drum memory, punched-tape input and output, and a magnetic tape unit; its words were 40 bits long and it could perform about 12,000 operations a second.41 It was installed by Russian engineers, eight of them according to both Menon and Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors.42 'All the manuals were in Russian and the ISI engineers learnt Russian to enable them to maintain the machine,' Rajaraman writes.43 The machine was unveiled in Calcutta in December 1958, and the Times of India reported, in Menon's account, that it could calculate 600 times faster than a single man.44 Like the HEC-2M, it was formally transferred to the Government of India and lent to ISI, with the government keeping, in the words of a 1959 note by Mahalanobis cited by Menon, 'complete freedom to utilize the equipment at their discretion'.45

With two working computers and a group of people who could program and maintain them, ISI was, Rajaraman writes, 'the de-facto National Computer Centre' of India in 1958.46 Its users included universities, the atomic energy establishment and defence laboratories.47 Menon lists about a dozen institutional users, among them Andhra University, the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore, IIT Kharagpur, TIFR and the Atomic Energy Commission.48

The URAL's origins show how far Indian computing in the 1950s depended on the Cold War. The machine was designed by the Soviet engineer Bashir Rameyev and produced at Penza.49 India took a British machine with no support and a Soviet one with eight engineers and Russian manuals, because its attempt to obtain the American machine it wanted had been hampered by Cold War suspicion. The country's non-alignment allowed it to buy from both sides, but on terms the sellers set.

The same thinness of expertise explains one of the stranger episodes of the period. In February 1959 Morton Nadler, a 37-year-old American engineer, flew from Prague to Calcutta to take a two-year contract at ISI.50 Nadler had worked at Czechoslovakia's Institute of Mathematical Machines and, by Menon's account, was under suspicion of espionage by both the American and the Czechoslovak authorities. He had joined the Communist Party in 1936, lost a radio-engineering job in Chicago over his politics, moved to Prague in 1948 and renounced his US citizenship after the embassy seized his passport in 1950. He grew disillusioned with communism, in Menon's account; Nadler's own autobiography names the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising in 1956 as the final straw. He could neither leave Czechoslovakia easily nor go home, and Mahalanobis's offer gave him a way out.51 At ISI he worked mainly as a consultant to the Computer Laboratory. His lectures there in the spring of 1959 became a book, Topics in Engineering Logic, published in 1962, and in 1960 he left for the French computer maker Bull in Paris.52 The episode shows how scarce such expertise was, and how far Mahalanobis would go to find it.

Bombay decides to build

The physicist Homi Bhabha founded TIFR in Bombay in 1945, and the Atomic Energy Establishment in 1949.53 Computing work began there in 1954 in the institute's Instrumentation Group, and towards the end of that year TIFR decided, as Narasimhan put it, 'to design and build a fullscale, general purpose, electronic digital computer using contemporary technology'.54 Narasimhan led the project. He credited Bhabha's 'acceptance and active support' as essential to it, and named the physicists Vikram Sarabhai and M.G.K. Menon as his other benefactors.55

Narasimhan had a bachelor's degree in engineering with honours from the College of Engineering, Guindy, in Madras (Chennai since 1996), awarded in 1947. He spent 1948 to 1954 in the United States, supported by a part-loan scholarship from the J.N. Tata Endowment, before returning to join TIFR.56 He was, as he noted, the only member of the team who had been abroad. The others had studied in India, most of them physics. With no one who had built a computer, the team worked in two stages: first a small pilot machine, to try out circuit and logic designs and to train the team on the job, and then a full-scale computer.57

The pilot, which Narasimhan called a 'digital calculator', was completed in September 1956 and kept running for about a year.58 (His 1999 memoir gives October or November 1956; this book follows the paper he wrote in 1960.)59 Design of the full-scale machine began early in 1957, and its final assembly was completed in February 1959.60 Then came the long wait for cool air with which this chapter began.

What the team built was a parallel, binary, asynchronous machine that stored its own program, with single-address instructions and a memory of 1,024 words held in ferrite cores, tiny magnetic rings that each stored one bit.61 Its words were 40 bits long. Programs and data went in on punched paper tape of the kind used by teleprinters, and results came out printed or punched.62 Apart from two bias supplies, no circuit in it used a transistor.63 It could add two numbers in 45 microseconds and multiply them in about 500.64 The URAL in Calcutta, by Rajaraman's figure, managed about 12,000 operations a second, a roughly comparable speed.65 TIFR's archive records that the memory was later doubled to 2,048 words.66 In the account of the book Against All Odds, P.V.S. Rao, a member of TIFR's computer group, described how they strung the memory matrix together themselves, threading hair-thin enamelled wire through magnetic cores less than a millimetre across, held in Plexiglass frames.67

They did not work entirely alone. 'The TIFRAC control logic design benefited crucially through design details kindly made available by the ILLIAC team,' Narasimhan wrote in 1999, referring to the computer group at the University of Illinois.68 This matters for one of the arguments about this period. American knowledge was not simply withheld from India. Some of it was shared, scientist to scientist, even while Mahalanobis's talks about a UNIVAC were failing.

A name, and a verdict

The machine entered routine service in February 1960 and received its name two years later. When Nehru inaugurated TIFR's new building in 1962, he named the computer TIFRAC.69 TIFR's archive and Rajaraman expand the name as 'TIFR Automatic Calculator'. Narasimhan, writing in 1999, gave it as 'TIFR Automatic Computer'.70 The difference is small, but 'calculator' was the more modest word.

TIFRAC ran from 1960 until early 1964, and by the end it was working two shifts a day.71 Its users were TIFR's own scientists, the Atomic Energy Establishment and some universities, including a crystallography group in Madras. The analysis of TIFR's cosmic-ray air-shower experiments, Narasimhan wrote, would not have been feasible without it.72 Its programming support was an interpretive routine for floating-point arithmetic, and its storage was too small to attempt a compiler, the program that translates instructions written in a human-readable language into machine code.73

Narasimhan's judgement of his own machine was unsentimental. The pilot, he wrote in 1999, had been close to the state of the art in 1954, and the 1957 design not very far behind it. But by the time it was commissioned in 1960, TIFRAC was 'an obsolete first generation machine'.74 The second generation of computers, built with transistors, needed production know-how that only large manufacturers had.75 A research institute could build one machine. It could not build the next generation, and it could not build many.

What TIFRAC left behind was people. The computer section had grown from about half a dozen members to some two dozen. A 'hard core of specialists' could now design digital equipment, and a group had learned to maintain complex electronic systems.76 And, in Narasimhan's words, 'many of the computer users in India, handling sophisticated and advanced computational techniques, had their first introduction to programming through the use of TIFRAC.'77

The first to leave

Some of those people left. Between 1959 and 1962 about half a dozen of the scientists who had designed and built TIFRAC took visiting appointments at American universities. Three of them settled permanently in the United States. One returned in 1962 after two years on a second-generation computer project at Illinois, and others came back later.78 Narasimhan himself spent the early 1960s as a visiting scientist at the Digital Computer Laboratory of the University of Illinois.79

This is the earliest documented case in this book of computer engineers trained in India being drawn to the United States. The numbers were tiny. Three people are not a brain drain. But the pattern would recur at much larger scale. India trained engineers at public expense, often on projects that could not employ them for long, and the United States offered the next stage of their careers. Chapter 13 follows that pattern into the era of the H-1B visa.

From building to buying

The engineers who went to America also helped TIFR decide what to do next. Because some of its staff now had working knowledge of American machines, TIFR could set up an expert committee, chaired by Narasimhan, to assess the large commercial systems on offer. It compared the IBM 7094, the CDC 3600, the Philco 211 and the Univac 1107, and recommended the CDC 3600, of which only one was then in operation, at its maker's plant in Minneapolis.80 Rajaraman calls it 'a bold decision'.81

The machine was bought for $1.5 million, with funds provided by the United States Agency for International Development; Bhabha, Rajaraman writes, persuaded the Government of India to arrange the funding.82 The CDC 3600-160A was installed at TIFR in mid-1964, and from October that year it ran as a National Computation Centre, maintained and operated entirely by TIFR staff once the manufacturer's early support ended.83 Over 150 institutions from across India used it for large-scale calculations for more than a decade.84 According to TIFR's own reports, as cited by Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors, it was available more than 98 per cent of the time between 1965 and 1970.85 In the first year users had the machine free of charge. The only demand on them was that they write and debug their own programs, and to help them TIFR's staff programmers ran 'many short courses on programming'.86 The choice also settled a quiet contest. ISI, with two working computers and a decade of experience, had been, in the words of Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors, a strong contender to become India's first national computation centre. TIFR, with the newer and larger American machine, won it.87

The irony is plain. The institute that had built India's first computer now bought an American computer with American aid money, and the scientist who had led the building chose it. Narasimhan saw no contradiction. In his account, once second-generation machines required production know-how that only large manufacturers had, TIFR was 'wise' to look to the world market.88

The end of building, for now

ISI tried once more to build. In the early 1960s (Rajaraman dates the decision to 1960, when TIFRAC was inaugurated) ISI decided to build a transistorised computer jointly with Jadavpur University in Calcutta. The machine, called ISIJU, was completed in 1966, but in Rajaraman's words 'It was not a successful project as the computer was unreliable and did not have appropriate software.'89 Gopalakrishnan and his co-authors record practical troubles: the United Nations did not supply a card reader and line printer free of charge, and fluctuations in the electricity supply disrupted its link to other equipment.90

By then the country's stock of computers had grown, slowly. Menon counts 15 digital computers in India by the end of 1964.91 Rajaraman records that between 1961 and 1964 twelve computers were installed in research and development organisations and two in educational institutions.92 In the autumn of 1965 the Planning Commission opened its own computer centre in Delhi, an IBM 1620 bought with a Ford Foundation grant of Rs 2.3 million (Rs 23 lakh), which Menon's journal article gives as $462,000. It ran 'open shop', available to outside users, on a no-profit, no-loss basis, one shift a day until 1968.93 The planners who had once queued for time on ISI's machines now had one of their own, made by IBM. The reviewer Angela Saini writes that Banerjee's 2026 history (discussed below) finds IBM's dominance of Indian computing 'nearly absolute' by the mid-1960s.94

Policy without a policy

The Indian state had a view about science, but not yet about computers. In March 1958 the Government of India adopted a Scientific Policy Resolution. It declared that 'Science and technology can make up for deficiencies in raw materials by providing substitutes, or, indeed, by providing skills which can be exported in return for raw materials.' Among its aims was to begin 'with all possible speed, programmes for the training of scientific and technical personnel'.95 Read with hindsight, the first sentence describes what India would later sell: skills, exported in return for what the country lacked.

Beyond that resolution, Rajaraman notes, 'there was no government policy on computers'.96 The state paid for the institutes, lent them the machines and, through Nehru, lent them prestige. It did not plan an industry. The machines were scientific instruments, and the people who used them were scientists. A national committee on electronics was appointed only in 1963, under Bhabha, after the 1962 war with China. Chapter 3 tells that story.97

Two explanations

Why did these beginnings not lead to an Indian computer industry? Two explanations are on offer, and the evidence supports parts of both.

The first is Narasimhan's own. Building a first-generation machine was within the reach of a talented research group, and TIFR did it. Building second-generation machines, and building them in numbers, required production know-how and investment that only large manufacturers had.98 TIFR's decision to buy the CDC 3600 was, on this view, a wise one, and the real product of the TIFRAC years was trained people.

The second comes from Dwaipayan Banerjee, an associate professor of science, technology and society at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), whose book Computing in the Age of Decolonization was published by Princeton University Press in February 2026. It has not been read for this book. What follows rests on the publisher's description and on a summary published by MIT News, both consulted in October 2026, and on three reviews published in 2026.99 According to those accounts, Banerjee argues that India's attempt from the 1950s to the 1980s to build a self-reliant computing industry was blocked by Cold War politics, trade imbalances, the monopolies of American corporations and the preference of India's technocratic elite for profitable technical services. Among the constraints he identifies are limits on the flow of American designs, a foreign-exchange crisis in 1958 that made larger memories unaffordable, and rescue loans led by the World Bank that were conditional on opening markets to Western capital. 'This is very much an external constraint story,' he is quoted as saying.100 One of the reviews adds a detail that bears directly on this chapter. Tracie Farrell, reviewing the book in the International Journal of Asian Studies, writes that it contrasts IBM with Control Data Corporation, the maker of TIFR's CDC 3600, which 'had invited Indian engineers to train using their best technology and even proposed to bring some manufacturing of memory to India, a long-standing hope of TIFR'. She quotes the book's judgement that CDC was 'treating Indian researchers as peers'.101

The two accounts overlap more than they disagree. Both see the 1950s as the moment when India learned that it could build a computer but not a computer industry. They differ on the cause: the economics of manufacturing in one account, the politics of the Cold War and of aid in the other. The record in this chapter contains evidence for both. Mahalanobis could not buy a UNIVAC, partly because of a diplomatic dispatch. His second machine came on Soviet terms. TIFR's next machine was bought with American aid. Yet the ILLIAC team in Illinois shared design details with TIFR; CDC, on Farrell's account of Banerjee, treated TIFR's researchers as peers; and Narasimhan, who knew the Americans' work at first hand, chose to buy rather than build because he judged that second-generation machines needed production know-how that only large manufacturers had. Later chapters return to this debate, which runs through the book's first central question: why India came to sell services rather than make machines.

Who learned, and who worked

The education of India's first computer people was informal and tiny. The sources read for this chapter record no computer course anywhere in the country before the machines arrived. Mukherjee and Roy learned by watching the HEC-2M being built in London. Around a dozen people at ISI then learned to program it.102 ISI's engineers learned Russian in order to read the URAL's manuals.103 Nadler's lectures at ISI in the spring of 1959 were an early instance of teaching in the subject.104 At TIFR, a team of physics graduates learned by building.105 By Rajaraman's estimate only a few dozen scientists and engineers in India knew about computers in 1955.106

The profession was a scientific civil service. Computer work meant a salaried post at a state-funded institute, such as ISI, TIFR or the Atomic Energy Establishment, working on statistics, physics and planning. There were no private employers of programmers, no market for computer services and no exports. The sources read for this chapter record almost nothing about the pay, families or backgrounds of the people who did the work, and no woman appears in them as a computer worker. The only labour-market signal of the period was an outflow: three of TIFRAC's builders settling in the United States.107

Coda: from building machines to teaching people

By 1966 the experiment of the 1950s had reached its end. ISI's own machine had failed, TIFRAC had been retired, and the best computer in the country was American. The people who had learned their trade on TIFRAC and the URAL were the asset that remained, and there were not many of them.

The next step was to train more, and to do it in classrooms rather than workshops. Chapter 2 tells how a new campus at Kanpur, in Uttar Pradesh, became the place where India first taught computing as a subject, and where the engineer factory began.
