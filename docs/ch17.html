The Engineer Factory

Chapter 17. The product question (1986-2024)

In 1992 Rajesh Hukku, then head of Citicorp Overseas Software, the bank's in-house programming unit in India, spun the unit off from the bank as a company of its own. Fifteen years later, in an interview with Ravi Aron of the Wharton School published in March 2007, he remembered one of the Citibank chiefs asking him, at the start, a blunt question: 'Why should I start the one-thousand-and-first software company in India?'1 His answer, as he recalled it, was that the new firm would be the first to be wholly focused on understanding its customers' business, so that no banker could say of its staff, 'These guys are great techies, but they don't understand my business.'2

In Hukku's account, Citi had decided that writing software for sale was not its business. It invested $400,000 in the venture anyway, and the unit became Citicorp Information Technology Industries Ltd. (CITIL), later renamed i-flex solutions.3 The bank, as he remembered it, liked the team but would give the new firm no 'management mind-share', and asked one thing more: 'Please don't come back asking for business from Citibank.'4 In hindsight, he said, it was the best combination, 'because you get the money and you don't get controlled'.5

The question the Citibank chief put to Hukku is the question of this chapter, turned round. By 1992 India already had hundreds of software companies, and almost all of them sold the same thing: the time of Indian engineers, billed to foreign clients by the hour, the month or the project. A software product is something different. It is a program that a firm owns and sells many times over, so that the cost of writing it is paid once and the revenue keeps coming. The country that became the world's largest supplier of software labour built remarkably few products of its own. Why that happened, and what changed when it did, is the subject here.

The question matters beyond national pride. A firm that owns a product keeps the gains when its engineers become more productive. A firm that sells hours must negotiate with its clients over who keeps them. That difference decides how the tools of artificial intelligence, which make a programmer's hour more productive, land on the two kinds of firm.

An accounting package for small business

The longest-lived Indian software product grew from the home market. According to the history page on its own website, Tally Solutions began in 1986 as 'Peutronics Financial Accountant', founded by Bharat Goenka, 'inspired by his father, Shyam Sundar Goenka's vision to simplify business operations'.6 The company says the firm grew 'from an accounting package for SMEs', small and medium enterprises, into business software for firms of all sizes.7 Its timeline dates 'code-less accounting' to Tally 3.0 in 1988 and the formal establishment of Tally Solutions Private Limited to 1991.8

What the timeline shows clearly is how closely the product followed the Indian state's tax law rather than any foreign client's needs. Tally 6.3, in 1999, added support for several currencies and more advanced inventory management. Tally 7.2, in 2005, 'introduced VAT compliance, aiding businesses with tax regulations'. In 2017 the company launched software compliant with the new Goods and Services Tax (GST), and in 2020 a new generation of the product, TallyPrime.9 Its buyers were small and medium Indian businesses, and it reached them not through onsite consultants but through a network of partners. It says it is now trusted by more than 2.5 million businesses.10

Tally's model was the opposite of the export firms' in almost every respect. It sold a packaged product, not people. Its customers were Indian, not American. It earned in rupees from small businesses rather than in dollars from large ones. Its survival over four decades is evidence that a domestic market for software existed through the years when the export industry was growing much faster. Its modest public profile beside Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) or Infosys is a sign of how small that market was. Goenka himself would become a public critic of how the industry's institutions favoured services.

Selling to Africa first

i-flex took a different road, out of India and into the world, but not to the countries everyone else was going to. Hukku had studied electrical and electronics engineering at the Birla Institute of Technology and Science (BITS) at Pilani in Rajasthan, a desert campus where, he recalled, students with no television built their own radio station, and had worked for TCS, the export firm F.C. Kohli built, before joining Citicorp's in-house unit.11

The new company began poor, selling professional services and consulting and spending as little as it could.12 It could not afford to chase clients in Britain or America. 'We decided early on that we were not going to go to the U.K. or the U.S. because we couldn't even afford the airfare. We said, "Let's go to Africa. That's all we can handle right now."'13 The firm took the rights to an older Citicorp banking program called MicroBanker, improved it and sold it to African banks. By 1995, by Hukku's account, MicroBanker was number one in the league tables, sold to 117 banks, most of them in Africa.14 He remembered a billboard in Nairobi in those years: 'Wanted. IT people, MicroBanker experience essential.'15

The services work and the old product paid for a new one. In Hukku's words, 'The money we were making on the services, consulting, MicroBanker, etc., was used to fund the creation of our dream product', which was FLEXCUBE, launched in 1997, an integrated banking package covering consumer, corporate, investment and internet banking and asset management.16 This is the most important detail in his account for the argument of this chapter. India's best-known early global product was not built in spite of services. It was paid for by them.

Hukku's way of teaching his engineers the business they were automating was itself unusual. 'Right from the beginning, we said 10% to 15% of the staff have to be bankers, because how else will I learn the domain?'17 The bankers came not from Wall Street but from Indian banks, among them Bank of India and the Reserve Bank of India, and many of them were not computer science graduates. The company asked them to teach.18 In an industry whose training turned graduates into programmers for foreign clients' systems, i-flex was turning programmers into people who understood banking.

Two of the company's choices ran against the industry as a whole. One concerned markets. Hukku saw that the services business depended on America. 'We said that's a very big limitation for the whole industry to be dependent on one country.'19 By 2007 i-flex claimed customers in more than 125 countries.20 The other choice concerned the Y2K boom, the rush to fix two-digit dates in old programs before the year 2000, which brought Infosys about a fifth of its revenue at its height (Chapter 11). 'We're perhaps the only IT company from India who made $0 from Y2K,' Hukku claimed, looking back.21

FLEXCUBE climbed quickly. On Hukku's reading of an annual league table compiled by International Banking Systems, a British research firm, it was 16th of 60 or 70 core banking products in 1997, second in 1999 and first 'since 2002'.22 By early 2007 it had been sold to more than 300 banks in 120 countries.23 i-flex went public in 2002 on the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange.24 By the interviewer's account its shares doubled while almost every other technology stock fell in the dot-com bust, and Hukku put that down to the business model: 'We were a balanced product and services company with a globally hedged customer base.'25

The ending was a sale. In 2005 Oracle bought Citicorp's stake: 42 per cent for $592 million, in Wharton's introduction to the interview, or 41 per cent, held by Citigroup Venture Capital International, in Oracle's own announcement of 2 August 2005, which stated no price.26 Either way, Hukku said, 'No one at the time, including me, ever dreamed that the $400,000 they put in would become almost $600 million in 13 years.'27

Hukku was clear that i-flex had never been a pure product company. 'We are a product company. We're also a services company,' he said.28 As with Cognizant (Chapter 15), a foreign company's captive centre in India had become an independent firm, but this one sold a product, and its largest exit was to an American buyer.

A product inside the factory

Infosys, the archetype of the services firm, built a banking product of its own. Its annual report to American investors for the year to March 2002 said that, besides its IT services, it marketed and licensed 'our own centralized bank automation software application, known as FINACLE(TM)'.29 It described Finacle as 'a fully web-enabled, integrated core banking solution that addresses the retail, corporate and trade finance activities of a bank'.30

The numbers show where the product sat in the firm. In the year to March 2009 Infosys told investors that it had earned 3.9 per cent of its total revenue from licensing software products, against 3.6 per cent the year before. 'The markets for our primary suite of software products which we call Finacle TM are competitive,' it added, and the costs of developing new products 'may not be recouped'.31 Three years later the share was a little higher but still small: 4.6 per cent in the year to March 2012.32

The product was also sold with services wrapped round it. 'We also provide a core banking software solution, Finacle TM, for the banking industry and provide customization and implementation services around this solution,' the company said in 2009.33 By March 2012, Infosys said, Finacle was used by 160 banks in 78 countries.34

One detail of the 2009 report shows how a services firm thought about its product. The director responsible for Finacle, T.V. Mohandas Pai, who had been chief financial officer from 1994 to 2006, was listed as head of 'Administration, Education and Research, Finacle, Human Resources Development' and the Infosys Leadership Institute.35 The banking product shared a portfolio with the training machine. Finacle, like FLEXCUBE, was never a pure product business. It was a product nested inside a services company, contributing less than a twentieth of revenue.

A round table of its own

By 2013 some of the product companies had decided that the industry's lobby did not speak for them. The National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, had represented the industry for about 25 years, as it grew from $100 million in 1992 to $108 billion in 2012.36 In March 2013 Knowledge at Wharton reported that a group of 30 software product companies had come together to form the Indian Software Product Industry Round Table, known as iSPIRT, a think-tank for the products segment.37

It was a spin-off rather than a walk-out. The round table grew out of Nasscom's own annual product conclave, and its founders were Nasscom members, among them Goenka of Tally and Sharad Sharma.38 Goenka told the business daily Business Standard: 'At Nasscom, the product companies do not have the same weightage as the services companies. The policy interventions Nasscom is trying to make are more in favor of the services industry.'39 Sharma said: 'We believe that with this think-tank, we will get a louder voice in policy making and other initiatives.'40

Nasscom played the split down. Its chairman, N. Chandrasekaran, who was also chief executive of TCS, said: 'When you are on a growth paradigm, you cannot do everything that you want to do. I don't see any conflict.'41 The association had set up an expert committee the previous July, chaired by N.R. Narayana Murthy, co-founder of Infosys, to chart a roadmap for the industry. Murthy said the industry should aim to reach $300 billion by 2020.42 Of that, the committee suggested, the internet and mobile could account for $100 billion, the domestic market $75 billion, and software products 'around US$10 billion'.43 Products were on the list, at about one-thirtieth of the target.

The round table did lobby for its members. In January 2019 it wrote to the prime minister on behalf of start-ups about the 'angel tax', asking that investments of up to Rs 10 crore a year from resident investors in recognised start-ups be exempt, and claiming that the number of angel investors had fallen 48 per cent since 2015.44 But by then it was best known for something else. It describes itself as 'a non-profit think tank which champions digital public goods', and maintains the website of India Stack, a set of public digital services built around Aadhaar.45 Inc42, a start-up news site, described it in 2023 as 'building the India Stack since 2013'.46 That closeness to the state drew criticism. In 2017 Sharma, a member of its governing council, admitted to anonymously trolling critics of Aadhaar on Twitter and apologised.47 The round table formed to speak for product companies had become, above all, an advocate of public platforms.

The village office

One Indian firm set out to make products from the start. Sridhar Vembu graduated from the Indian Institute of Technology Madras in 1989, took a doctorate at Princeton and worked as a wireless systems engineer at Qualcomm in San Diego. In 1996 he co-founded AdventNet with members of his family and friends, among them Tony Thomas, then at Bell Labs.48 The company's own timeline begins: 'AdventNet Inc. is born in New Jersey, USA. It all begins with Network Management.'49 Its first customers, Vembu recalled in 2010, were makers of network equipment such as Cisco.50 He told Forbes India in 2020 what he had seen at the start: 'in India we have perfect talent but we produce almost zero software products.'51

On the company's account, the products followed one another: a sister brand, ManageEngine, in 2003, then online applications under the Zoho name, starting with a word processor and a customer relationship management (CRM) program in 2005. In 2009 AdventNet renamed itself Zoho Corporation.52 It has never taken outside capital, by its own and its founder's statements. 'We have never taken any outside money,' Vembu said in 2010, and the company's website still says it has 'never taken money from investors'.53 Forbes India reported that in 2019 Zoho made profits of Rs 516 crore on revenue of Rs 3,410 crore (Rs 34.1 billion, about $480 million at that year's average rate).54

In 2011, the company says, it opened an office in Tenkasi, a district in southern Tamil Nadu, and in 2018 another in Renigunta in Andhra Pradesh.55 By 2020, Forbes India reported, 500 of its 9,300 employees, 8,800 of them in India, worked from those two rural offices, and Vembu himself had moved to Tenkasi in October 2019. 'I always thought that people migrating from villages to cities is not a good idea,' he said.56

Zoho also built its own school. Zoho University, which the company dates to 2005 and describes as 'an experiment in providing an alternative to the conventional college education', paid its students a stipend rather than charging fees, and by 2020, Forbes India reported, 875 of the firm's 9,300 staff had come through it.57 Vembu said that 15 to 20 per cent of Zoho's engineers had no engineering degree.58 One graduate, Saran Babu Paramasivam, told Forbes India that in 2005 he could not operate a computer; by 2020 he was a senior product manager, and he said 'a degree is not important anymore'.59 Where the services firms recruited engineering graduates by the hundred thousand and billed their time, Zoho trained many of its own engineers and put their work into products it owned. When those engineers became more productive, the gain stayed with Zoho.

The warehouse in Chennai

The next generation of Indian product companies did not sell boxes of software to shopkeepers or core banking systems to banks. They sold subscriptions over the internet, software as a service (SaaS), mostly to small businesses abroad. The best documented is Freshworks, because it listed its shares in the United States and had to explain itself to American investors.

Its founder and chief executive, Girish Mathrubootham, began his letter in the 2021 registration statement with its origins: 'We started in a 700 square foot warehouse in Chennai in 2010. Our simple idea was to create a "fresh" helpdesk in response to a poor customer service experience I had.'60 He listed the doubts he had heard along the way. Among them were 'You can't find talent in Chennai; you can't win unless you move to Bangalore' and 'You really can't build a global SaaS company from India.'61

The company's model needed none of the apparatus of the services industry. 'Freshworks was global from "day one" with our first few customers coming from four different continents,' Mathrubootham wrote. 'Our initial sales came through search-driven online acquisition, powered by robust product-led growth.'62 Customers found the software by searching the web, tried it and paid for it, without a salesman on a plane. There were no onsite engineers, no visas, no time sheets and no client billing by the hour. By mid-2021, the company said, businesses from more than 120 countries used its products, and it had about 52,500 paying customers.63

It also lost money as it grew. Its revenue was $172.4 million in 2019 and $249.7 million in 2020, a rise of 45 per cent, and its net losses in those years were $31.1 million and $57.3 million.64 The work was done in India. As of July 2021, Freshworks said, it had about 4,300 employees, and about 3,800 of them were in India, 'where most of engineering, product design, sales and marketing, customer support, and general and administrative personnel are located'. Its headquarters were in San Mateo, California.65

The rivalry between two Chennai product firms reached an American court. On 17 March 2020 Zoho sued Freshworks in the Northern District of California, alleging the misappropriation of trade secrets and violations of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.66 In October 2020 the judge refused to dismiss the amended complaint, ruling that Zoho had 'clearly met the standard for pleading trade secret misappropriation', a ruling on the pleading rather than a finding of fact.67 In December 2021 the companies settled and Zoho dismissed the suit. Freshworks's announcement and its annual report gave no terms. Freshworks said that a now-former sales employee had 'wrongfully accessed and used Zoho confidential information relating to sales leads', and that he had not acted at the company's direction.68

By then Freshworks was a public company. On 22 September 2021 the final prospectus fixed the price: 'The initial public offering price is $36.00 per share.' The company offered 28,500,000 shares of Class A common stock for listing on the Nasdaq Global Select Market, about $1.03 billion before any extra shares the underwriters might buy.69

The listing settled something that the Chennai warehouse had left open. The most celebrated Indian software-as-a-service company was, legally and financially, an American one: incorporated in Delaware, headquartered in San Mateo and listed in New York, with its engineers in India.70 The value it created was priced in dollars on an American exchange. Like i-flex's sale to Oracle, the reward for building a product in India was realised abroad.

Money arrives, and leaves

In Hukku's account, the shortage that had mattered most in 1992 was money. 'Even in 1992, there was not enough capital,' he said in 2007. By then, he believed, the position had reversed. 'So capital is not a problem today.'71

Fourteen years later capital arrived on a scale far beyond anything he described, and much of it went to consumer internet businesses rather than to software products in his sense. According to figures from Tracxn, a data firm, reported by The Week in January 2025, Indian start-ups raised $25.7 billion in 2022, a fall of 31 per cent on the year before, which implies about $37 billion in 2021.72 On Tracxn's count, funding then fell by close to 58 per cent in 2023, to $10.8 billion, and rose by about 2 per cent in 2024, to $11.1 billion.73 The emblem of the bust was Byju's, an education technology company, which by mid-2024 faced insolvency proceedings brought by the Board of Control for Cricket in India over unpaid dues.74

Sidebar: Why was India's most-used software never sold?

By the measure of daily use, the most successful Indian software of the 2010s was not sold by a private firm at all. Aadhaar, the biometric identity number, is one case (Chapter 16). The other is the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), a system run by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) that lets a person pay another directly from a bank account using a phone.

The Reserve Bank of India's statistics show how fast it grew. In the year to March 2017, the year in which UPI was introduced, it carried about 18 million transactions, worth about Rs 6,961 crore (Rs 69.6 billion).75 Seven years later, in the year to March 2024, it carried 131.13 billion transactions, worth about Rs 199.95 lakh crore (Rs 199.95 trillion, roughly $2.4 trillion at that year's average rate).76 All India Radio, reporting NPCI's figures, said that in December 2024 alone UPI processed about 16.73 billion transactions worth around Rs 23.25 lakh crore (Rs 23.25 trillion), with the number of transactions about 39 per cent higher than a year earlier.77

No private Indian software product in this chapter came near that scale of use. Tally counts its users in millions of businesses, by its own claim; Freshworks counted its paying customers in tens of thousands.78 UPI counts its transactions in billions each month.

Part of the answer lies in who built it. India Stack's own timeline, on the website iSPIRT maintains, credits the launch of UPI in 2016 to NPCI, and the identity and electronic-signature services beside it to other public bodies.79 The press gives the volunteers a larger part: Inc42 counted UPI among the projects iSPIRT 'has been behind'.80 Either way the builders came from two worlds. The institutions were public, but some of the people around them came from the software industry: Nandan Nilekani, the Infosys co-founder who chaired the identity authority (Chapter 16), was a mentor to iSPIRT.81

The rest of the answer, this book argues, lies in what a payments network is. Such a network is worth most when every bank can join it and every phone can use it. A private firm that built one would have had reason to charge for it or close it to rivals. A body that the banks answered to could bring them all in at once.

That is a different answer to the product question. Where private Indian firms built products, they either sold them to small Indian businesses, as Tally did, or sold them abroad and realised their value abroad, as i-flex and Freshworks did. UPI was built for Indians, used at home, and run as infrastructure rather than sold as a product. The country's most-used software was the one piece that no one could sell.

Products and people

The 'services mindset' is the most common explanation in India for why the country built so few products. It is the participants' own. Hukku said Indian software companies had followed the model F.C. Kohli pioneered at TCS in the 1970s, 'of exporting the Indian brain, or doing software services projects offshore'. Kohli had worked very hard to make it succeed, Hukku said. 'The not-so-great news was that hundreds of companies that came after copied him. Basically it is a human tendency to take the route which is already a proven success.'82 When Aron asked whether i-flex was 'Made in Spite of India', he replied: 'It's not in spite of India, but it's in spite of whatever was successful when we came into existence.'83 Goenka's complaint about Nasscom, and Nasscom's own roadmap, said the same thing about the industry's institutions.84

These are explanations from people with a stake in them. Several structural explanations fit the same facts. One is capital, which Hukku himself named: there was not enough of it in 1992, and venture funding at scale came only in the following decades.85 Zoho's founders, by their own account, never raised any. A second is the market. Tally's history suggests that small Indian businesses would pay for software when the state's tax rules required it, but no series of Indian spending on software over the period has been located.86 Building a product for India was no guarantee of success either. The Simputer, a low-cost multilingual hand-held computer designed in 2000 by a group from the Indian Institute of Science and Encore Software in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), received worldwide attention but was, in the words of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, 'not a commercial success'.87 A third is talent. The services firms offered graduates secure, well-paid jobs with training attached, and a young product firm had to compete with that. Talent also meant knowledge of the customer's trade. i-flex's answer was to hire bankers to teach its programmers banking, and Zoho's was a school of its own.88 A fourth is the exit. The largest rewards for building products in India were realised through a foreign buyer, as with Oracle and i-flex, or a foreign listing, as with Freshworks.89 A 'mindset' may be the participants' name for these conditions rather than a separate cause. The question stays open.

What can be measured is the difference in scale, and it cuts both ways. At the end of 2024 Freshworks had about 4,400 employees, and approximately 3,700 of them, 83 per cent, lived in India.90 Its revenue that year was $720.4 million, up from $596.4 million in 2023 and $498.0 million in 2022.91 In the same year Cognizant, the American services firm born from a captive centre in India, had about 336,800 employees, 241,500 of them in India.92 The figures are given only to show orders of magnitude. Freshworks earned its revenue with a workforce less than a fiftieth the size of Cognizant's in India.

The debate in India treated products as the higher path, and on revenue per engineer they were. But the engineer factory was turning out graduates in the hundreds of thousands each year. No set of product companies of the size documented here could have employed them. The services model was not only a habit of mind. It was the model in which the industry's growth turned into mass employment, because a firm that sold hours could grow only by hiring more people to sell.

Hukku had made a prediction in 2007: 'I can guarantee you that five years from now, there will be many famous product companies growing out of India.'93 By the early 2020s there were some, and a flood of venture money that peaked in 2021. Yet in those same pandemic years the old model did not shrink. The firms that sold engineers' hours went on hiring, in numbers that no product company could match.

Freshworks's annual report for 2024 shows what happened when the product side met the new technology. On 5 November 2024, the company said, it had committed to a plan to realign its workforce 'that resulted in a 13% reduction in force', at a cost of about $9.7 million.94 The same report described its generative AI offerings. 'Freddy AI Agent offers always-on, autonomous, personalized resolutions to customer and employee queries,' the company told investors, while Freddy AI Copilot provided 'always-on contextual assistance' for support, marketing and sales staff, 'designed to boost productivity'.95 Among its risks it listed the chance that competitors 'may be able to develop new AI offerings that disrupt our customers' needs and negatively impact demand for our products'.96

Read beside Chapter 14, the description is pointed. An Indian-built product company was selling software designed to answer customers' and employees' questions without a person, which is much of what India's call centres and IT help desks had been paid by the hour to do. Products had not escaped the AI question. They met it from the other side, as sellers of the automation that the services firms would have to absorb. It was the services boom, and what it did to the people hired in it, that shaped the lives of most young Indian engineers, and the next chapter follows them.
