The Engineer Factory

Chapter 16. Fraud and foundation (2008-2018)

On 7 January 2009 the board of Satyam Computer Services, a software company with its headquarters in Hyderabad, received a letter from its chairman and founder, B. Ramalinga Raju. It began: 'It is with deep regret, and tremendous burden that I am carrying on my conscience, that I would like to bring the following facts to your notice.' The first fact was that the company's balance sheet at 30 September 2008 carried 'Inflated (non-existent) cash and bank balances of Rs.5,040 crore (as against Rs. 5361 crore reflected in the books).'1 Rs 5,040 crore is Rs 50.4 billion, or about $1.16 billion at the 2008 average exchange rate. On Raju's own account, nearly all the cash the company said it held did not exist.2

Copies went to the chairman of the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), the market regulator, and to the stock exchanges. Satyam filed the letter the same day with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), because its shares also traded in New York.3 By the end of that day, according to the order of the Company Law Board two days later, the share price had fallen from Rs 188 to Rs 38.40.4

Satyam was not a marginal firm. Its own filings called it the fourth-largest Indian IT services company by export revenue.5 Its engineers were recruited, trained and deployed in much the same way as those of Infosys, Wipro and Tata Consultancy Services (TCS). The fraud therefore put a question to the whole industry this book describes. If the books of one of its four largest firms could be false for years, what exactly had clients, investors and graduates been buying? The answer that emerged over the next three months was that the work was real and the accounts were not.

The same month, in an unrelated decision, the government of India created an agency to give every resident a unique identity number. Within six months it had hired an Infosys co-founder to run it.6 Satyam showed what happened when a software company's governance failed. Aadhaar, the identity system, showed what happened when the state borrowed the industry's people and methods for a project of its own, and when the Supreme Court then had to decide what that system could be made to do to Indian citizens.

The company on paper

The last annual report Satyam filed before the letter was its Form 20-F for the year to March 2008, lodged with the SEC that summer. It described a business growing very fast. 'As of March 31, 2008, we had 50,570 employees, whom we refer to as associates, worldwide as compared to 39,018 associates as of March 31, 2007.'7 Revenue for the year was reported as $2,138.1 million, and the company said its revenue had grown at a compound rate of 39.1 per cent a year over three years.8 It had 654 active customers, 'including 185 Fortune Global 500 or Fortune U.S. 500 companies'.9

The 20-F is also a record of how such a company made engineers. 'We recruit graduates from the engineering departments of India's leading universities, engineering and technical colleges and management institutes,' it said. It counted 'over 1,500 such institutions' in India, and described a selection process 'involving a series of tests and interviews'.10 Each new technical recruit, the filing said, went through 'an intensive 12 week initial training program' and then at least 40 hours of training a year.11 The company reported that 47,405 of its associates were technical staff in IT services, and that 9,941 of them were working onsite, at clients' premises abroad.12 Attrition in IT services, the share of staff who left in a year, had been 13.1 per cent in the year to March 2008. 'None of our associates are represented by a union,' the filing said.13

That was the profession Satyam offered: a campus test, three months of company schooling, a project, perhaps a posting abroad. Nothing in it was unusual for the industry in 2008.

The financial pages were where the fiction lay, though no outside reader could see it. The 20-F reported 'cash and cash equivalents of $290.5 million' at 31 March 2008. It explained that cash used in investing in the year to March 2007 had risen 'primarily due to investment in bank deposits amounting to $745.6 million'.14 Raju's letter, written five months after the filing, said that cash and bank balances of Rs 5,361 crore in the books included Rs 5,040 crore that did not exist.15 A company growing at nearly 40 per cent a year, adding more than 11,000 people to its payroll in a year did not look strange from outside. That was the difficulty.

Riding a tiger

The letter, as filed with the SEC, runs to about two pages.16 After the cash, Raju listed 'An accrued interest of Rs. 376 crore which is non-existent', 'An understated liability of Rs. 1,230 crore on account of funds arranged by me', and debtors (money owed by customers) overstated by Rs 490 crore.17 For the quarter to September 2008, he wrote, the company had reported revenue of Rs 2,700 crore and an operating margin of Rs 649 crore, or 24 per cent of revenue. The actual figures were revenue of Rs 2,112 crore and an operating margin of Rs 61 crore, 3 per cent of revenue. In that quarter alone, he said, the false cash and bank balances had grown by Rs 588 crore.18

He explained how it had happened. 'What started as a marginal gap between actual operating profit and the one reflected in the books of accounts continued to grow over the years. It has attained unmanageable proportions as the size of company operations grew significantly.' The company, he wrote, had to carry extra staff and assets to justify the higher level of operations it was reporting, which raised its costs further. Every attempt to close the gap had failed. Because the promoters (the founding family) held only a small share of the equity, he feared that poor results would bring a takeover that would expose the gap.19 Then he wrote: 'It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten.'20

The letter named the event that had brought the crisis into the open. According to the government's petition, as the Company Law Board summarised it, Satyam's board had recently approved a proposal to buy stakes, at huge cost, in two companies controlled by Raju's near relations; the Board itself recorded that four independent directors had resigned once the decision became public.21 Raju called the 'aborted Maytas acquisition deal' 'the last attempt to fill the fictitious assets with real ones'.22

Much of the letter is Raju's defence of himself. He claimed that neither he nor the managing director, his brother B. Rama Raju, had sold shares in eight years except a small number for charity. He wrote that 'Every attempt was made to keep the wheel moving and to ensure prompt payment of salaries to the associates.' He claimed that 'neither me, nor the Managing Director took even one rupee/dollar from the company', and that none of the board members, past or present, had known. He listed seventeen senior executives who, he said, were also unaware.23 These are the claims of the man who had confessed. SEBI later held him, his brother and three officials liable for fraud and insider trading, and an appeals tribunal upheld those findings in 2017.24

He closed with what he had built. 'I have seen it grow from few people to 53,000 people, with 185 Fortune 500 companies as customers and operations in 66 countries.' And: 'I sincerely apologize to all Satyamites and stakeholders'.25 He recommended that the bank Merrill Lynch 'can be entrusted with the task of quickly exploring some Merger opportunities'.26

Two days in January

Merrill Lynch had already gone. The same SEC filing carried a letter from Kevan Watts, president of DSP Merrill Lynch, to the chairman of SEBI, dated 7 January. It said the bank had ended its advisory engagement with Satyam the day before. 'In the course of such engagement, we came to understand that there were material accounting irregularities, which prompted our aforesaid decision.'27 An outside adviser had seen enough to walk away a day before the chairman confessed.

Satyam's own press release that day said its priorities included protecting 'the careers and security of its approximately 53,000 associates'.28 SEBI appointed an investigating authority the same day to examine dealings in Satyam's shares, including possible fraud and insider trading.29

The state then moved quickly. On 8 January the Registrar of Companies for Andhra Pradesh petitioned the Special Judge for Economic Offences in Hyderabad for permission to search Satyam's offices and seize its books, papers and computer records. The judge's order records that the petition was filed 'at 04.30 PM at my residence'. The Registrar, the order says, apprehended that the company appeared to have shown 'artificial and inflated profits for the last several years'. The judge found a prima facie case and allowed the search.30

On 9 January the Union of India petitioned the Company Law Board in New Delhi to remove the directors. The petition, as the Board summarised it, described a listed company with about 300,000 shareholders and more than 53,000 employees, the fourth-largest IT company in India, which had 'received a number of awards for best corporate governance'.31 The Board noted that the promoter group now held less than 4 per cent of the shares and financial institutions about 60 per cent. Its chairman, S. Balasubramanian, wrote that Raju's written admission established 'beyond any shadow of doubt' that there had been 'financial impropriety and jugglery of financial statements'. 'It appears that a serious fraud has been perpetrated on the society as a whole.'32 He suspended the board with immediate effect and authorised the central government to appoint up to ten 'persons of eminence' as directors.33

Two years later the SEC summarised the order bluntly: 'the India government seized control of the company by dissolving its board of directors and appointing new government-nominated directors'.34

The people who did the work

For the engineers, the first question was whether they would be paid. Business Today, a business magazine, sent reporters to Satyam's staff in the weeks after the letter and published their accounts in its issue of 8 February 2009.35 A week after Raju's arrest, the magazine reported, a European client had decided to move a project, with the team working on it, to another Indian vendor. Satyam staff in Europe told the magazine that '95 per cent of the team had agreed to join the competing vendor'.36

The rest of the industry did not rush to hire them. T. Sreedhar, managing director of TMI Network, a recruitment firm in Hyderabad, said his office was overrun by Satyam employees. They were, he said, 'stuck between the devil and the deep sea. They cannot quit the job because there is no option being made available by the large companies (who have chosen not to hire them)'. If they stayed, he added, they could not be sure of being paid that month.37 The magazine reported that Nasscom (the National Association of Software and Service Companies), the industry's lobby group, and Infosys had issued statements against recruiting Satyam's people, 'not because they are tainted but to ensure that the company doesn't suffer because of an exodus'.38 Rishi Das, chief executive of another recruiter, CareerNet Consulting, estimated that about 35,000 Satyam employees, 70 per cent of the workforce, were already looking for jobs with rivals. That was a recruiter's estimate, not a count.39

Some staff were tied in. Insiders told the magazine that about 7,000 employees had been required to give 'Rs 2 lakh bonds' (Rs 200,000) as a deposit under two-year contracts, which they would forfeit if they left.40 The newest recruits were the most exposed. In 2007, the magazine said, Satyam had issued about 6,000 offer letters on campuses across the country. 'Those offers are not worth the paper they are written on.'41

The magazine printed five short first-person accounts, each 'as told to' one of its reporters. N. Kavitha, 22, had studied engineering at the Muthukumaran Institute of Technology near Mangadu, a suburb of Chennai, and had been selected by Satyam in a campus placement in 2007-08. 'I was told that I would get a joining letter by September 2008. This did not happen. The company HR said that I could definitely expect to join in January 2009.' Now, she said, she had lost hope of getting it. But she would still join if it came. 'In this recession, a fresher needs to grab any opportunity. I am not worried too much about Satyam's reputation in the market; I want the work experience first.'42 Her position was close to the one Chapter 14 traces in the collapse of campus offers in 2009: a campus offer was a promise, not a job, and the graduate carried the risk when the company's plans changed.

S. Mastanamma, 24, had worked at Satyam's Chennai centre for two years after graduating from Newton's College of Engineering in Guntur, Andhra Pradesh. 'I am very positive about the company's future,' she said. She praised the project-level leadership, the employee portal and the training, and said human resources had opened a round-the-clock helpline for staff. 'I am not worried about lay-offs. The existing clients have to be serviced and the new board at Satyam is saying that people are needed to continue to service the clients.'43

A consultant in Europe, two years with the firm, who was not named, said the client had arranged to move the whole project team to another vendor. 'We, as employees, are very clear we want to save our livelihoods.' As an employer, the consultant said, Satyam had paid well. 'Raju was always elusive, I have never seen or heard from him in the two years I have been with the firm', except for one mail defending the Maytas deal.44 A retail consultant five months into the job, also unnamed, said: 'There is a bit of uncertainty in every job.'45

In a bracketed note of its own in its issue of 22 February 2009, the magazine reported that employees had started to have their pay credited to their accounts.46 When the board held a press conference in April, the company said that on 20 March 2009, by its internal data, it had about 42,000 employees on a stand-alone basis (the parent company without its subsidiaries).47

Rs 58 a share

The government's nominees took over a company whose clients were still being served and whose cash was gone. The new directors included Kiran Karnik, who became chairman, and Deepak Parekh.48 With Goldman Sachs and Avendus Capital as financial advisers, the board ran an auction under the supervision of Justice S.P. Bharucha. Bidders were judged first on technical criteria, including their record in corporate governance and in managing distressed companies, and then on price.49 On 13 April 2009 the financial bids were opened. They were 'Rs. 58 (Tech Mahindra), Rs. 45.90 (Larsen & Toubro Limited) and Rs. 20 (consortium of WL Ross funds) per share'.50

Venturbay Consultants, a company controlled by Tech Mahindra, agreed to buy 31 per cent of the enlarged share capital at Rs 58 a share, putting Rs 1,756 crore into Satyam, which the company converted at about $351 million at a rate of Rs 50 to the dollar. It would then have to offer to buy at least a further 20 per cent from other shareholders.51 Karnik said the result 'signals a new stage for the Company in its progress towards stabilization and growth'. He hoped it would reassure customers, 'who continue to be happy with Satyam's excellent service delivery'.52 That was a claim by the chairman of a company being sold, but the bids themselves were evidence of a kind. The process, the company noted, 'has reached this significant stage within three months of the new Board's first meeting'.53

The speed of the sale says something about what an Indian services firm was. Its accounts had been falsified for years and its founder had been arrested. Yet within three months two large Indian companies offered real money for control of it. The board told the press that Satyam held about 450 acres of land and that two Hyderabad campuses had been valued at an average of Rs 1,700 crore.54 But it was the engineers who kept the clients, and the clients who made the company worth buying.

The auditors

Satyam's auditors were Indian affiliates of PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC), and the 20-F carried the consent of Price Waterhouse as its independent registered public accounting firm.55 In April 2011 the SEC set out what it found they had done. Five Indian affiliates, which it called PW India, had 'repeatedly' conducted deficient audits of Satyam and enabled 'a massive accounting fraud to go undetected for several years'.56 They agreed to settle and pay 'a $6 million penalty, the largest ever by a foreign-based accounting firm in an SEC enforcement action'. They also agreed not to take new American clients for six months, to train their staff, to revise their audit procedures and to appoint an independent monitor.57

The core finding was simple. When an auditor wants to know whether a company really holds the cash it reports, it writes to the banks and asks them to confirm the balances directly to the auditor. According to the SEC's order, PW India staff 'failed to conduct procedures to confirm Satyam's cash and cash equivalent balances or its accounts receivables'. They 'routinely relinquished control of the delivery and receipt of cash confirmations entirely to their audit clients and rarely, if ever, questioned the integrity of the confirmation responses they received from the client by following up with the banks'.58 The SEC found that this was not limited to Satyam but was 'indicative of a much larger quality control failure throughout PW India'.59 Robert Khuzami, the SEC's director of enforcement, said: 'PW India violated its most fundamental duty as a public watchdog'.60

These were findings in a settled case, not the verdict of a trial. The same day, Satyam itself, now under new management, agreed to settle the SEC's fraud charges, pay a $10 million penalty and carry out internal reforms.61

SEBI's own case against the auditors took nine years. In January 2018 it barred the firms practising under the Price Waterhouse name from certifying the accounts of listed companies for two years, and the two engagement partners on Satyam, S. Gopalakrishnan and Srinivas Talluri, for three. It ordered the partners and the Bangalore firm to disgorge Rs 13.09 crore in fees, with interest.62 On 9 September 2019 the Securities Appellate Tribunal quashed the bans. 'In the instant case, mens rea was not proved,' it held. 'There is no finding that the auditors or the audit firms had knowledge or had intention to connive or collude with the management in the fabrication of the books of account of the company.' It did find a professional lapse, above all the failure to seek direct confirmation of bank balances, which 'amounted to negligence', and it upheld the disgorgement.63 SEBI appealed. In November 2019 the Supreme Court issued notice and stayed the part of the ruling that limited SEBI's power to bar auditors; no decision on that appeal has been found.64 The tribunal was ruling on SEBI's powers under the securities laws; the criminal charges against the two partners were a matter for the criminal courts.

Findings and punishment

The Indian regulators moved more slowly. SEBI passed a final order on 15 July 2014 against Ramalinga Raju, Rama Raju and three officials: V. Srinivas, the former chief financial officer; G. Ramakrishna, a vice-president of finance; and V.S. Prabhakara Gupta, the head of internal audit. It held them liable for fraud and insider trading and barred them from the securities market for fourteen years.65 On 12 May 2017 the Securities Appellate Tribunal upheld SEBI's findings on the merits but sent back the amounts and the length of the bars.66 Its summary of SEBI's investigation is the clearest statement in the record of how the fraud worked: 'Investigation carried out by SEBI revealed that Ramalinga Raju as Chairman and Rama Raju as, MD of Satyam were instrumental in creating fictitious invoices, fictitious receipts etc. on the basis of which fictitious monthly bank statements were prepared'.67

SEBI's fresh order on the officials, in October 2018, recorded that the tribunal had traced their knowledge of the fraud to meetings with the Raju brothers from about 2001, at which, among other things, 'the need to show inflated results to attract customers/employees' was discussed.68 The penalties in that order, and in a companion order of November 2018 on the Raju family, did not stand for those who appealed: on 2 February 2023 the tribunal set both aside and sent the amounts and bars back to SEBI.69 SEBI's new order of 30 November 2023 barred the two brothers from the market until 14 July 2028 and ordered six parties, among them two of the officials, a third brother and the family company, to disgorge about Rs 624 crore in all (Rs 6.24 billion, about $76 million at the 2023 average rate). Because the brothers' and the two officials' appeals against the 2017 ruling were still pending in the Supreme Court, it was to take effect only from a date that court would direct.70

The criminal case ran on a separate track. The three charge-sheets of the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI), India's federal police agency, were tried together, and on 9 April 2015 the XXI Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate-cum-Special Sessions Judge at Nampally, in Hyderabad, convicted ten men and sentenced each to seven years' rigorous imprisonment.71 They were the Raju brothers; B. Suryanarayana Raju, a director of SRSR Advisory Services; V. Srinivas; the two Price Waterhouse partners, Gopalakrishnan and Talluri; and four former employees of the finance department, among them G. Ramakrishna.72 Reports of the fines differ.73

After a dispute over which court should hear their appeals, settled by the Hyderabad High Court on 30 April 2015 in favour of the Metropolitan Sessions Judge at Nampally, that court on 11 May suspended all ten sentences and granted bail pending the appeals.74 The CBI, by its own later account, appealed for heavier sentences, and in June 2022 it told the Telangana High Court that the brothers' appeals and its own were still pending.75 No decision on any of the appeals had been found by October 2026, and in February 2025 the tax department told an income-tax tribunal that no order acquitting Raju existed.76 Each of the ten, the two auditors among them, was therefore convicted by the trial court in April 2015 and had his sentence suspended in May 2015 pending appeal; for the brothers, the appeals were still undecided in June 2022, the latest record of their status.

A number for every resident

On 28 January 2009, three weeks after Raju's letter, the Planning Commission issued a notification creating the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI).77 The project had a longer history, which the Supreme Court later set out from government records. In March 2006 the Department of Information Technology had approved a project called 'Unique Identification for BPL Families', for households below the poverty line, to be carried out by the National Informatics Centre, the government's own computing agency.78 The notification of January 2009 made the new authority an attached office of the Commission, and the Finance Minister approved Rs 147.31 crore for the first phase of enrolment.79

Then the state hired from the industry. By a notification of 2 July 2009, 'Mr. Nandan Nilekani was appointed as the Chairman of UIDAI for an initial tenure of five years in the rank and status of a Cabinet Minister. He assumed charge on July 24, 2009.'80 Nilekani was a co-founder of Infosys and had been a director since 1981; from 1981 to 1987 he had managed its marketing and development in the United States.81 When the Supreme Court decided the case about the system in 2018, Justice A.K. Sikri began the majority judgment with a line from the project's own message: 'It is better to be unique than the best.' Aadhaar, he wrote, 'means, in English, "foundation" or "base"'.82

Building it

The first part of the agency to start work was not in Delhi. 'Appropriately, the Technology Centre was the first unit of UIDAI to start functioning in 2009 in Bangalore,' its director general, Vijay Madan, later wrote in the agency's account of its own architecture.83 Forbes India, which profiled the team in November 2010, described a hybrid: civil servants headed the departments and took the policy decisions, aided by experts from the private sector. Nilekani invited Srikanth Nadhamuni, an engineer who had spent fifteen years in Silicon Valley and with whom he had founded a non-profit in 2003 to help municipalities use IT, to head the technology centre. Raj Mashruwala, who had been ahead of Nilekani at IIT Bombay and had founded companies in manufacturing software in the United States, expected to be needed for three months at most. He ended up renting an apartment in Bangalore that doubled as the technology centre in its early days. Pramod Varma, 'an old Infosys hand', was the project's chief software architect.84 The agency's own document later named Nadhamuni, Mashruwala, Varma, Sanjay Jain, Vivek Raghavan and Jagdish Babu among 'a band of dedicated domain experts' who had worked with the centre.85

They worked on unusual terms. By the magazine's count, of about 1,000 people who had applied in the project's first sixteen months, 23 had come as unpaid volunteers, 14 on sabbatical from their jobs, and '23 took massive pay cuts to join the Project Management Unit'; another 140 had come from the government. Because the volunteers drew no salaries, the magazine noted, the project had started before its budgets were sanctioned.86 An industry that had sold its engineers' time by the hour to foreign clients was here lending some of them to the state for less, or for nothing. The magazine reported that MindTree, a Bangalore software firm, had built the enrolment software.87 The difference between the two worlds was put plainly by K. Ganga, the deputy director general for finance, who came from the government's audit service: 'If an Infosys engineer wrote a program for Burger King, he isn't answerable to the public. I, on the other hand, have to answer parliamentary questions.'88

The architects described their aims in the industry's terms. 'The primary technical requirements of the project were of scale and vendor neutrality at all levels,' Regunath Balasubramanian, its principal architect, told a trade magazine in 2011.89 The agency's document said the system was 'entirely built using open source components'.90 Nadhamuni later called it 'a massive technology challenge, doing a 1000 trillion biometric matches per day'.91 What it collected is recorded in Justice D.Y. Chandrachud's opinion in the 2018 case. Besides name, date of birth, gender, address and the names of parents or spouse, every resident would give 'All ten fingerprints, photograph and both iris scans'.92

The scheme was launched on 29 September 2010 at Tembhli, a village in Nandurbar district of Maharashtra, 'from where first Aadhaar was issued', according to the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG), the government's auditor.93 'On 29 September 2011, the Aadhaar project completed one year. An announcement was made of the generation of ten crore enrolments and of more than 3.75 crore Aadhaar numbers', that is, 100 million enrolments and 37.5 million numbers.94 For seven years the project rested on the Planning Commission's notification of 2009, which, Chandrachud pointed out, made no reference to biometrics at all. The Aadhaar Act came only in 2016, passed as a Money Bill, a form of legislation in which the upper house's part is limited.95 By the time it was notified, UIDAI had generated about one billion numbers.96 Twelve years after it was created, the agency itself was small. At the end of March 2021 it had 130 staff at its Delhi headquarters and 219 at its regional offices, and the work 'was being carried out by officers and staffs mostly either on deputation or from outsourced agencies'.97

The right to privacy

The challenge came early. In 2012, while Aadhaar still rested only on an executive notification, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy, a retired judge, and Pravesh Khanna filed a petition in the Supreme Court arguing that the scheme violated the right to privacy of 'innumerable citizens' under Article 21 of the Constitution, which protects life and personal liberty.98 Two older decisions of the Court had been read as holding that the Constitution did not protect a right to privacy, and the question was referred to nine judges.99 On 24 August 2017 they answered it unanimously: 'The right to privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and as a part of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution.'100 The judgment did not decide whether Aadhaar was lawful. It set the test that Aadhaar would have to pass.

Twenty-sixth of September, 2018

The Aadhaar case itself was decided on 26 September 2018 by five judges. Sikri wrote for Chief Justice Dipak Misra, Justice A.M. Khanwilkar and himself. Justice Ashok Bhushan wrote a separate concurring judgment. Chandrachud dissented.101

During the hearings the agency's chief executive, Dr Ajay Bhushan Pandey, had explained its engineering to the judges in a PowerPoint presentation.102 The Authority claimed that authentication was 'purpose blind', returning only a yes or a no, and that the biometric system 'provides high accuracy of over 99.86%'.103 Its own figures showed the other side. Of about 616 million Aadhaar numbers used for fingerprint authentication, 6.00 per cent were recorded as failed; for iris authentication the figure was 8.54 per cent. Failures, it said, 'do not mean exclusion or denial from subsidies, benefits or services', because agencies were obliged by law to provide other ways of establishing identity.104 The CAG later found that 'nearly 4.75 Lakh duplicate Aadhaar numbers were cancelled as of November 2019', about 475,000, and 'instances of issue of Aadhaars with the same biometric data to different residents indicating flaws in the de-duplication process'.105

The majority upheld the Act and its central purpose, the use of Aadhaar to deliver subsidies and benefits paid for from the Consolidated Fund of India, and held that it had been validly passed as a Money Bill.106 It then drew limits. School admission could not require Aadhaar, and 'CBSE, NEET, JEE, UGC etc. cannot make the requirement of Aadhaar mandatory as they are outside the purview of Section 7 and are not backed by any law.'107 JEE is the Joint Entrance Examination for the engineering colleges. The Court struck down the provision that let 'any body corporate or person' use Aadhaar under a contract, which would have enabled, it said, 'commercial exploitation of an individual biometric and demographic information by the private entities'.108 The compulsory linking of every bank account and every mobile number to Aadhaar went too.109

Chandrachud would have struck the Act down. He held that it was not a Money Bill, and that the programme violated 'essential norms pertaining to informational privacy, self-determination and data protection'.110 Much of his opinion is about people the system failed. 'For persons, who are engaged in manual labour, and persons who are disabled or aged, fingerprints actually cannot be captured by biometric devices.'111 He concluded: 'Dignity and rights of individuals cannot be based on algorithms or probabilities. Constitutional guarantees cannot be subject to the vicissitudes of technology.'112 The majority recorded the government's assurance that no eligible person would lose a benefit because a fingerprint failed to match, and treated such failures as matters of the system's working rather than its validity.113 Both sides accepted that the system made mistakes. They differed over whether a constitutional system could rest on a machine that was right most of the time.

Two lessons

The two halves of this chapter look unconnected: a private fraud in Hyderabad, a public database in New Delhi. They are connected by what the Indian software industry had become by 2009. It was large enough that its largest failure needed the state to rescue it, and capable enough that the state turned to it for a project of national scale.

Aadhaar lent the industry's people to a new client. For thirty years Indian programmers had built systems for foreign companies, under foreign contracts and foreign law. Here engineers from the same industry, some of them on reduced pay or none, built a system for the Indian state, meant for every resident. The Supreme Court's judgments of 2017 and 2018 were the first time Indian institutions had to decide, in detail, what Indian software could be made to do to Indian citizens.

Satyam showed where the value of an Indian services firm lay. By SEBI's investigation the books had been inflated from 2001 to 2008, and in the last quarter before the letter, by Raju's own figures, the real operating margin was 3 per cent of revenue.114 Yet the work went on. Engineers like Mastanamma expected to go on servicing the clients, the new chairman said the customers were still happy with the service, and within three months Tech Mahindra bid Rs 58 a share for control.115 What the bidder paid for was what the 20-F had described: some 50,000 associates recruited from the colleges, schooled for twelve weeks and placed on clients' work, nearly 10,000 of them at clients' sites abroad.116 That is the bargain this book follows. The firm sold its engineers' time, so its worth lay in the people it could put in front of a client and the clients who would take them, and because revenue grew with headcount, reported growth was itself something to sell. SEBI's 2018 summary of the tribunal's findings listed 'the need to show inflated results to attract customers/employees' among the matters the officials had discussed with the Raju brothers.117 That suggests at least one thing the fiction was for. The books failed and the delivery machine did not, because the clients had never been buying the books. They had been buying the people.

The question the next chapter takes up follows from both. An industry that could run a client's systems so reliably that a fraud at the top did not stop them, and that could lend its people to build a national identity platform, had still produced very few software products of its own. Chapter 17 asks why.
