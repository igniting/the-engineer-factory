The Engineer Factory

Chapter 13. H-1B (1990-2015)

Reedhar Gunda, an electrical engineer from Bombay (Mumbai since 1995), came to the United States in 1998 on a temporary work visa sponsored by a body shopper, a middleman who hired Indian engineers and rented them out to American companies. When he arrived, the Dallas Observer reporter Miriam Rozen wrote that November, he found that the body shopper was taking $22 an hour out of his pay and leaving him $28. To get out he had to find another job and have the new employer petition for his visa all over again, which took several months.1

His friend Reddy Bandi, a 27-year-old electrical engineer from Hyderabad, the son of a rice farmer and the first in his family to go to college, had taken a different road. He had come on a student visa, taken a master's degree at Arizona State University within 18 months, and switched to the work visa.2 He had kept away from body shops. 'I don't like to apply to companies who get people from the outside,' he said. 'I know what their tactics are.' He blamed both sides. Of those who came on the work visa he said, 'Their main intention is just to get in'; once they found they were underpaid, some switched employers 'two, three times a year'.3 Working through a large American subcontractor, he thought he earned more cash than the permanent staff beside him at the Sabre Group, though they had better benefits and more authority. His wife, a physical therapist, could not work in her field on his visa.4

Rozen built her article around a body shop in suburban Plano. Its owner, an engineer from Madras (Chennai since 1996), recruited engineers and programmers in India, signed them to contracts with his firm and hired them out to American companies, often through a further middleman. By Rozen's account he took 'sometimes as much as 40 percent' of their pay.5 The visa had been closed to new workers for almost five months after the annual quota ran out in May, she wrote, and he was 'making up for lost time'.6 He said he had brought some 350 engineers and programmers from India since 1993.7 By his own account, which he would not document, he paid salaries of $45,000 to $60,000; rival recruiters told Rozen he could bill a programmer at $90,000 to $200,000 a year.8 He would not let her speak to any of his contract workers.9 He had also begun sending American project specifications to his Madras office, where the work was done at Indian wages and emailed back. 'They are going to lose the whole business to India,' he said.10 It was the forecast of an interested party, made in a year when the visas had run out by May.

Rozen doubted that the wage rules were much enforced. An enforcement coordinator in the Labor Department's Dallas office told her he received complaints about the visa about once a month; engineers, he believed, moved on rather than pursue a case. Nationally, she reported, prosecutors had filed only eight cases in six years alleging wilful breaches of the prevailing-wage rules for temporary workers.11

The body shop was the industry's bargain at its barest. What it sold was an engineer's hour. Its margin was the gap between the rate it billed and the wage it paid, and it could grow only by bringing in more engineers. The visa that made the trade possible was eight years old, and it was called the H-1B.

A visa with a cap

The Immigration Act of 1990 admitted foreign nationals 'to work temporarily in the United States if hired by a U.S. employer to perform work in a specialty occupation', in the words of the Congressional Research Service (CRS), the research arm of the US Congress. A specialty occupation required at least a bachelor's degree. The visa lasted up to three years and could be renewed for three more.12 New visas were capped at 65,000 a year. That cap, the CRS noted in 2025, 'is the same now as when it was established in 1990', although Congress raised it for a few years around the turn of the century.13

The CRS has described the programme as one that 'epitomizes the tensions' between American employers competing for talent worldwide and the possible harm to American workers of their doing so.14 The protection for American workers was a promise on paper. Before petitioning for a worker, the employer filed a labour condition application with the Department of Labor, attesting that it would pay 'the greater of the actual wages paid to similar employees or the prevailing wages for that occupation'.15 The CRS added a dry sentence that explains much of what followed: 'The LCA is a statement of intent rather than a documentation of actions taken'.16 The department reviewed each application 'for completeness and obvious inaccuracies', and investigated only if someone later complained.17

For its first few years the cap did not bind. A table compiled from government data by the National Foundation for American Policy (NFAP), a pro-immigration policy group, shows 48,600 new petitions issued against the 65,000 cap in the US fiscal year 1992, and between 54,000 and 62,000 in each of the next four years.18 Nor was it at first mainly a programmers' visa. The economists Gaurav Khanna and Nicolas Morales cite a 1992 government report finding that computing and related occupations accounted for only 11 per cent of H-1 visas in 1989. By 1999, according to the immigration service, close to two-thirds of H-1B visas went to computer-related occupations.19 The visa was not free to use. A 2011 survey by the GAO, the US government's audit office, cited by Khanna and Morales, put the legal and administrative cost of each H-1B hire at between $2,300 and $7,500.20 The new visa also changed the economics of sending Indian programmers to American client sites, the trade Chapter 6 follows. 'In 1993, the shift from B-1 to H-1 visas lowered the incentives to hire Indian engineers for on-site work,' Khanna and Morales write, 'as they were to be paid the prevailing market wage'.21 The B-1 was a business visitor's visa, meant for meetings and negotiations, not for work. On this reading, one reason the Indian firms moved more of their work offshore in the 1990s was that the visa made on-site labour dearer.22

The cap began to bind as the American technology industry grew. Visa numbers ran out by September 1997, and the next year's in May 1998.23 Both Congress and the Federal Reserve, the CRS wrote, worried 'that a scarcity of labor could curtail the pace of economic growth'.24 Congress raised the ceiling to 115,000 for fiscal 1999 and 2000, and in 2000 to 195,000 for three years.25 The government's own counting failed under the strain. The immigration service 'admitted in autumn 1999 that thousands of H-1B visas beyond the 115,000 ceiling were approved in FY1999'.26 The 1998 law also charged employers $500 a worker, which, the Observer reported, would pay for scholarships for low-income students in mathematics, engineering and computer science, with about 5 per cent going to enforce the wage rules.27 Firms that relied heavily on the visa faced extra duties. An 'H-1B dependent' employer, generally one with at least 15 per cent of its workforce on H-1Bs, had to attest that it had tried to recruit American workers and had not laid any off in the 90 days before or after hiring a visa holder.28 Then the market turned. NFAP's table shows new petitions falling from 163,600 in fiscal 2001 to 79,100 in 2002 and 78,000 in 2003, against a cap of 195,000.29 When the limit reverted to 65,000 in fiscal 2005, the CRS wrote, 'that limit was reached on the first day'.30 In April 2007 the immigration service received about 119,193 petitions for the fiscal 2008 cap on the first two days it accepted them, and chose among them by a computer-generated random draw.31 From the mid-2000s the visa was allocated by lottery.32 A young engineer's chance of an American job now depended partly on luck.

How Indian the visa became

Indians did not dominate the visa at first. The sociologist AnnaLee Saxenian, writing in 2001 and citing a 2000 study of the visa by Lowell, reported that the number of Indian H-1B holders grew steadily from 1989, became the largest national group in 1994, doubled by 1996 and quintupled by 1999. In 1999 Indians received 47 per cent of all H-1B visas issued, 55,047 people; the next largest groups, from Britain and China, had 6 per cent or less each.33

The share kept rising, though the published figures measure different things. For fiscal 2005 the CRS reported that India was 'the leading country of origin for newly arriving H-1B workers, comprising almost half (49.0%) of all of the new arrivals', with China a distant second at 9.16 per cent.34 The same report repeated an undated government estimate that 'nearly three-fourths of all of the systems analysts and programmers are from India'.35 By 2014, according to immigration service data cited by Khanna and Morales, about 70 per cent of H-1B visas went to Indians; China, the next largest, had received only 8 per cent in 2012.36 A visa that Congress had written for any skilled foreigner had become, in its largest use, a channel between Indian engineering colleges and American information technology.

Who sponsored those visas is a separate question, and the answer is less simple than the common belief that the visas mostly went to Indian outsourcing firms. In 2013, by Khanna and Morales's tabulation of immigration service reports, Infosys led with 6,298 approvals, just ahead of Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), with Cognizant third; the year before, Cognizant had topped the list with 9,281. Eight more firms had over a thousand approvals each in 2013, among them Accenture, Wipro, IBM's Indian subsidiary and Deloitte.37

The authors drew the conclusion from the table: '10 out of the top 11 H-1B firms have Indians as their primary employment base'.38 Three of those ten had their headquarters outside India: Cognizant and IBM in the United States, Accenture in the Bahamas. The table lists iGate under both the United States and India. Their main workforce, like that of Infosys or Wipro, was in India. Only Deloitte, tenth on the list, employed mainly Americans.39 The largest sponsors, in other words, were firms built on the offshore services model, whoever owned them.40

The sponsors were also only part of the visa's use. Since fiscal 2000, the CRS noted, most H-1B workers had been exempt from the cap, because they were extending an existing status or worked for universities and research bodies.41 The lottery decided who got in. Extensions, and for some a pending application for permanent residence, decided how long they stayed.42

Who went, and what they had learned

Most temporary visa workers had been educated before they arrived. A 2010 national survey of American college graduates, tabulated by Khanna and Morales, found that among foreign-born workers on temporary work visas whose highest degree was a bachelor's, 94 per cent had earned it abroad. Among those with a master's, 77 per cent had.43 Since most H-1B holders were Indian, most of that education had been done in Indian colleges, without an American degree in between. Bandi, with his Arizona master's, was the exception.

That was a change from the earlier generation. The Indians already in Silicon Valley in 1990 had mostly come for graduate study. The 1990 census counted 28,520 Indians in the region's workforce, 98 per cent of them immigrants; 87 per cent of the Indians in its high-technology industries had arrived after 1970 and 60 per cent after 1980.44 National Science Foundation figures that Saxenian cited show that 79 per cent of Indians who received American science and engineering doctorates in 1990-91 were still working in the United States in 1995.45 Many were graduates of 'the prestigious Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) or Indian Institutes of Science (IISs)', she wrote, and were united by professional as well as ethnic ties; a sizable group had grown up in Africa and never lived in India.46

The IITs had been feeding America for decades; one IIT Bombay study found over 40 per cent of a 1970s cohort of its electrical engineering graduates abroad.47 The pattern outlasted the period of this chapter. A study published in 2023 by Prithwiraj Choudhury, Ina Ganguli and Patrick Gaulé, which followed students who sat the IIT entrance examination, found that 'among the top 1,000 scorers on the exam, 36% have migrated abroad, rising to 62% for the top 100 scorers'.48 The H-1B also gave Indian students a reason to take an American degree first. The 2004 allowance of 20,000 extra visas for holders of American master's degrees, Khanna and Morales note, 'may incentivize students to obtain their degrees in US universities'.49

The two routes together supplied much of America's computing workforce. A national survey of college graduates showed that 55 per cent of foreigners working in computing fields in 2003 had arrived on a temporary work visa or a student visa, and a Department of Commerce estimate for the late 1990s was that 28 per cent of programmer jobs went to H-1B holders.50 In 1990 immigrants (defined as people born abroad who arrived after the age of 18) made up 11.06 per cent of computer scientists in the United States with a bachelor's or master's degree. By 2000 they were 18.59 per cent, and by 2010 27.82 per cent.51

The pull on the colleges

The most important effect of the visa may have been on the people who never used it. Khanna and Morales built an economic model of students and firms in both countries to test a hypothesis: 'that Indian students enrolled in engineering schools to gain employment in the rapidly growing US IT industry via the H-1B visa program. Those who could not join the US workforce, due to the H-1B cap, remained in India, enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector'.52 Those who went and came back when their visas expired brought skills and technology with them.53 As the authors put it, workers who could not get a visa 'or return to India after their visa expires are a ready supply of labor for firms in India'.54

Their model's answer is that the visa 'induced Indians to switch to computer science (CS) occupations, increasing the CS workforce in India by 4.9% in 2010'.55 The same model finds a cost on the other side. The visa 'induced US workers to switch to non-CS occupations, reducing the US native CS workforce by 7%'.56 Indian IT output was 1.8 per cent higher, and the combined income of the two countries 1.5 per cent higher, than it would have been without the programme.57 These are estimates from a calibrated model, not counts. They describe a world without the H-1B that never existed. What they offer is a mechanism: the American prospect pulled Indian students towards engineering and computing, and the cap kept most of them at home, where the Indian firms hired them.

Over the same years, according to Nasscom (the National Association of Software and Service Companies), the industry's lobby group, information technology and business process outsourcing grew from 1.2 per cent of India's gross domestic product in 1998 to 7.5 per cent in 2012.58 The workforce it produced was young. In 2002 Indian software engineers had 'a median age of 26.5 years, and 58% of them had less than three years of experience', according to Nasscom. A Nasscom-Hewitt survey 'found that 88% of firms visited engineering colleges to recruit, and 47% recruited only there'.59 Drawing on a 2002 study by Arora and Athreye, Khanna and Morales report that the number of engineering doctorates in India fell from about 675 in 1987 to 375 in 1995.60 The industry, they conclude, was attracting 'some of the brightest young graduates with little experience'.

Silicon Valley's Indians

By 2000, 53 per cent of Silicon Valley's scientists and engineers were foreign-born, Saxenian reported, and about 20,000 of them were Indian.61 Many had found their careers stopped short. 'Many Indian engineers complained about a glass ceiling in the region's established companies, and responded by starting their own businesses', she wrote.62 Prabhat Andleigh, quoted in the Los Angeles Times in 1996, put it more sharply: 'Why do you think there are so many Indian entrepreneurs in Silicon Valley? Because they know that sooner or later they will be held back'.63

Some of the older arrivals had already built companies. Some time in 1992 a group of Indian businessmen in the Valley gathered to meet a visitor from India, the Secretary of Electronics. His flight was delayed. While they waited, they 'began to informally share complaints about the difficulties of running a business'.64 Among them were Suhas Patil, a former professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who had founded the chip company Cirrus Logic; Prabhu Goel, founder of Gateway Design Automation; and Kanwal Rekhi, who had started and run the networking firm Excelan until it merged with Novell.65 Patil and Goel had succeeded 'in spite of their lack of contacts or community support'.66 Patil put the discovery in a sentence that the San Jose Mercury News printed two years later: 'as individuals we are brilliant, but collectively we amounted to nothing'.67 Satish Gupta, another early member, said much the same in the same article: 'When some of us started our businesses we had nobody we could turn to for help'.68

Out of the delayed flight came a club with monthly meetings, called The Indus Entrepreneurs (TiE). Its founders chose 'Indus (rather than Indian)', Saxenian wrote, so that Pakistanis, Bangladeshis and Nepalese could belong too, though the Bay Area members were almost all Indian.69 TiE's own history dates the founding to December 1992.70 The first annual conference, in 1994, 'attracted over 500 people'. In 1997 more than 800 came to two days of talks on how to start a company, raise money and take it public.71 TiE turned to mentoring and, through its members, investing in younger South Asian founders.72 Its founders belonged to what Saxenian called 'an older generation of Indian immigrants'.73 The engineers the H-1B brought were younger, trained in India, and hired for a few years rather than for a career.

By Saxenian's 1999 count, firms with Indian chief executives were 7 per cent of the Silicon Valley technology companies founded between 1980 and 1998: 774 firms, with $3.6 billion in sales and 16,598 jobs in 1998.74 The Indian share of new start-ups rose from 3 per cent in 1980-84 to 9 per cent in 1995-98.75 Her count, she was explicit, used chief executives as 'a proxy for immigrant-founded businesses'. A later study that counted Indian immigrants among the key founders of 1995-2005 start-ups produced the more often quoted figure of 15.5 per cent.76 Any share of Indian start-ups is only as good as its definition.

Drain, then circulation

Saxenian's phrase 'brain circulation' is easily read back into the 1990s. Her own evidence for India in that decade points the other way.

The exception she described in detail was Radha Basu. Basu left 'her conservative South Indian family' for graduate study in computer science at the University of Southern California in the early 1970s and joined Hewlett-Packard.77 In the mid-1980s, after the Indian government invited her to set up one of the country's first foreign subsidiaries, she spent four years building HP's centre in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014). When she went back to California in 1989 it employed 400 people.78 The first office, she told Saxenian in 1997, 'consisted of a telex machine on her dining room table'.79 Customs officials could not accept that software could be exported over a satellite link without something physical to inspect, so for five years Basu copied HP's data onto tapes and shipped them to the United States so that the exports could be registered.80 Doing business in India, she told Saxenian, needed patience: 'You can't just fly in and out and stay in a five-star hotel and expect to get things done like you can elsewhere'.81 When officials talked of a 'single window of clearance' for foreign trade, she would joke: 'where is the window?'82

Basu was unusual. Saxenian wrote in 1999 that 'few Indian engineers choose to live and work permanently in India'; those who went home typically did so for a time.83 Bangalore software companies reported turnover of 20-30 per cent a year, 'primarily because so many workers jump at the first opportunity to emigrate'. On balance, 'the "brain drain" of skilled workers to the United States continued unabated throughout the 1990s'.84 The engineers who stayed in America played what she called 'an important, but largely arm's-length, role', persuading their employers to send work to India.85 The reason was cost. Programmers' wages were 'ten times lower in India', and the fully loaded cost of an engineer there was 35-40 per cent of the American figure.86

Much of the work was still body shopping, which Saxenian defined narrowly as programming at the customer's site on 'time and material' contracts billed by the programmer-hour. In the series by Balaji Parthasarathy that she cites, on-site services were about 90 per cent of the value of Indian software exports in 1990 and still 61 per cent in 1995.87 At the end of the decade much of the work in India was on the year 2000 problem, which earned exports but, in her judgement, created no new intellectual property.88 The big Indian firms, she judged, 'have been so profitable playing the wage gap that they have had few incentives to address higher value-added segments of the market'.89 Playing the wage gap was the bargain in another phrase: the firms sold hours, and the hours were cheap.

Writing in 2005, she repeated the point: 'few U.S.-educated emigrants returned to either India or China during the 1980s and 1990s'. It was the technology recession that 'triggered an upsurge in cross-regional entrepreneurship'.90 In Taiwan and Israel, by contrast, returning immigrants had carried the Silicon Valley model of venture capital home from the early 1980s.91 Indian entrepreneurs instead mobilised 'thousands of underemployed English-speaking Indian engineers to provide software development services for American corporations'.92 On her account, India's diaspora had sent work home rather than companies. Inside India, the 1990s turnover did not spread ideas between firms as it did in Silicon Valley. It mainly pushed up wages, by 25-40 per cent a year. Learning happened 'primarily within the firm and through long-distance relationships', not with local universities or laboratories, even in Bangalore, home of the Indian Institute of Science.93

By the mid-2000s the trade itself was changing who travelled. In Chennai, the anthropologists Chris Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan followed a group of engineers. Of their twenty younger software engineers, seven had been or were on overseas assignments in 2005, but only two had spent more than a year in the United States; 'the rest, as is now increasingly common, were away for only a few months'.94 They argued that 'the very logic of "offshoring"' meant that flows of people might diminish 'as the work travels to the site of labor, instead of the reverse'.95 For many engineers the American posting became a stint in a career spent in India.

At the Chennai firm they studied, which they called ICS, on-site assignments of several months or a year or two were a normal part of working life.96 Their individual stories show the range. Ravi (a pseudonym, as are all names in the study), a project manager of 33 at a large Chennai firm, had spent a year on-site in England, 'where he earned plenty of money and got "good exposure"', but came home to be with his elderly father and had no wish to live abroad.97 Balaram, 37, left the same company in 1995 for Australia, worked there in information technology for eight years, and came back in 2003 because 'the work available in Chennai is far more interesting and challenging, and the industry's growth prospects are much better'.98 Anuradha, a project manager who had worked for ICS in America from 2000 to 2002, now had a young daughter and refused on-site assignments; she said she did not need any more 'exposure'.99

The parents

Migration also reached into Indian homes. Fuller and Narasimhan found that Chennai was 'said to be full of lonely old folk whose sons and daughters send them dollars, but only ever come on short visits'. Other parents visited their children so often that they became 'bored, exploited childminders marooned in American suburbia under "house arrest", to quote one elderly grandfather'.100 One informant told them that overseas migration 'damages the proper reciprocal relationship between parents and children in a way that migration within India does not'.101 Children abroad could send money. They could not provide the daily care that sons and daughters were expected to give their parents.102

The B-1 channel

The H-1B was not the only door. The business visitor's visa that Khanna and Morales described as giving way to the H-1B in 1993 was still in use two decades later. The clearest record of how comes from a legal settlement.

On 30 October 2013 Infosys filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission a settlement agreement with the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Texas, the State Department and the Department of Homeland Security. Under it, 'Infosys agrees to pay a full settlement amount of $34,000,000'.103 The agreement separates what both sides accepted from what the government alleged.

Both sides accepted that 'Infosys failed to maintain accurate I-9 records for many of its foreign nationals in the United States in 2010 and 2011', the forms that record whether an employee may legally work. This included 'a widespread failure to update and re-verify the employment authorization status of a substantial percentage of its foreign national employees'.104

The government alleged more, and Infosys denied it. The allegation was that Infosys 'knowingly and unlawfully used B-1 visa holders to perform skilled labor' in jobs that would otherwise have gone to American citizens or to legitimate H-1B holders, for purposes that included 'increasing profits, minimizing costs of securing visas, increasing flexibility of employee movement'.105 According to the government, invitation letters described trips as 'customer discussions and related business development activities' when the work included 'coding and programming'.106 It quoted a 'Do's and Don'ts' memorandum given to B-1 travellers: 'Do not mention activities like implementation, design & testing, consulting, etc., which sound like work.' Another line read: 'Please do not mention anything about contract rates'.107 The government alleged that workers were told to give consular officers the destination named in the labour condition application when they were going elsewhere. In one case, it said, a worker was told to say Seattle when his real destination was Bentonville, Arkansas.108 And it alleged that on 29 September 2009 Infosys staff were directed by email to convert a time-and-materials contract, which disclosed the names and billing rates of everyone on a project, into a fixed-price contract, which disclosed only the total, in a way that hid the B-1 holders' skilled work.109

Infosys's answer, in the same document, was that its use of B-1 visas 'was for legitimate business purposes and not in any way to circumvent the requirements of the H-1B program'. It said the memorandum 'was not intended to deceive U.S. officials' and had been withdrawn in 2011, and that contract changes were 'made to comply with internal Company guidelines and rules regarding billing practices'.110 Infosys 'denies any allegations of civil or criminal wrongdoing and/or liability'.111 The settlement resolved the claims without resolving that dispute.

Two points can be made safely. The first is that the older on-site channel had not closed when the H-1B opened, and the agreed facts show that one of the largest sponsors had not kept accurate track of whether its foreign employees in America were authorised to work. The second concerns the contract forms. Whatever happened in that 2009 email exchange, the government's description of the two forms is accurate. A time-and-materials contract names the people and their rates. A fixed-price contract names only the price. The Indian industry grew up on the first kind, the hour sold and billed. Who captures the savings under each kind of contract is a question Chapter 19 takes up.

Ninety days in Orlando

The other side of the trade has a name and a voice. Leo Perrero had a bachelor's degree in information technology and, by his account, 'twenty years of hard work' in the field.112 He had worked for Disney in Orlando 'in some capacity for well over ten years', on the point-of-sale system through which visitors paid for food and merchandise.113 In the holiday season of 2014 he received a meeting invitation from a senior executive and, he later told the Senate Judiciary Committee, his mind 'buzzed with thoughts of a promotion or a bonus'. He walked into a small conference room with about two dozen colleagues.114 He recalled the executive's words: 'All of you in this room will be losing your jobs in the next 90 days. Your jobs have been given over to a foreign workforce.' Severance pay depended on training their replacements.115 Later that day, he recalled, he had to tell his children at a church pumpkin sale that the family could not buy one.116 Only '4 people would be directly rehired by Disney of the hundreds that were lost'.117

He described the handover in three stages. In the first, the newcomers sat beside the Disney staff and watched them work. 'Everything was recorded with audio and video. Then the very next day the recordings were put down on paper and read right back to us'.118 In the second the incumbents worked 'side by side with our replacements'. In the third they watched 'the foreign workers completely take over our jobs'.119 On 31 January 2015 they handed in their badges and laptops. His desk, chair and computer, he said, went to a worker 'who was just flown in to America weeks before'.120 Of the severance he had accepted, he said: 'Sadly, I choose the money over America'.121

What he described in the first stage was a transfer of knowledge in the most literal sense. The tacit work of a team, what it knew but had never written down, was recorded, transcribed and read back, so that people paid less could do it, first beside the old staff and then without them. A quarter of a century of the visa had refined a method for moving work from one set of people to another by writing it down.

His complaint was aimed at two things together: visa holders 'physically being flown in from foreign countries' and 'the growing use of foreign remote offshore workers'.122 The firms at the top of the sponsor tables earlier in this chapter ran both. To the worker in the conference room, the H-1B and the offshore centre were one system.
