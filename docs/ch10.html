The Engineer Factory

Chapter 10. Bangalore (1947-2010)

In the early 1970s the journalist Janardhan Roye used to see a young engineer named Vinay Deshpande working long hours in a basement. The basement belonged to the Classic Building on Richmond Road in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), and the firm that occupied it was called, in Roye's spelling, Processors Systems India. One evening Roye went down to see him. Deshpande was holding a soldering iron over a circuit board. He looked up, smiled, and explained that he was putting together 'hi-tech firmware, bound for the US'.1

Roye wrote the scene down forty years later, in 2013, in an article about how Bangalore had become a technology city. He dated it to about twelve years before the day in 1985 when Texas Instruments' satellite equipment arrived at Sona Towers by bullock cart. He thought Deshpande typical of the engineers of the 1970s, who worked quietly on their own or alongside the government's industrial, defence and science laboratories.2 His point is one the rest of the evidence supports. Bangalore was building electronics for American customers before any American software company had an office there.

In November 1991 Infosys advertised for sixty-four young engineers to start their careers in Bangalore.3 There are three answers to why the software trade gathered in that city. The first is older than the trade. Bangalore was a city the Indian state had filled with engineers, laboratories and colleges in the forty years after independence. The second is a set of decisions by firms, some of them about engineers and some of them about landlords. The third is a policy that made the city's software parks a kind of export enclave, with their own power and their own links to the world, and that required each firm in them to earn back its Indian wage bill one and a half times in exports every year.4 That rule tied what a firm had to sell to what its engineers cost. What the enclave did for the rest of Bangalore is the hardest question.

The state's city

N.R. Narayana Murthy, the founder of Infosys, gave his own account of the city's origins in an oral history for the Computer History Museum, recorded in Bangalore in January 2020. 'Right from 1947 when we got independence,' he said, 'the government of India decided to locate its defense oriented high-tech companies in Bangalore, which was away from the border with Pakistan and with China.'5 He added a reason of geography: the city lay about 250 miles from the Arabian Sea and about 200 from the Bay of Bengal, so that a plane coming to bomb it would be shot down first. That was why, in his telling, Jawaharlal Nehru and his cabinet made Bangalore a defence-oriented, high-technology place.6 That is Murthy's explanation; no cabinet record of it has surfaced.

The institutions themselves are not in doubt. Murthy named the Electronics and Radar Development Establishment (LRDE), a defence laboratory; Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL); and Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL). His interviewer added the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), and Murthy agreed that space research came 'in the late '70s'.7 A social scientist studying the city's poor, Supriya RoyChowdhury, describes the same city from another angle. In the decades after independence Bangalore was known as 'a garden city of retirees' bunglows [sic], and for a significant public sector presence', with central and state government enterprises set up from the early to mid-1950s and 'premier scientific research institutions' founded by the central government. 'The city's development in the first four decades was closely aligned with the development of the state sector,' she writes.8

The city's growth followed the same timetable. A research group at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) reports that Bangalore's population grew by 76 per cent between 1971 and 1981, a rise it attributes to the public-sector industries and defence establishments of the 1970s. Between 1991 and 2001, the decade of the software boom, growth was 38 per cent, and the urban agglomeration's population at the 2001 census was 5.7 million.9 Software did not make Bangalore big. It arrived in a city that the public sector had already made large and full of engineers.

The private technology community grew inside that public one. Vijay Chandru, a former professor of computer science at IISc who later founded a life-sciences company, told Roye: 'Bangalore was a tech city well before the arrival of the MNCs (multi national corporations)'.10 He called it 'the original Beantown', after Boston, and said that from the 1960s 'a host of engineers were engaged in creating hardware and software solutions' for avionics, defence, telecommunications and weather forecasting, mostly in the public sector.11

The education half of the story sits in the same institutions. When Wipro, then a maker of vegetable oils, decided to make computers in 1979, its research team worked inside the Indian Institute of Science's Digital Systems Lab, on equipment the institute could import more easily than a company could, and several of its first employees had trained there.12 The computer business of a company that later sold programmers to the world began in a university laboratory.

A flat in Pune

The software trade did not start in Bangalore. Richard Heeks of the University of Manchester, who studied the industry from the late 1980s, wrote in 1998 that it began in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995), 'in the business district around Nariman Point in the late 1970s'. When prices and commuting times rose, part of the industry moved north to the Santa Cruz Electronics Export Processing Zone (SEEPZ), and Bombay remained the centre of the trade 'until at least the mid-1980s'.13 Then its firms began to run short of people, partly because programmers were leaving for jobs abroad, and the city's high prices and poor quality of life told against it. Firms, foreign investors and would-be founders began to compare Bombay with other places.14

Murthy's account of one such comparison turns on housing law. Infosys was founded in Pune in 1981. 'We pretty soon realized that Maharashtra was not the best place to have a startup,' he said, because of the state's tenancy laws.15 A tenant who had lived in an apartment for seven years could not be made to leave and could claim it as his own. 'With the result, most landlords in Maharashtra refused to give an apartment on rent because of this fear. Unless you were a big company, unless you gave some big bank guarantee, etcetera, etcetera. But no startup could do that.'16 The founders wanted a state where the law was 'fair between the landlord and the tenant', and Karnataka was one. So the choice narrowed to Madras (Chennai since 1996), in Tamil Nadu, or Bangalore.17

They chose Bangalore, Murthy said, 'because of the availability of engineering talent'. Part of the reason was the defence public sector. The other part was education. In the 1970s and 1980s, he said, 'when there were very few engineering colleges in the country, Karnataka had a disproportionately higher percentage of engineering talent and engineering students in the country.'18 A later study put the regional supply in numbers. Writing in 2001 for a Stanford University conference on India's economy, the scholar of Silicon Valley AnnaLee Saxenian reported a finding that 60 per cent of India's computer science graduates came from three southern states, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and that the industry was concentrated there 'largely because of their concentration of engineering manpower'.19 The private engineering colleges described in Chapter 7 were part of that supply.

Beer, climate and perception

A common answer to the question of why Bangalore won is that companies liked the weather. The evidence makes this partly true but incomplete. Heeks gave the most systematic answer, using a model of how software firms choose locations. He found three factors at work in the mid-1980s and a fourth that took over later.20

The first was labour. Bangalore was seen to have 'abundant labour that could be drawn from its long-standing research laboratories, educational institutes and public sector electronics firms', among them IISc and the Indian Institute of Management.21 The second was quality of life. Bangalore had a reputation 'for a good climate and social life', and IT workers were quickly drawn into the city's social life and its 'pub culture'. Heeks recorded a semi-serious club of senior industry managers, the Beer-drinkers Association of Information Technology, whose members met to discuss strategy on condition that each drank at least six mugs of beer in the evening. Bombay, by contrast, had 'a long and troubled history of both communal and labour relations strife'.22 The third was infrastructure. 'You could get across town in a matter of minutes in a car,' where Bombay's peak-hour traffic could take hours, and Bangalore promised plentiful power and water.23

Heeks added a caution from his interviews: 'the perception of these factors as much as their reality has coloured locational decisions'.24 Some insiders gave the weather more weight. C.S. Murali of IISc, quoted by Roye in 2013, listed policy, skilled workers and the public-sector giants and then, 'above all', the city's 'inviting salubrious climate and cosmopolitan character'. Murthy, asked what had made Bangalore a hub, began with 'a pretty salubrious climate' and put engineering talent second.25 Both named skilled people too. As the main reason for Bangalore's rise, the climate story is mostly false; as one reason among several, it is true.

The infrastructure advantage was the least durable of Heeks's factors. By 1998 a rival city was advertising Bangalore's traffic as its weakness. By 2005 the concentration of technology production had brought fast-rising wages and worsening congestion, and Bangalore's engineering salaries were among the highest in India, yet firms kept clustering there rather than moving to cheaper places.26

Twenty-five rules

Heeks treated the arrival of Texas Instruments (TI) as 'a key event in Bangalore's history'. Once TI 'set up operations in 1986, it was the first proper offshore software facility, using satellite-based telecommunication links, and this was constantly reported in the home and international media'. The company was also 'known to have cut through many red tape barriers, particularly on imports'. From then on, he wrote, the Bangalore 'snowball effect' began.27

Saxenian put a number on the red tape. Citing an interview given in 1996 by N. Seshagiri, a former additional secretary in the Department of Electronics, she wrote: 'When Texas Instruments set up the first earth station in Bangalore in 1986, for example, the process involved removing or breaking 25 different government rules.'28 Without such links, she added, Indian firms 'had no alternative to providing contract programming on-site', at the customer's premises and usually in the United States.29

Other multinationals followed TI, and they explained themselves in terms of skill and risk. One American employer in Bangalore, in an interview of 1996 cited by the researcher Balaji Parthasarathy and quoted by Saxenian, said: 'we came here because of the skills. We expanded because of the skills.' The risks of distance, of the satellite link, the telephones and the flights, 'were offset by the costs'.30

Some of the work was hard engineering. TI's Bangalore centre began by designing tools for chip design. Its engineers went on to design a digital signal processing chip, announced in 1997 under the code name Ankur, Sanskrit for seedling, which V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, says was designed entirely in India and widely used in disk drives and instruments.31 It became a standard.32 Oracle opened a software development centre in Bangalore in 1994.33

By 1998 the snowball could be counted. A Dataquest survey of 558 Indian software companies, reproduced by Heeks, found 152 with headquarters in Bangalore, against 122 in Bombay, 93 in Madras, 86 in Delhi, 34 in Hyderabad and Secunderabad, 27 in Calcutta (Kolkata since 2001) and 22 in Pune.34 Heeks warned that the table understated Bombay, which was still home to several of the largest firms. But since the mid-1980s, when Bombay had led on numbers, Bangalore had grown at its expense and had come to be called India's 'Silicon Plateau'.35

The parks and the bargain

The state that had built Bangalore's laboratories now built its export infrastructure too. Bangalore's Software Technology Park (STP) opened in October 1990.36 An STP was in effect an export processing zone for software. Firms in it received five years' exemption from tax, guaranteed access to high-speed satellite links and reliable electricity, ready office space, duty-free imports of equipment and permission for full foreign ownership, 'in exchange for a sizable export obligation'.37

The obligation puts the book's bargain into the language of regulation. A park firm had to earn, net, 150 per cent of the value of its imported hardware within four years, and 'a net amount equal to 150% of their wage bill on an annual basis'.38 Every year, then, a firm's Indian payroll had to come back one and a half times over in foreign exchange. The rule measured exports against the cost of the people who produced them. On its arithmetic, every engineer a park firm hired raised the exports it owed, and a firm that sold time could earn more chiefly by billing more people's time. That is the bargain the rest of this book follows: India sold engineers' time, and revenue grew with headcount.

From January 1995 park firms could sell a quarter of their output in India, and from 1999 half.39 By 1998 the scheme had spread to 25 parks at various stages of planning, besides the government's own, and some were private ventures. The Information Technology Park in Bangalore was a partnership of the Karnataka government, Tata Industries and a consortium of Singapore firms.40

The parks moved work from America to India, slowly. The share of Indian software exports earned at clients' sites abroad fell through the 1990s but was still more than half in 1998.41 By 1995, 435 companies were registered under the scheme, accounting for more than 16 per cent of exports.42

Karnataka's numbers grew fastest. According to the state's development report of 2007, cited by RoyChowdhury, the number of software export companies in Karnataka rose from 13 to 1,154 between 1991 and 2003, and the state's software exports from Rs 5.6 crore (Rs 56 million, about $2.5 million) to Rs 12,350 crore (Rs 123.5 billion, about $2.65 billion).43 In the second half of the 1990s Karnataka was, she writes, the only state to grow faster than 8 per cent a year.44

What the work paid

The economics behind the cluster were simple, and Saxenian set them out without ornament. 'India's greatest asset is a large, educated, English-speaking workforce that is willing to work at relatively low wages.' In 1994, she wrote, 'wages for software programmers and systems analysts in India were less than one-tenth of those for their US counterparts, and lower even than other developing countries like Mexico'.45

Low wages meant low revenue per worker. On the figures Saxenian used, the industry employed about 180,000 people in 1998, and annual revenue per employee was $15,000 to $20,000, against about $100,000 per employee in Israel and Ireland.46 Revenue per head is the bargain's own measure: a firm that sold time at a low price could grow only by adding heads. Wages rose fast from their low base: the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, estimated that software pay rose 21 per cent a year in the late 1990s.47 That eroded the cost advantage that had brought the work. Ashank Desai, chairman of Mastek, a leading Indian software company, said in 1999 that Indian firms were finding it 'difficult for them to compete only on one differential', which was cost.48 The largest firms, among them Wipro and Infosys, were seeking bigger contracts, often on a turnkey basis, in which the firm delivers a finished system. 'Rather than competing on the basis of hourly productivity,' Saxenian wrote, 'they aim to accumulate intellectual property.'49

For the programmers, the city was often a waiting room for America. Those who worked abroad for Indian firms were 'paid their salaries in rupees and provided with minimal allowances for housing and expenses', and some called the business 'resume selling'.50 'Many thus aspire to work for foreign companies,' she wrote, 'not only for the relatively high wages but also for the opportunity be transferred overseas.'51 By 1999 Indians were receiving the largest share of the H-1B temporary work visas that the United States issued.52

Behind them came a large training system. By Nasscom's count, Indian colleges and polytechnics trained more than 67,000 computer science professionals a year, and another 200,000 people enrolled each year in the private software training institutes that had multiplied in the 1990s.53 Bangalore drew on that system and on the colleges of the three southern states that supplied most of the country's computing graduates.

The gap between those who got the jobs and everyone else stayed wide. Writing in 2013, Roye reported that the average software professional in Bangalore, outside the call-centre trade, earned Rs 50,000 a month, which he put at about $1,000, more than eight times the state's monthly income per head of Rs 6,000.54 The figure is a journalist's unsourced estimate, but it points the same way as the evidence of a decade earlier: fast-rising pay for a skilled minority in a poor state.

Cyberabad

In November 1998 the chief minister of the neighbouring state of Andhra Pradesh, the 48-year-old Nara Chandrababu Naidu, inaugurated a building called CyberTowers near Madhapur, on the edge of Hyderabad. It was to be the centrepiece of a planned Hi-Tech City. 'Until now, the world has heard of the twin cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad,' he said. 'From today, the world will know of the triplet cities of Hyderabad-Secunderabad-Cyberabad.'55

Business Today's cover story the next month, written by an economist based in Hyderabad, set the challenge in context. Hyderabad had once been called the nation's second capital, and public-sector firms such as Bharat Heavy Electricals and the Electronics Corporation of India had set up there in the 1970s. But 'the late 1980s saw Bangalore overtaking Hyderabad' as South India's most dynamic and corporate-friendly city.56 Naidu's answer was to court the largest names in software. 'When Naidu got Microsoft's CEO, Bill Gates, to locate its R&D centre at CyberTowers, he made sure no other company could ignore the city.'57 Oracle, BaaN, Tata IBM, Satyam, Wipro and others were already there.58

Hyderabad's pitch was aimed at Bangalore's weak point. J.A. Chowdhury, a director of the Software Technology Parks of India and, in the magazine's words, a key player in Naidu's Project Cyberabad, put it bluntly: 'Hyderabad is preparing for the future. Other cities will be left behind when traffic-congestion and urban chaos overtakes them.'59 He described the city as a 'knowledge hub'. It had the first Indian Institute of Information Technology, research institutes, defence laboratories and six universities, so that the supply of computing skills matched the demand.60 'Infotech professionals would like to live close to work so that they can work early morning or late into the night,' Chowdhury said, and the magazine added that housing could always be found within ten minutes of the workplace.61 The magazine, making Naidu's case, also cited a Nasscom survey of IT professionals that scored Hyderabad 3.40 out of 4 for the availability of qualified people and 3.20 for quality of life, but only average for climate, commuting and its educational facilities.62

The same article recorded doubts. Drinking water was short. Large parts of the city had 'grown haphazardly'. V.K. Bawa, one of its best-regarded urban planners, had drawn up a plan in the 1970s that no government had implemented, and he complained: 'The model of Hyderabad's development is regressive; it does not conform to modern concepts of urban growth.'63 The whole project depended on one politician. If Naidu won the next state election, due in November 1999, the direction was clear. 'If he loses, the imponderables are many.'64

Saxenian saw in Naidu something larger than one city's ambition. While the central government had begun liberalisation, she wrote, 'the state governments have pioneered some of the most far-reaching policy innovations in the IT sector', and Naidu had made 'e-governance', the use of computers to deliver public services, his signature.65 His campaign set off competition from his neighbours. Karnataka's new government announced plans to upgrade its 'overburdened roads', and Tamil Nadu and Kerala drew up IT policies of their own.66 By 2001 software employment was concentrated in Karnataka (Bangalore), Andhra Pradesh (Hyderabad), Tamil Nadu (Chennai), Maharashtra (Bombay) 'and areas surrounding Delhi'.67

The enclave

The sharpest contemporary warning about the cluster came from Saxenian herself, in a paper that asked whether Bangalore was 'the Silicon Valley of Asia'. The comparison, she answered, was 'not only misleading but also distracts attention from the deeper challenges and opportunities that IT affords the Indian economy'.68 Most IT development already took place in parks 'insulated from the day-to-day challenges of doing business in India by dedicated communications links, private power sources, and liberal rules for investment and taxation'.69 The insulation answered real shortages. In 1997 India had 18.6 telephone lines per 1,000 people, against 55.7 in China, and a new connection took more than twelve months to arrive.70

Its cost was disconnection. 'Professionals in IT enclaves could become better connected, both economically and socially, to distant regions than to the rest of the Indian economy.'71 The policy of the day, she concluded, 'risks accelerating the growth of IT as a small, modern enclave in a poor and backward economy'.72

The road not taken

Saxenian did not think the enclave was the only possible Bangalore. India, she wrote, 'already has the design capabilities' to build products of its own. 'However India lacks the environment needed to support experimentation with the application of these skills to new markets.'73 Her example was the Simputer, a very low-cost mobile computer priced at under Rs 9,000, about $200 by her conversion, built by a Bangalore team from IISc and Encore Software.74 Encore was the company of the same Vinay Deshpande whom Roye had seen with a soldering iron on Richmond Road.75 Her prescription was a level field, in which products and services sold at home would 'enjoy the same tax benefits as those currently enjoyed by software export units'.76 A regime built to earn foreign exchange had made it easier to sell software abroad than at home.

Three readings

The evidence supports three readings of what happened in Bangalore, and they are not exclusive. The first is a story of agglomeration. A city filled with public-sector engineers and laboratories attracted a few early movers, TI, Wipro and Infosys among them, and the cluster fed itself as employees left to found firms nearby.77 On this reading Bangalore's software industry was the delayed return on forty years of state investment in defence, science and engineering education.

The second reading is Saxenian's, that Bangalore depended on cheap skill and an export regime, and that its best engineers were drawn towards America.78 TI's chip showed that the capability was real. Revenue per worker showed how little of it was being used. What filled the parks at the end of the 1990s was routine work done at great volume, as hundreds of American and European companies outsourced 'coding, maintenance and Y2K solutions' to Indian firms.79

The third reading is about the city. The industry created a well-paid professional economy with private power, private links and few ties to the city's poor.80

K.S. Garden

What did the boom do for the people of Bangalore who did not write software? The most careful answer comes from RoyChowdhury's study of six slums in the inner city, on J.C. Road close to Lalbagh, which she calls the central commercial hub of the city. She surveyed 300 households and held focus groups.81 She begins with the transformation. The city had 'become the icon of India's information technology revolution'. Real estate prices soared. The large campuses of the public-sector firms became 'shadows of a past': much of their land was put up for sale, units closed, and their permanent workforces were 'downsized and contractualized'.82

The new economy grew, she writes, 'through the expansion of capital intensive rather than labour intensive industries, as well as skill-based industries, which offered limited scope of employment, and only to technically skilled personnel'.83 The poor found work in construction and in the export garment trade, at low wages, and in new service jobs: driving the cars and vans of call centres and outsourcing firms, guarding and cleaning malls and supermarkets, waiting at tables. Whether in factories or services, that work remained 'in the casual/contract mode with no security of service, insurance or pension'.84 'In Bangalore, nearly one million poor live in slums,' she reports, and about a third of slum dwellers lived below the poverty line, on less than Rs 2,500 a month, which she converts as $55.85

The IT economy reached one of her slums, K.S. Garden, in a particular way. It had the lowest poverty ratio of the six. Families in its better-paid occupations, from skilled trades to permanent jobs in public bodies, could send their children to private English-medium schools or to computer training.86 'About 50 youths in the slum had managed to get some kind of computer training. Of these, some 25-30 women were working as receptionist in small offices, or showrooms, at cash counters in small super markets.'87 They had left the low-paid work of their parents. But the gain was fragile. In the focus groups, 'even those with some computer training frequently remained unemployed or only partially employed', and where someone had found a foothold, 'a connection through family or network had worked in most cases'. RoyChowdhury's conclusion is plain: 'there had not been any systemic or policy based effort to incorporate the urban underclass into the city's growing economy'.88 In the same slum, coolie labour and domestic service still accounted for 52.3 per cent of households surveyed.89

Roye's article of 2013 names two individuals, both young women from the villages and towns around the city. Anusaya, from Immadihalli village, worked at an ice-cream kiosk in a shopping mall in Whitefield, one of the new technology districts, for Rs 6,000 a month, about $100 by Roye's conversion. 'I work here in the day, go to evening college,' she told him. 'The money I earn meets some of my family expenses and funds college fees.'90 Ramya Gowda, from Malur, worked at a doughnut counter. 'I got this job because I learnt English and have finished school,' she said. 'This is a part time job. I'd like to study more, especially computers, and work in a big company like Yahoo!'91 Neither was a software engineer. Both were working at the edge of the economy that software had built, and both described education, English first and computers next, as the way in.

Roye recorded what the corridors had replaced. The campuses of Whitefield, Electronic City and the Outer Ring Road stood largely on what had been farmland or swamp; many farmers on the city's edge had sold their land; the city had fewer trees, lakes and open spaces, and was by his account the third most expensive in India.92 He ended the article in a bar on Lavelle Road, where a young software worker from Thanisandra told him: 'Seriously uncle, the city has arrived!'93
