The Engineer Factory

Epilogue. India in 2035

Rishabh Mishra’s course at the Indian Institute of Information Technology, Design and Manufacturing in Jabalpur was due to end in May 2026. He had joined in 2022 to study computer science, write code and, in the words of the Rest of World reporter who told his story, ‘one day make it to Silicon Valley’. By December 2025, according to the article, fewer than a quarter of his 400 classmates had job offers. ‘It is really bad out there,’ he said.1 Earlier that year, at the other end of the same pipeline, the trainee whom the prologue called Gayatri had been let go from Infosys’s training campus in Mysuru, four months after a joining date she had waited nearly two years for. ‘They wasted two and a half years of my career,’ she said.2 Between them they stand at the two ends of the book’s present: a graduate who cannot get in, and a recruit who got in and was sent home.

What their degrees will be worth by 2035 is the question this epilogue takes up. The furthest official projection is a NITI Aayog report of October 2025, Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy, prepared by the Indian government’s policy think-tank with the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry’s lobby group, and the consultancy BCG.3 It offers two endpoints for 2031. In what it calls a business-as-usual case, ‘the headcount in the tech services sector could go down from 7.5 to 8 million in 2023 to 6 million by 2031’. If India acted on the report’s advice, the total ‘could swell to 10 million’.4 The numbers need caution. On one page the report calls its base the employment of the tech services industry, and on another a ‘total tech workforce’ that includes ‘tech jobs in other sectors’, which is not the industry headcount that Nasscom publishes.5 And the report was written to argue for a new national programme of AI teaching and retraining, so its two paths are framed to show the cost of not adopting it.6 That makes the numbers advocacy, published under a government’s name and co-written by the industry that would benefit from the spending. The 2035 of this epilogue’s title is therefore the book’s own horizon, about ten years from the time of writing.7

What the history shows

The book began with a working argument. India’s software profession, it proposed, was shaped less by technology than by three constraints: scarce foreign exchange, a surplus of English-educated engineers, and an American labour market hungry for them. The result priced engineering by the hour and grew by adding people, and AI attacks exactly that model. On the evidence of the chapters, the verdict is mixed.

The first constraint holds best. In the 1970s the Department of Electronics allowed computers to be imported ‘exclusively meant for software export’.8 The policy of 1986 still required an importer to ‘earn 250% of the cost of the imported computer within 4 years’, and the budget of July 1991 extended a tax concession to software so that ‘the exports of this industry should register rapid growth’.910 Selling programmers’ time abroad was the export the rules made easiest. But scarcity built skills as well as exporting labour: because software could not be imported, in the account of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, Indian hardware firms wrote their own, and ‘a large number of systems programmers in India became experts in UNIX development and usage’.11 And a trade justified by dollars kept fewer of them than its headline figures suggested: the development scholar Richard Heeks put net earnings at around 40 per cent of the gross.12

The second constraint is where the strongest counter-evidence lies. In the early 1990s the World Bank found not a surplus but a shortage: ‘the demand for trained software personnel far outstrips the supply’.13 The surplus came later, and it was built: on one reading, by decades of public investment in science and engineering in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014), and then by private colleges that multiplied the supply until, by 2016-17, on an analysis of the regulator’s data by the education charity Avanti Fellows, 49.5 per cent of approved undergraduate engineering seats had no student in them.1415 On this book’s reading, the surplus was less a starting condition than something the industry’s demand called into being.

The third constraint is real, but it did not stay a demand for people. The economists Gaurav Khanna and Nicolas Morales built a model in which Indian students enrolled in engineering ‘to gain employment in the rapidly growing US IT industry via the H-1B visa program’, with those the visa cap kept out ‘enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector’.16 The share of work done at the client’s site then fell from 90 per cent in 1988 to 56 per cent in 2000.17 The constraints set the price of the work; the wire decided how much of it could be done in India.

The middle of the argument is the best supported. Heeks wrote in 1998 that in custom software services ‘earnings are directly proportional to the number of people working in the industry’, and the pyramid, the bench and the training campuses were built on that arithmetic.18 Even in the year to March 2008, fixed-price projects brought Infosys only 31 per cent of its services revenue.19 Whether AI ends that model is the last clause, and the one the rest of this epilogue weighs.

The book’s judgement

What follows is the book’s own argument, built from the evidence of its chapters. It is that the hourly bargain is ending as a way to grow; that what replaces it depends on who keeps the hour a machine saves; and that the cost falls first on the bottom rung.

The first part rests on the companies’ own words. In three successive annual reports to the American regulator, from 2024 to 2026, Infosys said that clients were demanding higher commitments around productivity because of what AI could do, and that it was promising those improvements in its contracts in advance.20 In 2026 it added that clients were ‘selectively insourcing capabilities as AI improves productivity and reduces dependency on external labor-based models’.21 Wipro predicted that ‘Hiring will shift from volume to skills’, and TCS’s head of human resources said in October 2026 that the company was ‘hiring for demand’.22 Nasscom’s own figures show the result: in the year to March 2026 the industry’s revenue rose 6.1 per cent and its jobs 2.3 per cent.23 For fifty years revenue and headcount had risen together because revenue was, roughly, heads multiplied by a billing rate. A gap between them is small, but it is the bargain coming apart. It does not mean the offices empty. By 2026 headcount had stalled, not collapsed. It means that growth no longer requires the factory to keep hiring at the old pace.

The second part is the pricing mechanism the book has traced since the Y2K years. Under time-and-materials billing a saved hour belongs to the client, who simply pays for fewer of them. Under a fixed price the vendor keeps what it saves, which is why Infosys told investors in 1999 that it wanted more fixed-price work and reported in 2002 that the share of such work had risen.24 In a captive centre owned by the client there is nothing to bargain over: the parent keeps the saving outright (Chapter 15). And a product firm can sell the saving itself. Freshworks, an Indian-built software company with most of its staff in India, told investors that its Freddy AI Agent ‘offers always-on, autonomous, personalized resolutions to customer and employee queries’, which is much of what India’s help desks had been paid by the hour to do.25 Each of these owners would produce a different industry. In 2026 the evidence pointed one way. The vendors had promised the savings before making them, deals for the same work were worth, by HCLTech’s chief executive’s rough estimate, about a fifth less, and a broker’s report said the savings were ‘currently being extracted almost entirely by enterprise buyers through productivity demands and vendor price pressure’.26 For now, the client owns the saving.

The companies’ answer deserves its strongest statement. They argue that AI creates more work than it removes, and they had numbers to show for it. By the end of the year to March 2026 TCS reported AI revenue at an annualised rate above $2.3 billion, HCLTech reported $620 million for the year and Infosys $275 million in a single quarter, and Nasscom made a first estimate of the industry’s AI revenue at $10-12 billion. ‘Our expectation is AI will be net accretive,’ TCS’s chief executive said.27 Each firm defines AI revenue its own way, and one analyst put it at under 5-7 per cent of the five largest firms’ revenue.28 The new work is real. But on the evidence so far it is small beside the old work being repriced, and Wipro expected the industry to move towards ‘outcome-based pricing’, charging for a result rather than for the hours spent on it.22 New work sold that way is the point of the judgement, not an exception to it.

The third part follows from the second. The hours a client is most willing to stop paying for are the junior ones: the coding, testing and documentation that the book’s chapters followed from the Y2K years, broken into documented steps that any trained graduate could follow. Those are the tasks for which Infosys described its code assistant in 2024, and the tasks the NITI roadmap expects to go first: in an IT services company, it says, AI ‘typically translates to a shrinking of entry-level roles involving QA and support’.2930 The report illustrates this with invented characters, a junior test engineer in Pune and a support agent in Chennai.31 The starting salary had barely moved in a decade, and TCS’s chief executive said, in a newspaper’s paraphrase, that freshers take ‘up to nine months to come on the floor, while a lateral can deliver immediately’.32 Infosys’s offers had recovered from their trough, but in the year to March 2026 they were still well under half the 222,500 of the boom year to March 2022.33 The rung is still there. It is narrower, and the reason to expect it to widen again is weaker than it was.

The machines have been forecast to take this work before, and the forecast was wrong for thirty years. In 1994 the World Bank’s study of the Indian industry warned that ‘Demand for these skills is likely to be eroded by tools that automate many of these tasks’, and added that ‘a long-term decline in demand for routine or low-level programmers has been forecasted’.34 In 1998 Heeks named ‘automation from new technology’ among the risks to the trade and saw a trap in it: clients sent work offshore only when it was tightly specified, and such projects were ‘more amenable to automated software tools’.35 Instead exports kept growing fast, and by 2026 Nasscom counted 5.95 million direct employees.3623 The warnings were wrong because demand absorbed the productivity. Each tool that made an engineer faster made software cheaper, cheaper software found new buyers, and a firm that sold hours could use the saving to win more work. The book’s one documented case of the pattern in India is small but exact. When the railway booking office in New Delhi was computerised in 1986, Rajaraman records, there was ‘no reduction of staff; in fact there was an increase as more counters were operated with more clerks working simultaneously’.37 On the book’s reading, the machine raised each clerk’s output and demand that the queues had held down absorbed it.

For that to happen again, three things would have to be true. Demand for software at the new, lower price would have to grow faster than the hours needed for each piece of it fall. The saving would have to stay with someone who reinvests it in hiring, rather than being taken by the client in advance. And the new work would have to reach the firms and the graduates the factory was built to supply. On the evidence of 2026 the first is uncertain: the industry still grew, but HCLTech said winning the same revenue took ‘at least 25-30 percent more effort’, TCS’s and Wipro’s revenue fell in dollar terms, and one broker expected growth to stay ‘range-bound at 3 percent’.38 The second points the other way, for as long as the clients keep the saving. The third is partly true: Indian engineers were still being hired in large numbers, but increasingly by the captive centres, which by the year to March 2026 employed about 2.36 million people by an industry count, and the graduates still had to get past a narrower bottom rung.39 The book’s reading is that demand may again absorb much of the productivity, and that Indian engineers may again be employed in large numbers, but not through the bargain that built the factory. Heeks’s trap has closed on the hourly vendor, and most tightly at the bottom rung.

Four things to watch

The four futures that follow are signposts, not forecasts. They are not exclusive, and they are not equally likely. The evidence in this book currently favours the third, in which the client moves in, and the first, in which the pyramid thins, because both follow from the client keeping the saving. It gives least support to the second, which needs the price of each engineer to rise while the price of each deal falls. The fourth is real but small.

The thinning pyramid

An Indian services firm has long been described as a pyramid, with a wide base of junior engineers billed at rates well above their pay. In this future the base shrinks. The firms survive, smaller at the bottom and older on average, and the colleges keep producing graduates trained for a base that is narrowing, so that the prologue’s waiting fresher becomes the normal case. The evidence for it is the pricing evidence above. The supply side had begun to adjust before the tools arrived: by the Avanti Fellows analysis, approved undergraduate intake peaked at about 1.7 million seats in 2014-15 and fell to about 1.25 million by 2021-22, with roughly a third of the seats left empty.40 The counter-evidence is that by 2026 the pyramid had not visibly thinned: Infosys’s offers had tripled from their trough, TCS was still taking freshers by the tens of thousands, and Nasscom still reported net hiring, while the delayed joining dates began in 2022, two years before Infosys’s filings first mentioned AI code generation.4142 The signposts are fresher offers as a share of headcount at the five largest firms falling year after year, revenue per employee rising while headcount stays flat, and the share of engineering graduates in engineering jobs falling, which is why the missing placement data matter.

The diamond

In this future the shape becomes a diamond: fewer people at the bottom and a wide, better-paid middle that directs and checks what the tools produce. The image is the staffing executive Kamal Karanth’s, in a Reuters Breakingviews column’s paraphrase.43 The NITI roadmap expects ‘the price-per-FTE for the industry to grow 5-8% annually over the next few years’, FTE meaning one full-time person’s working time.44 The weight of the evidence is against it for now. Price per worker and value per deal measure different things, but the deflation reported seven months after NITI’s estimate points the other way, and nothing published by 2026 settles which is right.45 For education this future is kinder but harder: it needs fewer graduates who can follow a specification and more who can judge whether a system is right. The signposts would be revenue per employee and average pay rising together, lateral hiring outpacing campus hiring, and a revised curriculum actually taught: the All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE), the regulator, declared 2025 its ‘Year of Artificial Intelligence’, but no revised model curriculum had appeared by the time of writing.46

The client moves in

In this future multinationals stop buying Indian engineers’ time from Indian vendors and employ the engineers themselves, in global capability centres (GCCs), the Indian offices that a foreign company owns and staffs. It has a long history, from the software design centre that Texas Instruments, by its own account, began operating in Bangalore in 1985 to the centres of Chapter 15.47 It is the future the evidence most clearly shows arriving. By the year to March 2026, on Nasscom and Zinnov’s count, GCC revenue was already close to what the two had projected for 2030; Infosys listed clients ‘setting up or expanding their own GCC in India’ as a risk; and Karnataka set a target of 500 new GCCs by 2029.394849 Indian engineers keep their jobs but change employers, and the national champions matter less. The counter-evidence is about the numbers themselves: every GCC figure comes from Nasscom and from Zinnov, a consultancy that sells advice to GCCs, and Nasscom has restated its counts before.50 The signposts would be GCC headcount growing while the services firms’ stays flat, services firms earning more from building and running centres for clients, and states beyond Karnataka adopting their own policies under the national framework that the 2025-26 Union Budget promised.51

The builder state

In this future barriers abroad and ambitions at home turn the industry inward. The H-1B route was contested by 2026: a $100,000 fee proclaimed in September 2025, the administration’s claim of a 92 per cent fall in the largest outsourcing firms’ registrations, and courts that had stopped the fee being collected without striking down the proclamations.52 The Indian firms’ approved petitions had begun to fall before the fee, by the count of the National Foundation for American Policy, a pro-immigration research group.53 At home the state that had built Aadhaar, the biometric identity system, and UPI, the payments network, had launched the IndiaAI Mission and backed Sarvam’s first foundation models, and Nasscom expected domestic revenue to grow faster than the industry as a whole.5455 The counter-evidence is serious. Inc42 judged that Sarvam’s models trailed the leading foreign ones in ‘raw reasoning depth’, and the NITI report records that India’s share of granted AI patents fell from 8-10 per cent in 2010 to under 5 per cent in 2023.5657 Building identity and payments systems was a feat of engineering and organisation; building frontier AI needs far more capital. The signposts would be spending on AI sustained beyond one budget cycle, Indian-language models in use in state services, and a rising share of graduates hired by domestic product firms and public bodies. GitHub’s projection of Indian developers on its platform counts accounts on one company’s service, not jobs, but a rising number of Indians writing code for Indian users would show up there first.58

Reading the signposts

Several of these signposts are numbers that India does not collect well. AICTE’s data on graduates placed in jobs are unusable, by the Avanti Fellows analysis; Nasscom’s headcount is an industry body’s estimate; GCC counts come from parties that sell to GCCs; AI revenue has no common definition.59 Projections in this industry have also been overtaken within a few years: Nasscom and Zinnov’s GCC projection for 2030 was nearly reached by 2026, and NITI’s rising price per engineer was contradicted by reported deflation seven months later.6061 A reader in 2030 who wants to know which future is arriving will need, first, an independent count of software employment, a placement series for engineering graduates, and revenue per employee for the large firms on comparable terms.

The joining date

The book began with a joining date that kept moving. For Gayatri the date came after nearly two years, and the job lasted four months. She told the reporter it had been her dream job.62 For Rishabh Mishra and his classmates in Jabalpur, the reporting ends before any date. Some of them were thinking of further study instead. Mishra was doubtful: ‘after one year, if you return to the job market, your degree is even more irrelevant’.63

His sentence is about education as much as about jobs. For half a century the engineering degree in India was a ticket to a particular kind of work: billable hours, learned first on a company training campus and then sold to a client by the month. The colleges, the entrance examinations, the coaching towns and the company campuses were built around that ticket. If the book’s judgement is right, the ticket will still buy work in 2035, but less often at the bottom, more often from a client’s own centre, and on terms set by whoever keeps the hour the machine saves. The engineer factory was built on purpose, by the state and the companies and millions of families, in answer to the questions of its time. The question for the next ten years is what a joining date means when the factory’s product is no longer hours.
