The Engineer Factory

Epilogue. India in 2035

In October 2025 NITI Aayog, the Indian government's policy think-tank, published a 28-page report called Roadmap for Job Creation in the AI Economy. It was prepared by NITI's Frontier Tech Hub with the partnership of the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the software industry's lobby group, and the consultancy BCG.1 Two forewords open it. The second is signed by Debjani Ghosh, a Distinguished Fellow at NITI, and it puts the choice in one sentence: 'By 2031, India's technology sector stands at a crossroads: we could lose 1.5 million jobs or create up to 4 million new opportunities.'2

The executive summary turns those words into two endpoints. In what it calls a business-as-usual case, 'the headcount in the tech services sector could go down from 7.5 to 8 million in 2023 to 6 million by 2031'. If India acted on the report's advice, the total 'could swell to 10 million'.3 Between them lies a difference of four million working lives, and the report's case is that policy decides which way the country goes.

The numbers need three cautions before they can be used. The first is the base. On one page the report calls the 7.5 to 8 million the employment of the tech services industry, but on another it explains that the figure is a 'total tech workforce' that includes 'tech jobs in other sectors', outside the software industry itself.4 It is not the industry headcount that Nasscom publishes. Nasscom estimated the industry's workforce at 5.8 million for the year to March 2025, and the two figures are not measured on the same basis.5 The second is consistency. On one page the report says that 'by 2030, we can add 2 million more new jobs'; in the foreword the upside is up to 4 million by 2031.6 The third is interest. The report was written to argue for an 'India AI Talent Mission', a new national programme of AI teaching and retraining, and its two paths are framed to show the cost of not adopting it.7 That does not make the numbers wrong. It makes them advocacy, published under a government's name and co-written by the industry that would benefit from the spending.

The report also has characters. To show what AI does to ordinary jobs, it describes a junior quality-assurance engineer in Pune named Rekha, whose work is writing and running software tests, and a first-line support agent in Chennai named Aman, who sorts help-desk tickets.8 They are illustrations invented by the report's authors, not reported people; the report gives no surnames and no sign that they are real. A document about millions of jobs presents its workers as examples it made up.

This book looked for a forecast of Indian software employment in 2035 and did not find one. The furthest official projection is NITI's, to 2031. The only mention of 2035 in that report is a proposed vision statement for the talent mission: 'To position India as the global epicentre of AI talent by 2035'.9 That is a goal, not an estimate. The 2035 in this epilogue's title is therefore the book's own horizon, about ten years from the time of writing. Four scenarios follow. They are this book's scenarios, not anyone's prediction. Each takes one force that earlier chapters documented and asks what India would look like if that force dominated for a decade, and each comes with signposts, things a reader could check in 2030, and with the evidence that cuts against it. Before them comes the book's own verdict on the argument it set out to test.

What the history shows

This book began with a working argument and promised to look as hard for evidence against it as for it. The argument was that India's software profession was shaped less by technology than by three constraints: scarce foreign exchange, a surplus of English-educated engineers, and an American labour market hungry for them. The result, it said, priced engineering by the hour and grew by adding people, and AI attacks exactly that model. What follows is this book's reading of the evidence its chapters set out. It adds no new facts.

The first constraint holds best. In the 1970s, when computers were not considered a priority for scarce foreign exchange, the Department of Electronics allowed computers to be imported 'exclusively meant for software export', and the first policy paper on this appeared in 1974 (Chapter 3).10 The policy of 1986 still required an importer to 'earn 250% of the cost of the imported computer within 4 years' (Chapter 8).11 The budget of July 1991 extended a tax concession to software so that 'the exports of this industry should register rapid growth' (Chapter 9).12 Selling programmers' time abroad was the export the rules made easiest. But the constraint worked in two directions. Chapter 5 recorded the argument of V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, that because software could not be imported, Indian hardware firms wrote their own, and that between 1978 and 1984 'a large number of systems programmers in India became experts in UNIX development and usage'. That rests on one participant's account.13 Scarcity built skills as well as exporting labour. And a trade justified by dollars kept fewer of them than its headline figures suggested: the development scholar Richard Heeks put net earnings at around 40 per cent of the gross (Chapter 6).14

The second constraint is where the book's strongest counter-evidence lies. In the early 1990s the World Bank's study found not a surplus but a shortage: 'the demand for trained software personnel far outstrips the supply, and the shortage is a major constraint to the industry's expansion' (Chapter 7).15 Private training organisations were by then producing 'almost 85 percent of the programmers'.16 The surplus came later, and it was built. On one reading in Chapter 10, the software industry of Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) was the delayed return on decades of public investment in defence, science and engineering education; the city's development, in one social scientist's words, 'was closely aligned with the development of the state sector' (Chapter 10).17 The private colleges of Chapters 7 and 12 then multiplied the supply until it outran demand: by 2016-17, on an analysis of the regulator's data by the education charity Avanti Fellows, 49.5 per cent of approved undergraduate engineering seats had no student in them.18 On this book's reading the surplus was less a starting condition than something the industry's demand called into being.

The third constraint is real, but it did not stay a demand for people. Chapter 13 described the economists Gaurav Khanna and Nicolas Morales's model, which tests the hypothesis that Indian students 'enrolled in engineering schools to gain employment in the rapidly growing US IT industry via the H-1B visa program', and that those the visa cap kept out 'remained in India, enabling the growth of an Indian IT sector'.19 If the model is right, the American labour market did not only absorb a surplus; it helped to create one. The same economists report that the share of work done at the client's site fell from 90 per cent in 1988 to 66 per cent in 1995 and 56 per cent in 2000, as satellite links let the work travel without the worker and, they argue, as the 1993 shift from business visitors' visas to the H-1B made on-site labour dearer (Chapters 9 and 13).20 That is also the clearest evidence against 'less by technology'. The constraints set the price of the work; the wire decided how much of it could be done in India.

The middle of the argument is the best supported. Heeks wrote in 1998 that in custom software services 'earnings are directly proportional to the number of people working in the industry' (Chapter 6), and the pyramid, the bench and the training campuses of Chapters 11 and 12 were built on that arithmetic.21 Fixed-price work grew, but in the year to March 2008 fixed-price projects still brought Infosys only 31 per cent of its services revenue (Chapter 12).22

The last clause is not settled. Chapter 19 found three of the largest employers telling an American regulator that AI threatened the volume of work they sold, and it also found that the slowdown in graduate hiring began before AI coding tools were a reported part of their work.23 The verdict, then, is mixed. Foreign exchange explains why programmers' time became the export the state encouraged. The surplus of engineers followed a shortage and was as much a result of the trade as a cause of it. The American market pulled students into engineering, then bought more of their work over the wire than of their time on site. Whether AI ends the hourly model is the question the scenarios take up.

The present, in brief

The prologue and Chapter 19 set out where things stood in 2026: Nasscom estimating that revenue was growing faster than jobs, the largest firms' combined headcount stalled, and their annual filings warning that clients wanted the savings from AI.24 The supply side had been changing too, and earlier than the AI story suggests. According to the same Avanti Fellows analysis, the approved undergraduate intake of engineering colleges under the All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE) peaked at about 1.7 million seats in 2014-15 and fell to about 1.25 million by 2021-22, with roughly a third of those seats left empty.25 The same analysis found AICTE's own data on graduates who were placed in jobs unusable.26 The country that built an engineer factory cannot yet measure, from official figures, how many of its graduates find engineering work.

Two people stand at the ends of this book's present. One is the trainee whom the reporter Balakrishna Ganeshan called Gayatri, a pseudonym his report marks as a changed name. The prologue told how she waited nearly two years for her joining date and was let go from Infosys's Mysuru training campus in February 2025, four months after joining. 'They wasted two and a half years of my career,' she told him.27 The other is Rishabh Mishra, whose course at the Indian Institute of Information Technology, Design and Manufacturing in Jabalpur was due to end in May 2026; Rest of World reported that fewer than a quarter of his 400 classmates had job offers (Chapter 19).28 The four scenarios are, among other things, four answers to what their degrees will be worth.

The thinning pyramid

The first scenario supposes that clients stop paying for hours and keep the savings from AI for themselves.

An Indian services firm has long been described as a pyramid: a wide base of junior engineers, a narrower band of team leads, and a few senior managers at the top. The base was where the money was made, because juniors were billed at rates well above their pay. In this scenario the base is what shrinks. The firms survive, smaller at the bottom and older on average, and the engineer factory turns out graduates whom the firms no longer need in volume.

The evidence for it is in Chapter 19: the Infosys filings in which clients demand productivity commitments and begin 'selectively insourcing', and the reporting on what Forbes India called AI-led deflation, in which a deal for the same work was worth, by HCLTech's chief executive's rough estimate, a fifth less.29 The Forbes India report also linked to coverage of Claude Cowork, a product of Anthropic, a point the closing pages of this epilogue return to. Chapter 19 also applied to this work the test that Alan Blinder proposed in 2006 (Chapter 14): what could be sent down a wire was what the filings now showed being repriced. The NITI report itself describes the mechanism. In an IT services company, it says, AI 'typically translates to a shrinking of entry-level roles involving QA and support'.30

For education the consequence is stark. Colleges would keep producing graduates trained for the base of a pyramid that is narrowing. The prologue's waiting fresher would become the normal case, not the exception, and the joining date would stop being a date.

The signposts are measurable. Fresher offers as a share of total headcount at the five largest firms would fall year after year. Revenue per employee would rise while headcount stayed flat or fell. Company filings would show more fixed-price and outcome-based contracts, in which the client pays for a result rather than for time. Engineering intake would stay high while the share of graduates in engineering jobs fell, which is why the missing placement data matters.

The counter-evidence is just as concrete. As Chapter 19 records, Infosys's offers of employment had more than tripled from their trough in the year to March 2024, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) was still taking freshers and said it would hire on demand, and Nasscom still reported net hiring.31 And the delayed joining dates that left Gayatri waiting began in 2022, two years before Infosys's annual filings first mentioned AI code generation.32 By 2026 the pyramid had not visibly thinned. The scenario supposes that it will.

The diamond

The second scenario supposes that AI creates new paid work, and that the price of each remaining engineer rises.

In this version the shape changes from a pyramid to a diamond: fewer people at the bottom, a wide middle, and a narrow top. The image comes from the staffing executive Kamal Karanth, who, in a Reuters Breakingviews column's paraphrase, described a diamond with a big middle layer replacing the old pyramid (Chapter 19).33 The people in the middle are paid for judgement, for knowledge of a client's business and for checking and directing what AI tools produce.

The evidence comes partly from the companies, whose claims about AI revenue and their own optimism Chapter 19 sets out, and partly from the NITI report. 'We estimate the IT Services industry to grow 3-9% annually,' the roadmap says, and it expects 'the price-per-FTE for the industry to grow 5-8% annually over the next few years'.34 FTE means full-time equivalent, one person's working time. On that view each engineer becomes more valuable, and the upside path to 10 million tech jobs opens.

The book has one documented case of this pattern. In 1986, as Chapter 8 told, the computerised reservation system at the New Delhi railway booking office let any of its 50 counters sell a ticket for any train. In Rajaraman's account there was 'no reduction of staff; in fact there was an increase as more counters were operated with more clerks working simultaneously'.35 On Chapter 8's reading, the machine raised each clerk's output and demand that the queues had held down absorbed it. The diamond scenario is a wager that the same is true of software: that demand for it, at a lower price, is large enough to employ more people than the tools displace. The railway case is one system in one city, told by one historian. It shows only that the pattern has happened in India before, not how much demand is waiting for software in the 2030s.

For education this scenario is kinder but harder. It needs fewer graduates who can follow a specification and more who can judge whether a system is right. AICTE declared 2025 its 'Year of Artificial Intelligence' and its chairman said a committee of experts was revising the model curricula so that AI could be taught in all branches of engineering.36 No revised curriculum had been found by the time of writing. Chapter 12 showed employers judging most graduates of the new colleges unready, and the regulator responding with accreditation.

The signposts would be revenue per employee and average pay rising together, lateral hiring of experienced engineers outpacing campus hiring, and AI revenue growing faster than total revenue, reported on definitions that can be compared between firms. At present, as Chapter 19 found, they cannot be.37

The counter-evidence is the first scenario's evidence. NITI's rising price per worker and the deflation reported seven months later measure different things, price per person and value per deal, but they point in opposite directions, and nothing published by 2026 settles which is right.38

The client moves in

The third scenario supposes that multinationals stop buying Indian engineers' time from Indian vendors and employ the engineers themselves.

This has a long history in the book. Texas Instruments, by its own account, began operating a software design centre in Bangalore in 1985, and Chapter 15 followed the foreign-owned engineering centres that came after it.39 The industry now calls them global capability centres (GCCs): offices that a foreign company owns and staffs in India to do its own technology, research or back-office work. Chapters 15 and 19 give the counts by Nasscom and the consultancy Zinnov, which by the year to March 2026 put GCC revenue close to the level they had projected for 2030.40 Namita Adavi of Zinnov said India's long history as an offshoring centre had 'created a flywheel effect in terms of the talent that is being churned out'.41 Infosys listed the risk plainly in its report for the year to March 2025: clients might build their own technology capability 'by setting up or expanding their own GCC in India'.42 Karnataka's state policy for 2024-29 set a target of attracting 500 new GCCs to the state by 2029.43

In this scenario Indian engineers keep their jobs but change employers. The national champions that the book has followed since the 1970s, TCS, Infosys and Wipro, would matter less, and the Indian offices of foreign companies would matter more. It would bring back the question Chapter 5 asked about IBM's departure in 1978: who owns the capability that Indians build? For education it might change little. GCCs hire from the same colleges, though they may hire fewer people at higher pay.

The signposts would be GCC headcount growing while the large services firms' headcount stayed flat; services firms earning more from building and running GCCs for clients; office leasing in Bengaluru and Hyderabad led by GCCs; and states beyond Karnataka adopting their own policies under the national framework for states that the 2025-26 Union Budget promised to formulate.44

The counter-evidence is about the numbers themselves. Every GCC figure comes from Nasscom, the lobby group, and Zinnov, a consultancy that sells advice to GCCs. Both have an interest in a growing count. Definitions have shifted before: in September 2024 Nasscom restated its GCC revenue and headcount after one of its five-yearly surveys, and it later revised its estimate of the whole industry's revenue for the year to March 2025 without giving a reason.45 No independent count of GCCs or their employees was found for this book.

The builder state

The fourth scenario supposes that barriers abroad and ambitions at home turn the industry inward, with the Indian state as builder and buyer.

The route that carried Indian engineers to American client sites since the 1990s was the H-1B visa, the subject of Chapter 13. Chapter 19 told how a presidential proclamation of September 2025 put a price of $100,000 on new petitions, how the administration claimed a 92 per cent fall in the largest outsourcing firms' registrations, and how by October 2026 two federal courts had vacated the agencies' policies for collecting the fee, though not the proclamations themselves, while a third court had upheld it, so that the route was contested rather than closed.46 The Indian firms' approved petitions had begun to fall before the fee, by the count of the National Foundation for American Policy, a pro-immigration research group.47

At home, the state has a record of building at population scale. Chapters 16 and 17 told how it built Aadhaar, the biometric identity system, and UPI, the payments network, and asked why India's largest platforms came from the state rather than from private product firms. Chapter 19 followed the IndiaAI Mission of 2024 and the start-up Sarvam's first foundation models, the large general-purpose AI systems on which applications are built.48 The NITI roadmap proposes an 'India Open-Source AI Commons' and a 'federated national compute and innovation grid'.49 Nasscom reported that domestic revenue was set to grow 7.9 per cent in the year to March 2026, faster than the industry as a whole.50

In this scenario more Indian engineers work on Indian problems for Indian users, through the state, its contractors and domestic product firms. It is the scenario in which the book's first question, why India built services rather than products, might begin to get a different answer. For education it would mean colleges training engineers for a home market that has rarely been their main destination.

The signposts would be government spending on AI and digital infrastructure sustained beyond one budget cycle; Indian-language models in use in state services; domestic revenue rising as a share of the industry's total; and a rising share of new graduates hired by domestic product firms and public bodies. GitHub's projection of Indian developers on its platform, given in Chapter 19, counts accounts on one company's service, not jobs, but a rising number of Indians writing code for Indian users would show up there first.51

The counter-evidence is serious. Inc42 judged that Sarvam's models, while competitive, typically trail the leading foreign models in 'raw reasoning depth'.52 The NITI report itself records that India's share of granted AI patents fell from 8-10 per cent in 2010 to under 5 per cent in 2023, according to the 2025 AI Index, and that India loses more AI talent to migration than it gains.53 Building identity and payments systems was a feat of engineering and organisation. Building frontier AI needs far more capital, and the state's record in the first may not carry over to the second.

Reading the signposts

The four scenarios are not exclusive. The pyramid can thin at the base while the middle thickens; GCCs can grow while the state builds; visa barriers can push work offshore to the very GCCs that take it from Indian vendors. The likeliest India of 2035 combines them in proportions that no source available to this book can predict, and this book does not try.

What it can say is where to look. Several of the signposts are numbers that India does not yet collect well. AICTE's placement data are unusable, as noted above.54 Nasscom's headcount is an industry body's estimate, revised as definitions change. GCC counts come from parties that sell to GCCs. AI revenue has no common definition. The NITI roadmap's own base mixes the industry with every other employer of technical staff. A reader in 2030 who wants to know which future is arriving will need, first, better measures of the present: an independent count of software employment, a placement series for engineering graduates, and revenue per employee for the large firms, published on comparable terms.

The book's history also suggests a caution about forecasts. In 2024 Nasscom and Zinnov projected GCC revenue for 2030, and on their own later count it was nearly reached by 2026.55 In October 2025 NITI expected the price of each engineer to rise; by May 2026 Forbes India was reporting AI deflation.56 Projections in this industry have been overtaken or contradicted within a few years, and most of them were made by people with an interest in the answer.

The machines have been forecast to take this work before. In 1994 the World Bank's study of the Indian industry, discussed in Chapter 6, listed among the weaknesses of sending programmers abroad that 'Demand for these skills is likely to be eroded by tools that automate many of these tasks', and added that 'a long-term decline in demand for routine or low-level programmers has been forecasted'.57 Four years later Heeks named 'automation from new technology' among the risks to the trade, and saw a trap in it: clients let work go offshore only when it was tightly specified, and such projects were 'more amenable to automated software tools'.58 The tools of the 1990s did not erode the demand for Indian programmers; exports kept growing fast (Chapter 6).59 That does not mean the warning is wrong now. Heeks's trap describes the documented, specified work that Chapter 19's filings say the new tools are for. It means that a forecast of the end of this work has been made before, by careful people, and was wrong for thirty years.

Readers should weigh what this book says about AI with one more fact in mind. This book was researched and drafted with the help of an AI model made by Anthropic, a company whose products, Claude Code and Claude Cowork, appear in sources discussed in Chapter 19.

The joining date

The book began with a joining date that kept moving. For Gayatri the date came after nearly two years, and the job lasted four months. She told the reporter it had been her dream job.60 For Rishabh Mishra and his classmates in Jabalpur, the reporting ends before any date. Some of them were thinking of further study instead. Mishra was doubtful: 'after one year, if you return to the job market, your degree is even more irrelevant'.61

His sentence is about education as much as about jobs. For half a century the engineering degree in India was a ticket to a particular kind of work: billable hours, learned first on a company training campus and then sold to a client by the month. The colleges, the entrance examinations, the coaching towns and the company campuses were built around that ticket. Each of the four scenarios keeps some of that machinery and discards some. In the thinning pyramid the degree buys less. In the diamond it buys more, but only for those taught to judge as well as to code. In the third scenario it buys a job with a different employer. In the fourth it buys a place in building something for India itself.

None of the evidence in this book says which future will come. It says that the engineer factory was built on purpose, by the state and the companies and millions of families, in answer to the questions of its time. The question for the next ten years is what a joining date means when the factory's product is no longer hours.
