The Engineer Factory

Chapter 7. The training economy (1980-1995)

Mohini Jain, a student from Meerut, was told by the management of Sri Siddhartha Medical College in Tumkur, in Karnataka, that she could join its medical degree course in the session beginning in February or March 1991. The management asked her to deposit Rs 60,000 as the tuition fee for the first year and to furnish a bank guarantee for the fees of the years that followed.1 That was about $2,600 at the year's exchange rate.2 The Supreme Court later recorded what happened next in a single sentence: 'The petitioner's father informed the management that it was beyond his means to pay the exorbitant annual fee of Rs.60,000 and as a consequence she was denied admission to the medical college.'3 She also alleged that the college had asked for a further 'capitation fee of rupees four and a half lakhs', that is Rs 450,000. The management 'vehemently denied the same'.4

Mohini Jain wanted to be a doctor, not a programmer, but her case belongs in a book about software because of what the judges had to describe in deciding it. By 1991 a seat in a professional college in southern and western India, in engineering as in medicine, had a price, sometimes several prices, and the states had written them into notifications under the same laws. Within a year a larger bench of the Supreme Court heard private engineering and medical colleges from four states say that the ruling in her favour would force them to shut.5

This chapter is about how India made the people the software trade needed in the fifteen years after 1980, when the public universities could not grow fast enough, and about who paid. Chapter 6 followed the programmers who were sent abroad by the hour. Here the question is where they came from. There were three answers, and the state had a hand in each. A government panel invented a new degree. Four state governments let private trusts open engineering colleges that charged their students the cost of their education and, the courts later found, sometimes more. And private companies began to sell programming in four-week and twelve-week courses, and from 1987, through franchises, in smaller towns. Alongside all three, the race for the few elite seats created a coaching trade of its own.

A degree for commerce graduates

The state saw the shortage coming. In 1980, V. Rajaraman, the historian of Indian computing, later wrote, the Electronics Commission 'foresaw the problem of scarcity of human resources for the emerging computer industry'.6 Rajaraman, then the Kanpur professor who had built India's first academic computer science programme (Chapter 2), was a member of the Commission, and he chaired the Panel on Computer Manpower Development that it set up.7

The panel made two proposals. The first was to expand undergraduate computer science at the IITs and other engineering colleges. The second, which Rajaraman called novel, was a new postgraduate course, the Master of Computer Applications (MCA).8 It was aimed at the large number of Indian students who took three-year B.Sc degrees in physics, chemistry and mathematics, or B.Com degrees in commerce and economics. These degrees, in Rajaraman's words, 'do not prepare a student for a profession which requires specific skills'.9 The panel felt that 'India needed a large number of persons to develop business/management information systems for use in organizations both in India and abroad'.10 The phrase 'and abroad' is worth noticing. In 1980 the state's manpower planners already had foreign employers in view.

The curriculum combined three subjects: computer science 'with emphasis in software engineering and systems analysis', mathematics for operations research and statistics, and management science, so that graduates could talk to managers in their own language. It included 'a semester of actual system design in an organization', a placement in a working office.11 Where a B.Tech in computer science trained people to design hardware and software, the MCA trained them to design software for business. The government decided at first to fund ten institutions in full to run it.12 Rajaraman published his curriculum proposal in CSI Communications, the journal of the Computer Society of India, in October 1981.13

Three decades later Rajaraman judged his own creation generously. 'The availability of well trained MCAs was one of the contributing factors for the rapid growth of software services industry in India,' he wrote.14 That is the designer assessing the design. The panel's report has not been seen for this book, and no series of MCA admissions for the 1980s has been found. The first firm number in Rajaraman's history comes from much later: about 7,000 MCAs graduated in 1998.15

The other half of the panel's plan ran into the narrowness of the elite system. In 1978, the year IIT Kanpur opened the first undergraduate computer science course at any IIT, more than 100,000 students sat the entrance examination for 1,500 IIT places in all.16 The other IITs soon started computer science degrees of their own, but IIT places stayed scarce, and the World Bank later estimated that as many as half of their graduates emigrated.17 Rajendra Pawar, a co-founder of the training company NIIT, recalled the position in 1981 this way: 'there were a handful of people coming out of the IITs, but they were not the ones writing programs.'18

'A bold decision'

The decisive change came from the states. In the Sixth Five-Year Plan (1980-85), according to a 2010 paper from the National University of Educational Planning and Administration, quoted by the journalist Thomas Manuel, the central and state governments were 'finding it difficult to expand technical education', and 'a few state governments', especially Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, 'took a bold decision to permit private registered societies and trusts to establish and run technical institutions on a self-financing basis'.19 Self-financing meant that the students paid.

The official count of colleges shows how fast the decision worked. In the academic year 1980-81 India had 149 engineering and technology colleges. Five years later, in 1985-86, it had 242, an increase of 62 per cent. Growth then slowed: there were 289 colleges in 1990-91 and 337 in 1994-95.20 The great expansion of engineering colleges that Chapter 12 describes came after 1996. The surge of the early 1980s was the first, smaller rehearsal for it. The national series is not broken down by state, so it cannot show how much of the surge came from the four states the NUEPA paper names.21

These are counts of institutions, not of students. The government's own figure for seats is uncertain. The Programme of Action that accompanied the education policy, published in 1992, said there were 'over 200' recognised technical institutions at degree level with an annual admission capacity of 40,000.22 Yet the official series already counted 289 engineering colleges in 1990-91 and 300 in 1992-93.23 The Programme's numbers match the mid-1980s count better than the early-1990s one, and probably repeat older data.24 No reliable series of engineering admissions by year and state for this period has been found.

New colleges needed teachers, and the people who could teach computing were the people the industry wanted most. In 1985 the Department of Electronics set up a committee under S. Sampath, which 'suggested training teachers in Computer Science for the colleges which were being newly established'.25 The difficulty did not go away. A World Bank study of 1994 listed 'the scarcity of teachers and trainers, particularly in public education institutes' among the causes of the shortage of software staff.26 It also blamed the trade's habit of 'renting out programmers' for shortages of teaching staff as well as for the brain drain that Chapter 6 measures.27

Four weeks of BASIC

Outside the degree system, a different kind of supplier appeared. NIIT was founded in 1981 by Rajendra Pawar and Vijay Thadani.28 In a 2022 interview with the magazine PCQuest, Pawar described the state of computer education when they began: 'At that time, it was non-existent. But the manufacturing industry was already anticipating a problem of talent shortage.' NIIT, he said, 'was born out of that threat'.29 This is a founder's account, given forty years later, and it should be read as one.

The timetable he remembered is precise. 'The first two courses that NIIT announced in 1981 were one, an entry level BASIC. It was a 4-week programme, 5 days a week and 2 hours a day.' The second was a longer programme: 'Entry level COBOL, for 12 weeks.'30 BASIC was a simple teaching language. COBOL was the language of business data processing, and Pawar called it 'state of the art' at the time. NIIT also taught FORTRAN, the scientific language, to engineers and scientists.31 Forty hours of BASIC was not an education in computer science. It was an introduction to a trade.

The curriculum grew with its customers. NIIT ran a course for company executives called 'How to buy a computer', and added systems analysis, design and databases. It made an arrangement with the company of Edward Yourdon, whom Pawar called 'the father of software engineering', to teach Yourdon's structured methodology, and Pawar recalled that 'TCS was a very big participant'.32 Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), the firm whose early years Chapter 4 described, was buying training from a company that had existed for only a few years. But the core of the business stayed where it began: 'quite clearly the largest need was for people who could write programs.'33 The export trade of Chapter 6 bought programmers by the hour, and NIIT sold the hours of training that made them.

NIIT's growth came from franchising. Rajaraman dates the start of its franchise model, 'to spread their courses to smaller towns in India', to 1987.34 Pawar described it as an invention: 'We found like-minded entrepreneurs in cities and motivated them to go back to their towns and set up an NIIT centre.' He recalled that 'at its peak (there were thousands)' every centre had the same look and feel, and that the Far Eastern Economic Review 'talked of it as the McDonald's of education'.35 The Review article itself has not been seen.

The franchisees were small businesspeople, and a 2002 profile in the trade magazine Dataquest named some of them. Pratap Agarwal, who had worked as a software engineer in the United States, opened a single NIIT centre in Chandigarh in 1988 and said that by 2002 he ran more than sixty. Others opened centres in Dehradun in 1990 and Faridabad in 1992; the Faridabad franchisee, Captain Virendar Singh, had worked in human resources at TCS and Wipro. Ludhiana followed in 1993 and Amritsar in 1994.36 The same profile reported that in 1988 the magazine Asiaweek had called NIIT 'the biggest computer trainer this side of the Suez', and it dated the Review's comparison to November 2000, giving it as the 'McDonald's of Software Business'.37

NIIT was not alone. Aptech, founded by Atul Nishar in 1986 according to a 2014 profile in Outlook Business, was the other company that, on that profile's account, 'defined the space' with NIIT. Looking back, that profile described the 1980s and 1990s as years of queues of students waiting to sign up for multi-year computer courses.38 Rajaraman, writing as a historian, called Aptech and NIIT 'pioneers' of 'non-formal training in software development and computer use', which 'also provided the much needed human resources'.39

None of these companies has published its enrolments or fees for the 1980s, and the founders' accounts are the accounts of founders. The best independent picture comes from the World Bank's 1994 study by Nagy Hanna, which reviewed the trade in the early 1990s, drawing on industry surveys. It found that 'an estimated 1,000 proprietary organizations turn out about 80,000 trainees annually from courses ranging from two weeks to two years.' Fees ran from Rs 800 for two months to Rs 8,000 for a one-year course, roughly $25 to $250 at the 1993 exchange rate.40 The verdict on quality was harsh. 'Industry specialists estimate that 98 percent of the training organizations are below standard,' Hanna wrote, and 'Few of the graduates find employment'. Only ten to fourteen of the larger organisations offered good programmes. Between them they trained about 40,000 people a year, some 30,000 of them in basic two-year programmes meant to qualify them for entry-level programming jobs.41

Hanna's study also gave the private trade's share. The proprietary organisations, it said, 'now produce almost 85 percent of the programmers and 40 percent of the systems analysts', and the large ones had been doubling their capacity and output over the previous few years.42 If that estimate is right, by the early 1990s the typical Indian programmer had not been trained by a university at all. He, or less often she, had been trained by a company that sold courses, often in a franchised centre in a provincial town.

The state tried, late, to bring some order to this market. The Department of Electronics created a scheme called DOE Accreditation of Computer Courses (DOEACC), under which private institutes that met set standards were accredited to teach standard courses. These ran from basic programming, the 'O level', to a master's-level qualification in computer science, the 'C level', with examinations set by the Computer Society of India and the Institute of Electronics and Telecommunications Engineers. Hanna called the scheme 'recently-developed' in 1994 and saw in it 'flexibility for recycling graduates from various fields into information technology'.43 Recycling graduates from other fields was exactly what NIIT, Aptech and the MCA had been doing for a decade.

The dining table in Kota

At the other end of the market, competition for the few elite places produced its own industry. One account of its origin concerns V.K. Bansal, a mechanical engineer trained at Banaras Hindu University who had gone to work for JK Synthetics in Kota in 1971. According to a 2023 book by Pradip K. Saha about the education company Byju's, Bansal developed muscular dystrophy, began coaching eight students at his dining table in the early 1980s, and turned to teaching full-time in 1983 when his company shut down. Bansal Classes, as a named institution, dates from 1991.44 The account comes from a single recent source and is told here as Saha tells it. What grew from it lies outside this chapter. By 2023, on Saha's figures, Kota had more than 150 coaching institutes and about 200,000 students, and more than 1.1 million candidates competed for 17,385 IIT places.45

Regulating a surge

The states that opened the door to private colleges also tried to police what came through it. In Karnataka an ordinance of 1983 against capitation fees was replaced by the Karnataka Educational Institutions (Prohibition of Capitation Fee) Act, 1984, which took effect from 11 July 1983.46 A capitation fee was a payment for admission, over and above tuition, and the laws defined it broadly. Maharashtra's Act of 1987, which received the governor's assent on 13 April 1988, defined it as 'any amount, by whatever name called', in excess of the fees the government had prescribed or approved.47

Maharashtra's legislators explained themselves in the preamble. Capitation, they wrote, 'is on the increase in the State', and 'this undesirable practice has been contributing to large scale commercialisation of education which is not conducive to the maintenance of educational standards'. They called it 'this evil practice'.48 Tamil Nadu passed its own Act in 1992, and Andhra Pradesh already had one, from 1983.49 Each of the four states that had licensed the private colleges thus also had a law against what the colleges were doing.

National policy said the same thing in two minds. The National Policy on Education of 1986, as the Union government quoted it to the Supreme Court, declared that 'the commercialisation of technical and professional education will be curbed', and in the next sentence promised that 'An alternative system will be devised to involve private and voluntary effort in this section of education'.50 Maharashtra cited the first half of this in its preamble.51

In 1987 Parliament gave the All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE) statutory powers.52 The AICTE Act charged the Council with 'the proper planning and co-ordinated development of the technical education system throughout the country'.53 Its functions read like a list of the problems of the 1980s. The Council was to 'make forecast of the needed growth and development in technical education', the manpower planning of the 1980 panel now written into law. It was to 'fix norms and guidelines for charging tuition and other fees', to 'grant approval for starting new technical institutions and for introduction of new courses or programmes', and to 'take all necessary steps to prevent commercialisation of technical education'.54

Two features of the Act stand out when it is read against the training economy. The first is its definition of technical education: 'programmes of education, research and training in engineering, technology, architecture, town planning, management, pharmacy and applied art and crafts', together with any other field the central government chose to add by notification.55 Computer applications and software are not named. A technical institution, moreover, was 'an institution, not being a university'.56 On a reading of the text, the franchised programming courses of NIIT and Aptech fell outside the regulator's definitions unless the government notified otherwise. No source in hand shows how such courses were treated in practice, so this remains an inference from the statute. Hanna's report suggests that the inference holds for the early 1990s: the government, it said, was 'considering regulating the proprietary training organizations and setting quality standards, but nothing has been done up to now.'57

The second feature is timing. The regulator came after the surge it was meant to discipline: the number of engineering colleges had already risen from 149 to 242 before the Act was passed.58 By 1992 the government's Programme of Action admitted an urgent need to strengthen the AICTE. It asked the Council to work towards a 'realistic fee structure' based on the actual cost of education, to encourage private participation in technical education, and to add a 'penal clause' to its own Act to curb malpractices.59 The Union government told the Supreme Court that the AICTE was approving new engineering colleges on the condition that they collect no capitation fee, and that under its guidelines an unaided college could recover about 70 per cent of the cost of a student's education in fees, against 20 per cent in a government institution.60

Three prices for one seat

The fees on paper were set by the states. In Maharashtra the government prescribed a uniform fee of Rs 6,500 a year in private unaided engineering colleges, raised to Rs 8,500 in 1991 (about $375 at that year's rate). In 1992 it raised the fee only for students from outside the state, to Rs 17,000. The government filled 90 per cent of the seats in private engineering colleges with its own nominees and left 10 per cent to the managements.61 In Andhra Pradesh the permitted fee in private engineering colleges for 1991-92 was Rs 10,000 a year.62 These were the official fees. Capitation payments, by their nature, were not recorded, and no contemporary figure for capitation in engineering has been found for this book.

The clearest record of how a seat was priced comes from medicine, in the notification at the centre of Mohini Jain's case. On 5 June 1989 Karnataka fixed the fees that private medical colleges could charge. Students admitted to 'Government seats' paid Rs 2,000 a year. Other Karnataka students could be charged up to Rs 25,000. 'Indian students from outside Karnataka' could be charged up to Rs 60,000.63 The same course, in the same college, had three prices, and the highest was thirty times the lowest.

The colleges defended the scale. Their counsel told the Court that the students who paid more belonged to a different class: those on government seats were 'meritorious' and the rest were not. The association of private medical colleges argued that they received no aid from the central or state governments, that a five-year medical course cost them about Rs 5 lakh (Rs 500,000) per student, and that because 40 per cent of the seats paid only Rs 2,000, the cost had to fall on the rest. The fees, on this argument, were not profit but recovery of cost.64

The bench of two judges, Kuldip Singh and R.M. Sahai, rejected this entirely on 30 July 1992. In Justice Kuldip Singh's words: 'Capitation fee is nothing but a price for selling education. The concept of "teaching shops" is contrary to the constitutional scheme and is wholly abhorrent to the Indian culture and heritage.'65 'Education in India has never been a commodity for sale,' he added.66 The judgment named the social effect plainly. Capitation brought 'a clear class bias': 'A poor student with better merit cannot get admission because he has no money whereas the rich can purchase the admission.'67 The Court held that the right to education flowed directly from the right to life under Article 21 of the Constitution. The Rs 60,000 charged to outsiders was 'not tuition fee but in fact a capitation fee', and a private college run with government recognition should be prohibited from charging more than the government's Rs 2,000 from any student.68

The case concerned a medical college, but its reasoning applied to every recognised private professional college, and the engineering colleges understood that at once.

Hyderabad, 1992

What the private engineering colleges did when the law left them room is recorded in the judgment that followed. Andhra Pradesh had 'a number of private engineering colleges', the Supreme Court noted. Until the academic year 1992-93 all their seats were filled by the convenor of the state's common entrance test. The managements had no say in admissions and were allowed only to charge a fee 'relatively higher than the fees charged in the Government Engineering Colleges'.69 On 15 April 1992 the state inserted a new section, 3-A, into its Act. The colleges took it to mean that they could admit half their students 'according to their choice, irrespective of merit, so long as they have qualified in the entrance test'.70 The Court's comment was dry: 'It is obvious that such a stand meant collection of capitation fee as much as they could.'71

'There was an uproar among the student and teaching community against such admissions,' the judgment continues. The government told the colleges on 26 July 1992 not to admit anyone until rules had been made. By then the colleges said they had already filled their half. The convenor, for his part, had allotted students to only half of each college's seats, 'thereby sending an explicit signal that the colleges were free to fill up the rest on their own'.72 The colleges then charged the same Rs 10,000 fee to both halves. 'Thus they have reaped a double advantage,' the Court observed.73

The students admitted in this way were the rank and file of the training economy, and the judgment records them only as a class. Their counsel, Shanti Bhushan, argued that 'they were innocent parties and had obtained admission in a bona fide belief that their admissions were being made properly'. By the time the Supreme Court ruled they had been studying for months.74 The Court let them stay. It ordered the colleges to charge 'the 50% "free students"', for the rest of their course, only the fee of the university engineering colleges, which it called the 'government fee', and to pay the balance already collected that year into the government's account within six weeks or lose their recognition and affiliation.75 The judgment does not spell out which half it meant by 'free students'.

The free seat and the payment seat

The larger question reached a bench of five judges in Unni Krishnan, J.P. v. State of Andhra Pradesh, decided on 4 February 1993. The petitioners, Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy wrote, were 'running medical/engineering colleges in the States of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu', and they said that if Mohini Jain was followed 'they will have to close down; no other option is left to them'.76

Both sides put their case in strong terms. The colleges argued that the law of supply and demand should be given free play, and that 'only when there was profit motive that persons with means would come forward to open more and more schools and colleges'. At the least, they said, a citizen had the right to set up 'a self-financing educational institution, which institution might also be described as one providing cost-based education'.77 Against them stood, among others, the Indian Medical Council and the AICTE itself. Their argument, as the headnote summarised it, was that 'a capitation fee did not cease to be a capitation fee just because it was called as cost-based education or by calling the Institution concerned as a self-financing Institution'.78 The Union government took a middle line. Private institutions could not be forced to charge only government fees, it argued, and 'It would be unrealistic and unwise to discourage private initiative' in providing education.79

The Court partly overruled Mohini Jain. It agreed that a right to education flowed from the right to life, but held that its content was limited by what the state could afford. Citizens could not 'demand that the State provide adequate number of medical colleges, engineering colleges and other educational institutions to satisfy all their educational needs'.80 The right to free education ran to the age of fourteen. Above that, private colleges without state aid could charge more than government colleges, within a ceiling, but 'commercialisation of education' was still not allowed.81

To give this effect the Court wrote a scheme, to apply from 1993-94. It covered every private professional college, defined to include 'colleges of engineering and colleges and institutions imparting technical education including electronics, computer sciences'.82 At least half of the seats in each college were to be 'free seats', filled by the government or university on merit at the government fee. The rest were 'payment seats', filled by those prepared to pay a fixed higher fee, but still in order of merit. 'There shall be no quota reserved for the management or for any family, caste or community which may have established such college.'83 All admissions would run through one state notification for each kind of college.84 The payment half, in effect, was to pay for the free half.

The scheme was a compromise between the market reading and the commercialisation reading of the 1980s. It accepted that private colleges would charge their students much more than the state did, and it tried to keep merit as the rule for deciding who could pay. It governed admissions to private professional colleges for the rest of the period this chapter covers, and for some years after. On 31 October 2002 eleven judges of the Supreme Court, in T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka, held that the scheme 'was not correct' and overruled it, leaving unaided colleges to fix their own fees, though still without capitation fees or profiteering (Chapter 12).85

What the employers found

The people who came out of this system went into a job market that was short of them and suspicious of them at the same time. Hanna's study found that in India 'the demand for trained software personnel far outstrips the supply, and the shortage is a major constraint to the industry's expansion'. The shortage placed a heavy training burden on firms and caused high staff turnover.86 The same study warned that manpower forecasts made by the Department of Electronics and others were unreliable for lack of basic data.87

Yet employers trusted few of the public institutions. Only 275 industrial training institutes, polytechnics, colleges and universities were qualified and supported to run computer training. Most of them were short of teachers, equipment and books, and had weak links with industry. In 1991, Hanna reported, the number of MCA graduates rose by 50 per cent, yet only 5 per cent were hired by software exporters. One large software house, the report said, considered students from only thirty universities.88 The MCA was producing graduates, but the exporters were not yet taking many of them.

The large firms trained their own. The World Bank reproduced, from a background paper written by the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, a short account of in-house training at TCS, which had just completed twenty-five years. TCS said that it recruited able graduates from leading institutions and turned them into systems professionals through a demanding initial training programme. Its corporate training centre in Bombay (Mumbai since 1995) had run 6,000 programme days in the previous year, about 120,000 person-days of training, and the company said it spent 7 to 8 per cent of its revenue on training.89 These are the company's own figures, relayed by its industry association. Hanna judged that large firms did 'a satisfactory job' of in-house training, while small and medium firms did very little, for fear that trained staff would leave for better-paid jobs.90

The export trade also pulled people away from everything else. Hanna estimated that software exports could 'cannibalize' other sectors 'by taking almost one-half of all new computing course graduates from government-initiated courses'.91 The World Bank's remedy was more of what the 1980s had already produced: it proposed 'recycling graduates in other disciplines into software engineering', inviting multinationals and non-resident Indians into computer education, and building 'centers of excellence in IT education on the lines of IITs'.92

Who paid

The question of who could enter the new profession has a partial answer in the legal record, and the answer is those whose families could pay. Karnataka's notification charged an outsider thirty times what a government-seat student paid for the same medical course.93 The Andhra colleges filled half their engineering seats at their own discretion, and the Court found it 'not possible to investigate or verify for what consideration those admissions were made'.94 A family that wanted an engineering seat in the south or west, and could not win one on merit in a government college, could buy one. Mohini Jain's father, in the one sentence the record gives him, said that he could not.95

The training companies offered a cheaper door. Their courses cost from a few hundred to a few thousand rupees, and in the World Bank's phrase they recycled graduates from other fields into computing.96 They were also, on Hanna's evidence, a gamble: most institutes were below standard and few of their students found jobs.97 The franchise spread the gamble to towns like Chandigarh, Dehradun and Ludhiana, where local entrepreneurs opened centres under the NIIT name.98

Of the women in this story the sources say little. The only figure is from Richard Heeks of the University of Manchester, who found in research published in 1998 that 'Only some 10% of Indian software developers are female'. Women interviewed for his study nonetheless felt that software offered 'a more relaxed and less discriminatory atmosphere than many other Indian employment options'.99 Heeks cites a 1985 study of men and women in software from SNDT Women's University in Bombay, which has not been found for this book.100 The caste and regional background of the trainees is not recorded in any source used here. Nor, apart from Mohini Jain and the unnamed Andhra students, does any student of a private engineering college or computer institute of this period speak in the record. Their absence is the largest gap in this chapter.

The wire

By the mid-1990s the training economy had made software a career that could be bought. Before 1980 the way in ran through a handful of elite institutions, IBM and the state, as earlier chapters described. By 1995 it also ran through private engineering colleges in four states, MCA courses, and franchised centres in district towns. All of them taught the skills the trade bought, from COBOL to systems analysis.

What those people did depended on where the work could be done. In 1985, the same year the Sampath committee was asked to find teachers for the new colleges, the Department of Electronics negotiated with the Department of Telecommunications to let Texas Instruments set up a dedicated satellite link from its centre in Bangalore (Bengaluru since 2014) to Dallas, a story Chapter 8 tells.101 The training economy supplied the people. The policies of the mid-1980s, and that link to Dallas, began to decide whether they would work in India or be flown to their clients.
