The Engineer Factory

Chapter 18. The pandemic boom (2020-2023)

On 21 September 2022 Rishad Premji, the chairman of Wipro, spoke at the convention of the All India Management Association. 'The reality is that there are people today working for Wipro and working directly for one of our competitors and we have actually discovered 300 people in the last few months who are doing exactly that,' he said.1 Wipro had dismissed them.2 The practice he described, working for two employers at once, depended on something that had changed two and a half years earlier, when the industry's engineers left their offices.

For thirty years the Indian services firm had sold its clients a place as well as people: secure floors, dedicated links and guarded campuses. In a few weeks of 2020 the place went away and the work carried on. Demand for the industry's services surged, and the firms hired on a scale they had never attempted. Engineers left their jobs at rates the companies called unprecedented, and some took second jobs on the side, while the real pay of a new graduate fell. Then demand turned, and campus recruits found their joining dates pushed back by months and years. The workers who complained began, for the first time in large numbers, to do so through unions. By the end of the period, one sentence in a company filing showed that something new had entered the terms on which India sold its engineers' time.

The factory goes home

On 25 March 2020 the Indian government put the country under lockdown.3 Infosys, the second-largest of the Indian software services firms, had moved first.4 In the annual report it filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission two months later, the company wrote: 'By March 20, 2020, we had enabled 93% of our employees to be able to work remotely.'5 By the time it filed, the campuses in India were, on the company's figures, nearly empty. 'Only essential and critical services for our clients and our own operations are being supported from our campuses in India,' the filing said.6

The same report listed what else had changed, from secure laptops and wider bandwidth to 'information security controls for remote working and activity monitoring'.7 It would 'Continue to monitor the productivity of employees as they work remotely'.8 Even the training school moved. Infosys's next annual report recorded that 'The early days of the COVID-19 pandemic saw us relocate trainees from Mysuru and continue their training without missing a beat', using an in-house learning platform called Lex.9 Mysuru (Mysore until 2014) was where the company trained its new graduates before they were placed on projects. Now the trainees were sent away and taught on screens.

The move was not as smooth as the headline figure suggests. Many of the industry's contracts specified where the work would be done. Infosys's annual report for the year to March 2020 warned that its ability to deliver while employees worked from home was 'sometimes constrained by contractual terms with our clients'.10 Some clients had refused permission, or had been slow to give it: 'Since few of our clients did not give us the required permissions, or there were delays in obtaining them, our service delivery was marginally disrupted'.11

Within a year those doubts had mostly gone. 'As of March 31, 2021, we had enabled 99.3% of our employees with a secure remote working environment and 96.5% of our employees were working remotely,' Infosys reported.12 Staff on critical client projects and on work involving sensitive data still went into the offices.13

The filings also recorded what home working cost the people doing it, in the language of risk. 'The productivity of our employees may be negatively impacted due to isolated remote working from home, quarantine requirements, negative social sentiment and personal anxiety,' Infosys wrote in 2020 and repeated in 2021.14

What the filings do show is the factory's front door changing. Infosys's recruiters had tested and interviewed candidates in person. In the year to March 2021, the company said, 'All interviews across the Globe in fiscal 2021 were conducted virtually', and the process from application to offer was digitised.15 The training that followed was online too, and Infosys said it had 'repurposed Lex for over a million college students in India via our InfyTQ app', a platform that, in the company's words a year later, 'brings the best of our Mysuru training to the hands of the learners across the country'.16 Students who passed its assessments could earn an Infosys certification.17 The firm's training school, which had once been a campus that graduates travelled to after they were hired, was now partly a phone app that students used before.

These are the company's own descriptions, and they say nothing about how well remote training worked. They do establish the point that matters for the rest of the book. The work of the Indian services firm, and the teaching that prepared people for it, could be done almost anywhere. That opened a door the industry had kept shut: an engineer working at home could work for two employers at once.

Five million applications

Demand came back faster than the firms had planned for, and the clearest record of it is in the recruiting figures.

Infosys publishes its recruitment funnel every year. In the year to March 2020 (FY2019-20) it made 83,319 offers of employment, and in FY2020-21, the first pandemic year, 48,029.18 Then came the surge. In FY2021-22 Infosys received 5,866,636 applications and made 222,500 offers.19 The offers figure counts experienced recruits as well as new graduates, so it is not a count of freshers. Offers rose about four and a half times in a single year.

Headcount followed. Infosys added about 100,000 people in three years, from 242,371 employees in March 2020 to 343,234 in March 2023.20 The other large firms grew in step. Wipro's rounded totals rose by about a quarter between March 2021 and March 2023.21 Cognizant Technology Solutions, registered in the United States but with most of its staff in India, added about 66,000 people in the two years to the end of 2022, when 258,500 of its 355,300 employees were in India.22

Most of the new hires came from the same colleges that had fed the factory for two decades. A smaller stream came from elsewhere. Infosys said it had 'recruited over 7,280 employees locally in our markets' in FY2020-21, of whom 1,941 were fresh graduates, and that this hiring 'significantly de-risks our operations from regulatory changes related to immigration'.23 The company ran versions of its foundation training in six countries and a training centre in Indianapolis.24 Beside the hundreds of thousands hired in India these numbers are small. They show the firms insuring themselves against the visa politics of Chapter 13 by training some of their recruits in the client's own country.

A third of the workforce, every year

The boom was a labour-market event as much as a demand event. Engineers could leave, and they did.

Cognizant gives the clearest numbers, because, as an American company, it reports attrition (the share of staff who leave in a year) in its annual 10-K filing. Counting both resignations and dismissals, its attrition was 20.6 per cent in 2020, 30.8 per cent in 2021 and 31.7 per cent in 2022. In the last quarter of 2021, on an annualised basis, it reached 34.6 per cent.25 'In 2021, we experienced unprecedented attrition, which was considered industry-wide,' the company wrote. 'As a result, we hired over a hundred thousand new employees'.26 It hired more than a hundred thousand again in 2022.27 The departures were concentrated at the bottom of the pyramid. 'Our attrition is weighted towards our more junior employees,' Cognizant said, and in 2021 'voluntary attrition constituted the vast majority' of the total. In 2020, by contrast, only about half had been voluntary, because the company's own restructuring programme had raised the number of people it let go while the pandemic held down resignations.28

At those rates a large firm had to refill its pyramid almost from scratch every three years. Every departure meant another recruit to find, test, train and place on a project, and every replacement had to be paid more to stay. Cognizant's risk factors put the problem in the plainest terms of the business model: 'While we strive to adjust pricing to reduce the impact of compensation increases on our operating margin, we may not be successful in recovering these increases'.29 Wipro's filing for FY2021-22 listed the same pressures on its margins: 'strong demand for digital and other niche skills are expected to result in wage inflation', 'Increased hiring and training costs due to higher attrition rates', and a 'Limited ability of the market to accept increase in prices for our offerings'.30 This was the time-and-materials model under stress. A firm that sells hours at an agreed rate makes money only while the hours cost it less than the rate. When engineers' pay rose faster than clients would pay, the margin was squeezed from both sides.

Infosys gave its rates to investors on its quarterly results calls, whose transcripts it filed with the SEC, and they tell the same story with one caution: the company changed its measure partway through. In the quarter to March 2020 its executives reported voluntary attrition of 15.1 per cent, or 18.2 per cent counting dismissals. A year later voluntary attrition, 'calculated on annualized basis', was 15.2 per cent, and had risen, they said, 'as demand for talent increased'.31 From FY2021-22 the company reported voluntary attrition over the previous twelve months instead, a measure that moves more slowly. On that basis it was 27.7 per cent in the year to March 2022, 20.9 per cent in the year to March 2023 and 12.6 per cent in the year to March 2024.32 The first two figures are not strictly comparable with the last three, but the peak is not in doubt: in the year to March 2022 more than one Infosys employee in four chose to leave. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), the largest of the firms by headcount, with 592,195 employees in March 2022, went through the same cycle. As reported in the press, its attrition was 12.1 per cent in the year to March 2020 and 7.2 per cent a year later, then rose to 17.4 per cent in the twelve months to March 2022 and 20.1 per cent in the twelve months to March 2023, before falling back to 12.5 per cent in the year to March 2024.33

Women's share of the workforce held steady through the churn, at least at the firms that report it. Cognizant said women made up 36 per cent of its workforce at the end of 2020 and 38 per cent at the end of 2021, the share it reported again for 2023.34 Infosys said in its annual report for FY2023-24 that women made up 'nearly 40% of our workforce'.35

The price of a new engineer

For engineers who could change jobs, the boom was a seller's market. The demand for talent after the pandemic, HR Katha observed, had firms luring people with attractive packages and perks.36 For those entering the industry, the real price moved the other way. A study by the business website Moneycontrol, published in 2022 and reported by the trade site HR Katha and by Analytics India Magazine, put the median annual pay of freshers at large IT firms at about Rs 245,000 in 2012 and Rs 355,000 in 2022, a rise of 45 per cent in ten years.37 At each year's average exchange rate that is about $4,600 and $4,500.38 Adjusted for Indian consumer prices, the 2022 figure was worth nearly a fifth less than the 2012 one.39 Over the same decade, the study found, the median pay of chief executives at the large firms rose by 835 per cent.40

In this book's reading, these figures are the bargain seen from the bottom of the pyramid. A firm that sold engineers' time at rates its clients were reluctant to raise made its margin on the cheapest hours, the ones worked by new graduates, and it needed more of those graduates every year in order to grow. Whatever the boom did for engineers who changed jobs, on these figures it did not raise the real price of a new one.

Two employers, one laptop

Home working made a second job easy to hide. An engineer whose office was a bedroom, and whose manager saw her only on a video call, could in principle work for two companies at once. In 2022 the practice, known as moonlighting, became the subject of a public argument among the industry's leaders.

In August 2022 Premji wrote on Twitter that there was a lot of 'chatter about people moonlighting in the tech industry', and that 'it is cheating- plain and simple.'41 His speech to the management association the next month, quoted at the start of this chapter, turned the complaint into dismissals. Business Today reported that Premji regarded the practice as a 'complete violation of integrity in its deepest form'.2

The industry did not speak with one voice. Infosys, the magazine reported, had told its staff the week before, in 'a strong and firm message', that moonlighting was not allowed. N. Ganapathy Subramaniam, the chief operating officer of TCS, had warned that employees chasing short-term gains in this way 'might lose out in the long term'.42 T.V. Mohandas Pai, a former Infosys director, took the other side. 'No, moonlighting is not cheating,' he told the magazine. 'Employment is a contract between an employer who pays me for working for them for 'n' number of hours a day.' During those hours, he said, he was bound by the employer's conditions, including client confidentiality. 'Now what I do after that time is my freedom'.43

Infosys then wrote the problem into its annual report. From FY2022-23 its risk factors included a new line: 'Continued remote working exposes the company to the risk of some employees working for multiple employers simultaneously (moonlighting) without obtaining required prior approvals and this may result in potential conflict of interests, confidentiality breach, and reduced productivity.'44

The quarrel looks like one about loyalty. It was also about the unit in which the industry sold its work. The services firms billed their clients by the hour or the person-month. Pai's defence turned the same logic around: the employer bought a fixed number of hours, and the rest belonged to the worker. Premji's answer was that an engineer working directly for a competitor was breaking faith whatever the hours. When the work was done on a client's secure floor, the question did not arise, because the office day bounded what an engineer could do. At home that boundary was gone, and both sides found how much of the employment relationship had depended on it. The firms' answer, in the end, was to bring people back. Infosys described 2022 as the year it 'transitioned into a hybrid model of work for our employees and service providers following an extended period of remote work', and from FY2022-23 listed 'Our transition to a hybrid working model' among its risks.45

Packed bags

The turn in demand came in the second half of 2022. Cognizant dated it precisely: 'The rate of attrition began to decrease in the second half of 2022'.46 For the engineers already employed, that meant fewer outside offers. For the graduates who had been promised jobs during the boom, it meant waiting.

On 5 October 2022 Business Today published complaints from new graduates whose start dates at Accenture kept moving. The magazine had already reported similar delays at Wipro, Infosys, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra and Capgemini.47 The graduates it quoted were not named. One said: 'I was placed in Accenture as Associate Software Engineer (ASE) via campus placement in November 2021. I received my offer letter in the last week of April this year.' Since then, the start date had been 'pushed time after time'; the latest, 30 September, had now become 'somewhere in 2023'.48 Another described a postponement two days before the joining date: 'I had packed my bags and booked all tickets to move to the new city, just two days before joining, they postponed the date again. When will they finally make me join, who will pay for my expenses in the meanwhile?'49 A third had turned down other employers: 'I am riddled with depressive feelings because I rejected TCS, Wipro, and HCL for a job in Accenture.'50 Others worried about the record it would leave: 'It's a year since I have been waiting to get onboarded. It is going to reflect as a gap year in my CV.'51

The graduates also blamed their colleges. The placement cell, the office that had invited the companies to campus and organised the tests, was the link between the college and the factory. 'The college placement cell should have helped us in this tough time,' said a graduate of CV Raman Global University in Bhubaneswar. A graduate of Galgotias University in Greater Noida said the placement office had stopped answering once students had graduated: 'they don't care for me anymore.'52 For the colleges, a campus offer was a result to be counted at the end of the year. What happened after graduation was no longer their business.

None of this was new. The bust of 2001-02 had left recruits at Anna University waiting for deferred joining dates, and the collapse of Satyam in 2009 had stranded a batch of its campus hires (Chapters 11 and 16). This was the third time in about twenty years that the factory's newest products took the first shock of a turn in demand.

At Infosys the numbers turned with unusual speed. Offers of employment fell from 222,500 in FY2021-22 to 114,374 in FY2022-23, and to 26,975 in FY2023-24, a fall of about 88 per cent in two years. Applications fell from 5.9 million to 2.4 million.53 Headcount, which had peaked at 343,234 in March 2023, fell to 317,240 a year later.54 Wipro's rounded total fell from more than 250,000 to more than 234,000 over the same year.55 Cognizant ended 2023 with about 347,700 employees, 7,600 fewer than a year before, and with 254,000 in India.56 Its own measure of voluntary attrition in its technology services business fell from 25.6 per cent in 2022 to 13.8 per cent in 2023.57 The engineers who had changed jobs freely in 2021 now stayed put.

Outside the factory laws

The people who took the delayed recruits' complaints to the government belonged to organisations that had barely existed a few years earlier. The industry had grown without unions, and partly under exemptions from the labour laws that governed factories.

A note published in November 2017 by Arvind Gurumurthy of the law firm Kochhar & Co. set out the position. The Industrial Disputes Act of 1947 required employers with more than 100 workers to get the labour commissioner's approval before retrenching staff, 'but this does not cover IT industry'.58 The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act of 1946 required industrial employers to publish certified terms of employment covering hours, shifts, leave and dismissal. 'However, by a notification published on January 25, 2014 the Karnataka government exempted IT companies from applicability of Standing Orders Act for a period of five years.'59 The engineer factory, in other words, was not in law a factory. The note suggested that the exemptions might have been meant to help the industry grow, and recorded that many employees' leaders saw them as giving firms room to treat layoffs as they liked.60

On Sunday 20 August 2017 close to 200 IT workers gathered at the YWCA Hall in Koramangala, Bengaluru, to found the Karnataka State IT/ITeS Employees Union (ITeS stands for IT-enabled services), known as KITU. The News Minute reported that the meeting came amid 'reports of large-scale layoffs in the IT sector'.61 The union's general secretary was Vineeth, a testing lead at the Bengaluru software firm MindTree. He was also part of the IT Employees Centre, an association that had worked for IT employees' rights in Bengaluru for eight years and had received complaints about layoffs and maternity leave, but had never been registered as a union. 'What is currently happening is that if an employee is facing a problem, they have to fight in their individual capacity and the companies may not listen to them,' he told the reporter. 'The trade union will help them negotiate and bargain with the management, and even fight.'62

Others at the meeting spoke in the same terms. Sethumadhavan, a Wipro employee, said: 'As per our Constitution, we have the right to join a union.' The industry, he said, was facing many challenges, among them layoffs and automation, and 'This is the right time to form an organisation'.63 Akshaya, an IBM employee on the union's executive committee, described the hours: 'sometimes we are given a project that requires us to work 15 hours a day and we are paid just Rs 25 per extra hour.'64 The union's president, Amanullah Khan, answered the charge that unions held the country back: 'Trade unions are generally seen as anti-social or anti-national.' He said that neither the National Association of Software and Service Companies (Nasscom), the industry's lobby group, nor the government would be pleased. 'But we are not individuals anymore.'65 The report noted that Mohandas Pai, among others, had opposed unions in the IT sector, on the grounds that they would harm a service industry.66

On 7 November 2017 the Karnataka labour commissioner certified KITU's formation under the Trade Unions Act of 1926.67 Kochhar & Co. called it 'the first trade union in India, officially registered and recognised by the labour department, which is dedicated to the information technology ("IT") sector', with about 250 members by its own claim.68 The same note acknowledged that 'unrecognised groups' had already existed, and the IT Employees Centre had been working for eight years before KITU was founded.69 What is documented is narrower: KITU was the first IT employees' union registered in Karnataka, home to the city the reporter called India's Silicon Valley.

The pandemic gave such organisations work. A second body, the Nascent Information Technology Employees Senate (NITES), based in Pune, took up the cause of more than 2,000 graduates whom Infosys had recruited on campus in 2022 and had still not brought in.70 It complained to the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment on 20 August 2024, and by the union's account Infosys issued about 2,000 offer letters with October joining dates on 2 and 3 September.71 In February 2025 several hundred of those recruits, by then trainees at Mysuru, were let go after the company's internal assessments, over 300 by Infosys's count and 700 by NITES's, as the prologue tells.72

How many members either union had in these years is not on record. Their influence is easier to see than their size: complaints that would once have been one graduate's grievance against one company were now reaching a ministry in Delhi.

Offices, and the people in them

The bust brought restructuring as well as slower hiring. In the second quarter of 2023 Cognizant began what it called its NextGen programme, 'aimed at simplifying our operating model, optimizing corporate functions and consolidating and realigning office space to reflect the post-pandemic hybrid work environment'.73 In 2023 it cost $229 million, about half of it for employee separations and the rest for leaving offices and other items.74 The company warned that 'Our NextGen program and the associated reductions in headcount and consolidation of office space could disrupt our business'.75

The pandemic's lesson about place had come round again. In 2020 the firms had learned that the work could leave the campus. In 2023 one of them was giving up offices because hybrid working meant it needed fewer of them, and was cutting staff in the same programme.

The sentence in the risk factors

The most important single sentence of these years for the rest of this book appeared in Infosys's annual report for FY2023-24, filed in June 2024. In its risk factors, among the familiar warnings about competition and pricing, the company repeated a line it had carried in each annual report since 2020: 'due to competitive market conditions and pricing pressures, we are committing to higher productivity improvements in our contracts with our clients.' Then it added a new one: 'Due to the attention created by the code generation capabilities of AI technologies, clients have demanded higher commitments around productivity improvements.'76 If those promised gains did not materialise, the filing said, the company's profitability could suffer.77

The sentence is short, and it is easy to read too much into it. It does not say that artificial intelligence had cut Infosys's hiring, and the same filing pointed elsewhere when it described pressure on demand, warning that clients 'may become cautious and reduce their discretionary spending in the short term or delay their decision-making process'.78 Whether the fall in offers from 222,500 to 26,975 owed anything to AI is weighed in Chapter 19, which also follows how the sentence changed in later reports.

What the sentence does show is narrower and, for the book's argument, sharper. Clients had read about software that writes code. They had asked their Indian suppliers to commit, in contract, to doing the same work with less effort, and the supplier bore the risk if the gains did not come. Under time-and-materials billing, a productivity gain that the client captures means fewer billable hours. Under a fixed-price contract, it means a lower price for the same result. Either way the first share of the gain goes to the client, and the firm keeps something only if it beats its own promise.

The pandemic years had shown three things about the engineer factory. Its intake was set by clients' discretionary spending, not by the number of graduates: offers at one firm had risen four and a half times in a year and then fallen by nearly nine-tenths in two. Its workers, for a brief period, had the upper hand, and used it by leaving, by moonlighting and, in small numbers, by organising. And its work no longer needed a particular place. By 2024 the clients were asking whether it needed the same number of hours. For forty years the Indian services firms had grown by selling their engineers' time. Now they were being asked, in writing, to promise that the time would shrink.
